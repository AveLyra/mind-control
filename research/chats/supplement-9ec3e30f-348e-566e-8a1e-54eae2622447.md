**The study found that people who become deeply absorbed in experiences tend to respond somewhat more strongly to hypnosis—but the relationship is small.** It’s *“Absorption and hypnotizability: context effects reexamined,”* published in 1991. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

**Absorption** means becoming immersed in something—music, a story, or imagination—until other things fade from attention. **Hypnotizability** means responsiveness to standardized hypnotic suggestions.

Researchers were testing a competing explanation: perhaps asking people about imagination immediately before hypnosis creates expectations that produce the apparent connection.

Across two experiments involving **909 university students**, they compared absorption measured in a general survey with absorption measured immediately before hypnosis. If the testing setup created the link, it should have been consistently stronger immediately before hypnosis. **That pattern did not reliably appear:** small context effects changed direction between experiments. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

The behavioral correlations were approximately **0.15–0.22**—statistically accounting for roughly **2–5% of the variation** in hypnosis scores. Most differences between participants therefore remained unexplained.

The evidence supports absorption as a modest predictor. It does **not establish that absorption causes hypnotizability**, explain its brain mechanisms, or test whether someone can be compelled to act against their wishes. Student volunteers and questionnaire-based measurements also limit generalization. [citation marker unresolved in reader]
