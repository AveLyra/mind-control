Yes. The statement is well supported, and the literature now supports a somewhat stronger formulation than the one you quoted.

The key distinction is that TMR is generally not “teaching a sleeping person from scratch.” It exploits associations established before sleep, then selectively reactivates or modifies them while the brain is consolidating memories. The 2024 Carbone & Diekelmann review describes evidence across declarative, procedural, and emotional memory, forgetting, REM effects, and dream content. [2024 TMR review — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38622159/)

The evidence that the phenomenon itself is real is fairly strong. A major meta-analysis combined 91 experiments, 212 effect sizes, and 2,004 participants. TMR during sleep produced an overall memory effect of Hedges' g = 0.29 (95% CI 0.21–0.38). Effects were significant in N2 and slow-wave sleep; there was substantial heterogeneity, and the evidence at that time was much weaker for REM.  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/)

More recent work makes several aspects particularly interesting.

**Memory strengthening.** A 2025 experiment moved closed-loop TMR out of the laboratory. Twenty-four people learned Italian translations of pseudowords and then slept at home wearing an EEG headband. Previously learned words were replayed when the system detected slow waves. Accuracy increased 8.6% for cued words, versus a nonsignificant −4.6% change for uncued words. Successful effects were associated with spindle activity. [Salfi et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39925332/)

That matters because it demonstrates a technically important progression:

**EEG → detect a favorable brain state → deliver a specific stimulus → produce a measurable later memory difference.**

This isn't merely a theoretical mechanism requiring a full sleep laboratory anymore.

**There is increasingly direct physiological evidence of reactivation.** In 2024, Schreiner et al. combined scalp EEG with intracranial recordings. Experimentally induced memory reactivation coincided with coordinated slow oscillation/spindle activity, while spindle-locked medial-temporal-lobe ripples correlated with detectable memory reactivation. That strengthens the mechanistic case that TMR isn't simply producing nonspecific arousal. [Schreiner et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38898100/)

**TMR can sometimes change a memory rather than merely strengthen it.** This is probably the most consequential development.

A 2024 PNAS experiment first associated nonsense words with aversive images. The following evening, researchers associated half of those words with positive images. During subsequent NREM sleep, they replayed memory cues. Cueing memories containing the newer positive association subsequently **reduced recall of the aversive images and increased positive-memory intrusions**. [Xia et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39052838/)

That is considerably more interesting than “sleep helps you remember vocabulary.” It shows experimentally induced competition between different contents associated with the same cue, with sleep reactivation biasing subsequent recollection.

There are analogous results involving self-evaluation. Another 2024 experiment paired positive personality traits with a waking cue-approach task, replayed some of those traits during slow-wave sleep, and found faster positive self-endorsement immediately afterward and more positive traits endorsed as self-descriptive one week later. Importantly, this involved **pre-sleep training plus TMR**, not arbitrary messages introduced into an unprepared sleeping brain. [Yao et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39055925/)

Social evaluation is an especially interesting frontier. A 2024 study had participants learn other people's evaluations of snacks, then replayed some snack names during NREM sleep. Sleep EEG responses to the cues predicted subsequent evaluation updating. The behavioral evidence wasn't simply “cued items changed and controls didn't,” so this should be interpreted cautiously, but it connects TMR with consolidation of socially acquired evaluations.  [source 6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00255-5)

Then a much stronger experiment appeared in August 2025. Participants learned faces, names, and personality information. During slow-wave sleep, researchers repeatedly played one person's **name paired with new positive personality traits**. Afterward, compared with the uncued person, participants produced more attractive mental representations of the cued face, recalled relatively more positive information, and showed more favorable explicit and implicit evaluations. A control experiment found that merely replaying the person's name wasn't enough.  [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1038%2Fs41539-025-00356-9)

That result is conceptually important because it goes somewhat beyond classical TMR. The researchers weren't simply replaying a previously learned name–trait association. They presented **name + novel positive trait combinations during sleep**, and concluded that social evaluation could be updated through sleep-mediated semantic association.  [source 8](https://doi.org/10.1038%2Fs41539-025-00356-9)

There is also increasingly good evidence that sensory stimulation can influence dreams. In a 2025 REM experiment, subjects learned two tasks associated with different sounds. Researchers subsequently played one sound during REM sleep. Dream reports contained significantly more elements of the cued task than the uncued task. [Konkoly et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40675367/)

An adjacent 2024 experiment took this outside the sleep laboratory. Participants associated sounds before sleep with the cognitive state used for recognizing dreams. Re-presenting those sounds during sleep increased reported lucid dreaming compared with blinded control procedures. [Konkoly et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39278157/)

There is preliminary evidence for direct modulation of emotional processing as well. A 2025 REM experiment re-presented tones associated with negative images. Forty-eight hours later, cued stimuli produced reduced responses in several salience/emotion-related brain regions and reduced heart-rate deceleration; subjective effects depended on the original emotional intensity. [Greco et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41089190/)

But there are important boundaries. Effects are generally modest and highly dependent on sleep stage, timing, cue strength, prior learning, memory strength, and whether stimulation causes awakening. Some experiments fail. For example, attempting to weaken week-old memories by combining TMR with sleep disruption did **not** weaken them. [Whitmore et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39500927/) Recent 2026 work also reports cases where competing odor and sound cues interfere with one another rather than producing the intended effect. [Subramanian et al. — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41702567/)

So I would update your paragraph to something like this:

The sleeping brain remains selectively responsive to its environment. Sensory information presented during particular sleep states can interact with ongoing memory consolidation. Targeted memory reactivation (TMR) exploits this by associating memories with sounds or odors and presenting related cues again during sleep. Controlled studies show that this can selectively strengthen some memories, bias competition between memories, alter emotional responses, influence dream content, and under some conditions modify learned evaluations and associations. Closed-loop EEG systems can now detect favorable sleep oscillations and automatically time cues to them, including in home settings. These effects are generally modest and context-dependent, and they do not imply unrestricted learning or control of a sleeping person's thoughts.

The last sentence is important. The evidence has moved substantially beyond the simplistic proposition that “sounds during sleep might improve recall.” There is now credible experimental evidence for **selective memory strengthening, memory interference/updating, emotional modulation, dream-content biasing, self-referential effects, and social-evaluation updating**. But these are constrained manipulations built around particular cues, memories, brain states, and experimental procedures—not evidence that arbitrary beliefs or complex behaviors can reliably be implanted in an unaware sleeper.

The 2025 social-evaluation experiment is probably the most consequential paper to examine next because it pushes closest to the boundary between conventional TMR and **introducing new evaluative information during sleep**.  [source 14](https://doi.org/10.1038%2Fs41539-025-00356-9)
