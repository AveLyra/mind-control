== Origins ==

Bowers developed dissociated control theory as a revision of earlier dissociation approaches to hypnosis. In a 1992 paper, he argued that goal-directed behavior does not necessarily require deliberate executive initiation. He distinguished behavior that serves a goal from behavior that is consciously performed in order to achieve that goal, proposing that hypnotic suggestions may directly activate cognitive subsystems without the usual degree of executive initiative.<ref name="Bowers1992" />

Woody and Bowers subsequently elaborated the theory in the 1994 chapter "A frontal assault on dissociated control", relating it to contemporary models of executive functioning.<ref name="WoodyBowers1994">{{cite book
 |last1=Woody
 |first1=Erik Z.
 |last2=Bowers

The theory drew particularly on models that distinguish routine or automatically activated behavioral schemas from higher-order supervisory processes. On this account, normal executive control biases or modifies lower-level systems when circumstances require flexible adjustment. Hypnosis was proposed to weaken this supervisory influence, making behavior more directly responsive to suggestions.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

== Theoretical framework ==

=== Supervisory control and behavioral schemas ===

In the original dissociated-control model, behavior is organized through multiple levels of control. Relatively routine responses can be produced by lower-level schemas, while a supervisory attentional system contributes when behavior requires planning, conflict resolution, error correction, or flexible adjustment.<ref name="Bowers1992" />

The theory proposes that a hypnotic induction can reduce the influence of supervisory control on these lower-level systems. A suggestion can therefore activate an appropriate response without requiring the person to construct and consciously implement a strategy for producing it.<ref name="Bowers1992" />

This mechanism was proposed to help explain the subjective [[hypnotic involuntariness|involuntariness]] of hypnotic responses. Because the response is not experienced as resulting from ordinary deliberate initiation, it can seem to occur automatically or by itself.<ref name="Bowers1992" />

=== Dissociated experience and dissociated control ===

Dissociated control is distinct from what Bowers termed '''dissociated experience'''. The latter is associated with [[Ernest Hilgard]]'s neodissociation approach and proposes that relevant cognitive processes may remain active but become inaccessible to ordinary conscious awareness through a division or barrier between streams of processing.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

Under dissociated control, by contrast, the principal alteration is proposed to occur in the organization of control itself. Suggestions may activate behavior without the normal degree of executive planning or intervention. In this interpretation, a participant's report of reduced control need not merely reflect lack of awareness of otherwise intact executive activity.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

Kirsch and Lynn characterized the distinction as one between a theory in which executive processes remain active but are excluded from awareness and a theory in which frontal or supervisory control over behavioral schemas is itself weakened.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

== Revised and second-order accounts ==

Later formulations of dissociated control moved away from a simple model in which hypnosis globally suppresses executive functioning. Evidence that hypnotized participants retain many executive abilities prompted theorists to distinguish between implementing control and using monitoring information to flexibly modify that control.<ref name="JamiesonWoody2007">{{cite book
 |last1=Jamieson
 |first1=Graham A.
 |last2=Woody
 |first2=Erik Z.
 |chapter=Dissociated control as a paradigm for cognitive neuroscience research and theorizing in hypnosis
 |editor-last=Jamieson
 |editor-first=Graham A.
 |title=Hypnosis and Conscious States: The Cognitive Neuroscience Perspective
 |publisher=Oxford University Press
 |year=2007
 |pages=111–129
 |doi=10.1093/oso/9780198569794.003.0007

In this revised account, hypnotized individuals may still maintain a task goal and execute behavior effectively, while communication between conflict-monitoring systems and systems implementing cognitive control is reduced. The resulting response can remain organized and goal-directed while becoming less responsive to monitoring signals that would ordinarily prompt strategic adjustment.<ref name="JamiesonWoody2007" /><ref name="Egner2005" />

This revised formulation has sometimes been described as '''second-order dissociated control''' or as a '''decoupling''' model because it emphasizes impaired coordination among executive processes rather than their wholesale removal.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />

== Empirical research ==

=== Hypnotic analgesia ===

Miller and Bowers tested predictions of dissociated control using hypnotic analgesia. Eighteen highly hypnotizable and eighteen minimally hypnotizable participants received both a hypnotic analgesia procedure and a stress-inoculation procedure. Highly hypnotizable participants showed greater pain reduction in both conditions, but the stress-inoculation procedure impaired performance on a competing cognitive task whereas hypnotic analgesia did not.<ref name="MillerBowers1993">{{cite journal
 |last1=Miller
 |first1=Mark E.
 |last2=Bowers
 |first2=Kenneth S.

 |pages=29–38
 |doi=10.1037/0021-843X.102.1.29
 |pmid=8436696

The authors interpreted the result as evidence that hypnotic analgesia could reduce pain without requiring the same degree of effortful cognitive strategy used in the comparison procedure. They argued that this pattern was more consistent with direct activation of pain-control processes than with either deliberate coping strategies or a model in which effortful pain-control processes merely became inaccessible to awareness.<ref name="MillerBowers1993" />

The finding does not establish that all hypnotic responses require little cognitive effort. Reviews have distinguished between perceived and actual effort and between the effort required to initiate a response and the effort required to sustain it.<ref name="Ruehle1997">{{cite journal

=== Stroop and attentional control ===

Jamieson and Sheehan tested dissociated-control predictions using the [[Stroop effect|Stroop task]]. Measures of supervisory attentional functioning declined during hypnosis, and highly hypnotizable participants made more Stroop errors during hypnosis than in the comparison condition. The authors interpreted the pattern as consistent with reduced supervisory control and proposed a functional disruption involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex.<ref name="JamiesonSheehan2004">{{cite journal

Because the study was behavioral, the proposed neural mechanism was an interpretation rather than a direct measurement of functional connectivity.<ref name="JamiesonSheehan2004" />

=== Conflict monitoring and functional connectivity ===

Egner, Jamieson, and Gruzelier subsequently combined a Stroop task with functional magnetic resonance imaging and EEG coherence measurements. Highly hypnotizable participants showed increased conflict-related activity in the [[anterior cingulate cortex]] during hypnosis, while activity associated with cognitive control in lateral frontal cortex did not show the same change. EEG measurements also showed decreased coherence between frontal midline and left lateral sites after hypnotic induction.<ref name="Egner2005" />

The authors interpreted these findings as evidence for a functional separation between conflict monitoring and cognitive control. The result contributed to the revised version of dissociated control: rather than showing general frontal inactivity, it suggested altered coordination between executive processes.<ref name="Egner2005" />

Research has also questioned whether dissociated control characterizes all highly hypnotizable individuals equally. == Criticism and competing evidence ==

Kirsch and Lynn published a major critical review of dissociation theories in 1998. They argued that both neodissociation and dissociated-control explanations faced conceptual and empirical problems and maintained that many hypnotic phenomena could instead be understood through ordinary mechanisms such as expectations, intentions, automatic activation, motivation, and response sets.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

== Relationship to other theories ==

=== Neodissociation ===

[[Neodissociation]] theory, associated with Ernest Hilgard, proposes that hypnosis can divide mental processing into partially independent streams and that some processes may become inaccessible to ordinary consciousness. Dissociated control was developed partly to avoid explaining hypnotic involuntariness solely by placing intact executive processes behind an awareness barrier.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

In dissociated control, the relevant alteration instead concerns the degree to which higher-level control participates in generating or modifying the response.<ref name="Bowers1992" />

=== Social-cognitive accounts ===

Sociocognitive theories generally explain hypnotic behavior through ordinary psychological processes such as expectations, motivation, social context, goal-directed action, and automatic response activation. They therefore reject the need to infer a special dissociation of executive control from the subjective experience that a response felt involuntary.<ref name="KirschLynn1998" />

Both dissociated-control and sociocognitive theories can permit responses to be automatic and goal-directed. Their disagreement concerns whether hypnosis produces a distinctive functional separation in control processes or whether the relevant phenomena can be explained without such a dissociation.<ref name="WoodySadler1998" />

=== Cold-control theory ===

[[Cold-control theory]] also attempts to explain why hypnotic responses feel involuntary but makes a substantially different claim. Cold control proposes that the response remains intentionally controlled at a first-order level while the person has an inaccurate higher-order representation of having intended it.

Dissociated control instead proposes, particularly in its original form, that ordinary executive involvement in generating the response is genuinely reduced. The theories therefore differ over whether subjective involuntariness reflects altered executive control or altered metacognition concerning otherwise intentional control.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />

Dissociated control was one of the first hypnosis theories to be explicitly linked to frontal executive systems. Woody and Farvolden proposed that the theory could be integrated with research on frontal-lobe functions involved in memory, monitoring, and volition.<ref name="WoodyFarvolden1998">{{cite journal
