Yes. Mechanically, the important concept is that the sound is not itself an “alpha sound” or “deep-sleep sound.” The brain's state when the sound arrives changes its effect.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660843/)

Middle-school version: imagine brain activity as someone repeatedly pushing a child on a swing. If you push at the right moment, the swing goes higher. Push at the wrong moment, and you can slow it down.

An EEG is watching the “swing.” A computer sees where the brain is in its repeating electrical rhythm, predicts where it will be a fraction of a second later, and plays a tiny sound at the chosen moment:

**EEG → detect wave → predict next part → click → brain responds → repeat**

Your ears still process a quiet click while you're asleep. That little sensory input perturbs the brain. Repeated at favorable moments, it can reinforce an oscillation; at other phases, it can interfere with it.  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105379)

This has been demonstrated around sleep onset with **alpha rhythms**: sounds delivered at different phases produced different effects on alpha activity and the transition into sleep.  [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11185466/)

More technically: EEG is filtered around the oscillation of interest and an algorithm estimates its instantaneous phase. Hardware/software/audio latency is accounted for so the auditory-evoked neural response occurs near a predetermined phase rather than merely playing the sound when that phase is detected.  [source 3](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105379)

For deep NREM sleep, slow oscillations reflect cortical populations alternating between relatively inactive **down-states** and active **up-states**. Auditory stimulation near the transition toward/up-state can increase subsequent slow oscillations and associated sleep spindles. Stimulation around the opposite phase can instead disrupt an ongoing train of slow oscillations.  [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105379)

For alpha (~8–12 Hz), the timing problem is considerably faster. The 2024 αCLAS experiments found evidence consistent with **phase resetting**: an auditory event perturbs the ongoing oscillator, and the resulting trajectory depends on the phase at which that perturbation occurred.  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11185466/) A randomized trial subsequently used EEG-triggered, alpha-phase-locked sounds in people with prolonged sleep latency and reduced average sleep-onset time by about 10.5 minutes (29%), although it was a small study of 21 analyzed participants.  [source 6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-63385-1)

On **resisting it without compromising sleep**, there is an interesting answer: the sleeping brain already does.

It isn't a passive oscillator that an external sound simply takes control of. Sleep contains mechanisms that process stimuli while suppressing unnecessary awakening; K-complexes, for example, have been interpreted partly as sleep-protective responses to nonsalient stimulation. The system also shows **habituation, homeostatic compensation, variable responsiveness, and outright arousal** when stimulation becomes excessive.  [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105379)

So there are at least three natural forms of “resistance”:

**Habituation:** repeated irrelevant sounds become less effective.

**Homeostasis:** experimentally suppressing deep-sleep slow-wave activity can cause slow-wave activity to rebound later in the night.  [source 8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2023.105379)

**Arousal:** make the stimulus sufficiently salient and the brain transitions toward lighter sleep or wakes—which defeats the experiment.

What hasn't been demonstrated, to my knowledge, is a conscious technique whereby a sleeping person can decide, *“don't let this phase-locked sound alter my alpha rhythm,”* while remaining asleep. Once unconscious, the relevant defenses are largely automatic physiological ones.

That last point also limits the more speculative scenario we were discussing earlier: **closed-loop stimulation is influencing an adaptive biological system, not writing instructions into it.** The brain changes its response to the intervention itself.
