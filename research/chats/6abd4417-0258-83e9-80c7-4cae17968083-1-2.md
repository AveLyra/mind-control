== Terminology and historical development ==

In 1974, psychologist André Weitzenhoffer used the term ''classical suggestion-effect'' for a proposed class of non-voluntary behaviors elicited by communications intended to function as suggestions. His experiments also indicated that statements described as instructions could sometimes function psychologically like suggestions.<ref name="Weitzenhoffer1974" /> The shortened expression ''classic suggestion effect'' subsequently became common in the hypnosis literature for the experience that suggested responses occur nonvolitionally.<ref name="Bowers1981">{{cite journal

== Phenomenology ==

Hypnotic suggestions can concern motor behavior, perception, memory, cognition, or subjective experience. A characteristic feature of successful responding is that the resulting event may seem to occur automatically or without the participant's ordinary experience of initiating it.<ref name="Kihlstrom2015" /> The degree of experienced involuntariness varies between individuals and between different types of suggestion.

Researchers distinguish '''involuntariness''' from '''effortlessness'''. Involuntariness concerns the degree to which a person experiences reduced authorship or control over a response, whereas effortlessness concerns how little deliberate effort the response appears to require. The two experiences can covary but are empirically separable.<ref name="Polito2013">{{cite journal
 |last1=Polito
 |first1=Vince
 |last2=Barnier
 |first2=Amanda J.
 |last3=Woody
 |first3=Erik Z.
 |title=Developing the Sense of Agency Rating Scale (SOARS): An empirical measure of agency disruption in hypnosis
 |journal=Consciousness and Cognition
 |year=2013
 |volume=22
 |issue=3
 |pages=684–696
 |doi=10.1016/j.concog.2013.04.003
 |pmid=23685619
}}</ref>

This distinction is important because a response can be easy to perform without feeling involuntary, or can be experienced as involuntary while still requiring substantial cognitive processing. Contemporary theories therefore generally distinguish the subjective sense of agency from the mechanisms that generate the behavior itself.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />

== Measurement ==

=== Behavioral and subjective measures ===

Traditional hypnotizability scales typically score whether a participant satisfies behavioral criteria for a series of suggestions. Examples include movement of a limb, inability to perform a challenged movement, suggested perceptual changes, or suggested amnesia. Such measures provide an index of responsiveness but do not necessarily determine whether the participant experienced the response as involuntary.<ref name="Oakley2021" />

Cunningham and Ramos examined responses to the Harvard Group Scale of Hypnotic Susceptibility and found a significant minority of participants who passed suggestions by behavioral criteria but reported performing them voluntarily. Conversely, some participants who failed the behavioral criteria nevertheless described aspects of their responses as involuntary. The wording of post-experimental questions did not significantly account for their reports of involuntariness.<ref name="Cunningham2012" />

Mohl and Schutkofsky similarly distinguished deliberate responses from nonvolitional responses. They reported that conventional scoring could count deliberately enacted responses as successful hypnotic responses and argued that research using hypnotic inventories should take volitional responding into account.<ref name="Mohl2017">{{cite journal

=== Sense of Agency Rating Scale ===

The '''Sense of Agency Rating Scale''' (SOARS) was developed by Vince Polito, Amanda Barnier, and Erik Woody to measure subjective changes in agency during hypnosis. In an initial study of 370 participants and a second study of 113 participants with differing levels of hypnotizability, factor analysis identified two dimensions: '''Involuntariness''' and '''Effortlessness'''. Both were associated with hypnotizability and with responses to several types of hypnotic suggestion.<ref name="Polito2013" />

A Hungarian adaptation of the scale subsequently reproduced the two-factor structure in a sample of 197 participants. Changes in involuntariness and effortlessness were associated with other alterations in subjective conscious experience even after statistical control for conventional hypnotizability scores.<ref name="Kolto2017">{{cite journal

== Experimental findings ==

Experimental work has examined whether reports of involuntariness can be reduced to simple behavioral compliance or deliberate imagery. Zamansky and Ruehle instructed hypnotized participants to generate thoughts and imagery that conflicted with hypnotic suggestions and to pay explicit attention to their own behavior. Participants continued to report that their responses felt involuntary. The authors interpreted the findings as evidence against the particular sociocognitive explanations they tested, although later sociocognitive theories developed other accounts of hypnotic involuntariness.<ref name="Zamansky1995">{{cite journal

== Relationship to hypnotizability ==

[[Hypnotic susceptibility|Hypnotizability]] refers to individual differences in responsiveness to standardized hypnotic suggestions. It is related to hypnotic involuntariness but is not synonymous with it. Behavioral scales can register responses that participants describe as deliberately produced, and subjective involuntariness can sometimes occur even when the formal behavioral criterion for a suggestion is not met.<ref name="Cunningham2012" /><ref name="Mohl2017" />

SOARS measurements show that involuntariness and effortlessness correlate with hypnotizability, but their separation into distinct factors supports treating alterations in agency as an additional dimension of hypnotic experience rather than simply another name for behavioral responsiveness.<ref name="Polito2013" />

Kihlstrom has argued that differences in experienced involuntariness may also help distinguish patterns among highly hypnotizable individuals. However, research has not established a generally accepted classification of discrete hypnotic subtypes, and larger datasets would be needed to determine whether proposed patterns represent stable categories.<ref name="Kihlstrom2015" />

== Theoretical explanations ==

=== Social-cognitive and response-expectancy accounts ===

Social-cognitive theories generally treat hypnotic responses as goal-directed behaviors whose subjective interpretation is strongly influenced by expectations, beliefs, context, and automaticity. Kirsch and Lynn proposed that many intentional behaviors outside hypnosis are already highly automatic and can be triggered by situational cues with relatively little conscious deliberation. In their account, hypnotic suggestions can establish response expectancies that function similarly to implementation intentions: a response can be intentional in its organization while nevertheless being experienced as nonvolitional.<ref name="KirschLynn1997">{{cite journal

These accounts replaced an explanatory dissociative mechanism with combinations of ordinary automaticity, preparation of responses, expectations, and interpretations of experience.<ref name="KirschLynn1998">{{cite journal

=== Dissociation and dissociated-control accounts ===

Dissociation theories instead propose that hypnotic involuntariness reflects some separation among psychological processes that are normally integrated. In classical neodissociation accounts, suggested responses can be produced outside the ordinary stream of executive awareness. Dissociated-control theories place greater emphasis on altered relations between executive control systems and lower-level processes involved in generating responses.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />

Under these approaches, reports of reduced agency may correspond more directly to an alteration in the mechanisms of executive control rather than solely to a reinterpretation of otherwise ordinary intentional behavior. Reviews of hypnosis theory have nevertheless found continuing disagreement over what forms of dissociation, if any, are required to explain hypnotic responding.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />

=== Cold-control theory ===

[[Cold-control theory]] offers a metacognitive account of involuntariness. It proposes that a hypnotic response can remain intentional at a first-order level while the person lacks an accurate higher-order representation of having intended the response. On this account, the distinctive feature is not loss of the first-order intention itself, but altered awareness of that intention.<ref name="LushDienes2019">{{cite journal
 |last1=Lush
 |first1=Peter
 |last2=Dienes
 |first2=Zoltán
 |title=Time perception and the experience of agency in meditation and hypnosis
 |journal=PsyCh Journal

 |pages=36–50
 |doi=10.1002/pchj.276
 |pmid=30912626

}}</ref>

This differs from dissociated-control accounts because cold control retains intentional control over the response while locating the unusual experience in [[metacognition]] of intention. Research using subjective timing and intentional-binding paradigms has been interpreted as consistent with differences in access to intention-related information, although those measures and their interpretation remain debated.<ref name="LushDienes2019" /><ref name="Lush2017" />

== Debate and unresolved questions ==

A central unresolved question is whether the subjective experience of involuntariness primarily reflects altered executive control, altered metacognitive access to otherwise intentional processes, expectations and interpretations applied to highly automatic behavior, or some combination of these mechanisms. Theories also differ on whether a single mechanism can explain motor, perceptual, cognitive, and memory suggestions.<ref name="Zahedi2024" />
