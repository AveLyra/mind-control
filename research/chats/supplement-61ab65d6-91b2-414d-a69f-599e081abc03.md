## What “compliance” means—and how it can change

Consider a suggestion that an arm is becoming heavy.

A person might deliberately lower their arm to produce the expected result, while knowing that they do not actually feel unusual heaviness or involuntariness. That illustrates **compliant enactment**. Another person might genuinely experience heaviness and experience the lowering as automatic. The visible movement alone would not distinguish them. This is why behavioral scores and subjective reports matter separately in this literature. [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207149508409376)

**One qualification about the exact study:** I verified its abstract and related discussions, but could not obtain the complete 1995 measurement protocol. I therefore cannot responsibly give its precise compliance questionnaire items or scoring formula. The explanation above is the conceptual distinction, not a reconstructed operational score.

This distinction remains meaningful under cold-control theory. A response can involve an underlying intention **without the person knowingly acting or pretending**. Cold-control theory proposes that the person’s awareness of that intention is altered; compliant enactment, by contrast, involves knowingly producing the expected behavior. Whether cold control explains every hypnotic response remains disputed. [source 5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)

**Decreasing compliance in yourself during an agreed hypnosis exercise** means avoiding the substitution of performance for experience. A practical approach is to make accurate reporting—not “passing” the suggestion—the goal: allow no response, do not deliberately assist a supposedly automatic movement merely to satisfy expectations, and report separately what you did, what you felt, and how deliberate it seemed. These are safeguards against misleading performance, not a proven treatment for a general personality trait.

**Increasing willing cooperation** is different: you can knowingly agree to perform a requested action or practice an exercise. But increasing genuine responsiveness is not simply “cooperating harder.” In the 1995 experiment, removing physical-enactment instructions still allowed increases in behavioral and subjective responsiveness; greater imagination use accompanied those improvements. That is precisely why the researchers preferred the modified training over encouraging enactment. [source 6](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207149508409376)

## Which drugs affect it?

**I did not find evidence that a drug reliably increases or decreases the specific compliance measure used in that study.** There are experiments on related outcomes, but those outcomes should not be conflated.

| Substance | What the experiment found | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Alcohol** | A placebo-controlled study of 32 moderately hypnotizable participants found increased hypnotic responsiveness after alcohol. | A reliable increase in deliberate compliance, acceptance of beliefs, or obedience. [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2013.07.001) |
| **LSD** | A small placebo-controlled study of 10 volunteers found stronger reported responses on the Creative Imagination Scale, but no statistically significant improvement on a separate cued-imagery test. | That all imagery, suggestibility, and cooperation increase together. [source 8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-014-3714-z) |
| **Oxytocin** | One study of 40 low-hypnotizable men reported improved hypnotic responding. Another study found that oxytocin **weakened** the word-blindness suggestion’s effect on Stroop performance. | A general “trust” or “compliance” switch. The effect depended on the task and population. [source 9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.010) [source 10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4090953/) |

These are not grounds for self-experimentation to alter compliance. They concern particular laboratory responses, not the production of consent. **Neither a positive nor a negative result on one task establishes a dependable way to increase or decrease someone’s willingness to follow instructions.** The contrasting oxytocin findings are especially relevant to your question: changing a chemical system can help one form of responding while interfering with another. [source 11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2011.05.010) [source 12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4090953/)

## The related research that most strengthens the connection

### Unnoticed information can influence what later comes to mind

Kouider and Dupoux’s 2005 study demonstrates that a masked spoken word can activate a representation of that word without conscious identification. A 2010 follow-up added brain-imaging evidence: repeated masked words altered activity in speech-processing regions, with some effects surviving a change of speaker. These findings concern **word processing**, rather than the understanding of arbitrary concealed instructions. [source 13](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01584.x) [source 14](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2009.08.043)

**The more direct extension is research on involuntary autobiographical memories—personal memories that come to mind without deliberately searching for them.**

In **Mace and Keller’s 2024 study**, words presented below the authors’ awareness threshold primed the later production of content-related involuntary personal memories. The reported priming was comparable to that obtained with consciously available words. This goes beyond recognizing a later word faster: the outcome concerned **which personal memories subsequently entered awareness**. [source 15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2024.103723)

That is particularly pertinent to the possibility of an experience arising without awareness of its initiating cue. However, the experiment used visual words followed by a separate, cued memory task—not camouflaged speech delivered during immersive visualization. I could verify the original abstract, but not independently audit the complete awareness-testing procedure.

The same research program supplies several additional links:

**Imagery versus verbal judgments.** In a separate 2024 experiment, imagining the meanings of words produced more subsequent autobiographical-memory priming than making judgments about their meanings. Thus, processing something imaginatively can change its later influence on spontaneous thought. Importantly, this was **not** the subliminal-word experiment; combining the two results does not prove that imagery amplifies subliminal priming. [source 16](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-024-01999-w)

**Repetitive thinking.** A study published in January 2026 had one group repeatedly think about food and another think about varied topics. The repetitive-thinking group subsequently produced more food-related involuntary memories, as well as more involuntary memories overall. This provides a direct connection between **rehearsed mental content and later apparently spontaneous remembering**. [source 17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2025.103966)

**Divided attention.** A June 2026 study found that word-processing activity still primed later involuntary memories while participants simultaneously monitored a number sequence. Priming did not differ significantly from a full-attention condition. This suggests that the effect can survive competing demands—but participants were consciously judging the words, so divided attention here is not evidence of complete unawareness. [source 18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2026.104062)

Together, these studies support the idea that **a memory can arrive spontaneously even though earlier processing helped determine why that particular memory arrived**. They are largely from the same research group, so they should not be counted as independent replications of the entire proposed mechanism.

### Imagining and perceiving can contribute to the same experience

A second relevant line concerns **source monitoring**: deciding whether an experience came from imagination or the environment.

In **Dijkstra and Fleming’s 2023 study**, participants imagined visual patterns while looking at noise. On an unexpected trial, a faint real pattern appeared. The results favored a model in which imagined and externally generated signals contribute to a combined experience, with judgments about reality influenced by the strength of that experience. A 2025 brain-imaging study further linked these judgments to combined imagery–perception activity in visual cortex and its interaction with frontal regions. [source 19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036541/) [source 20](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.05.015)

**This provides a concrete reason why an experience need not come with a perfectly reliable label identifying its source.** But it does not imply that imagery always conceals external input. In the 2023 experiment, matching imagery actually made the real pattern **more likely to be classified as real** than mismatching imagery. The finding is interaction and occasional source confusion—not a universal suppression of external awareness. [source 21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036541/)

A still closer, though preliminary, experiment appeared in 2025: **Byczynski and D’Angiulli asked participants to imagine objects while detecting briefly masked spatial information unrelated to those objects.** Accuracy averaged approximately **54% with imagery versus 51% without imagery**. This directly combined internal imagery with weak external processing. However, it tested spatial location, not word meaning; participants knew they were detecting a cue; and the reported methods did not independently establish the stringent condition of being unaware that any stimulus occurred. Its complicated subgroup findings also resist a simple “more vivid imagery means more unconscious processing” conclusion. [source 22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12342863/)

### Rehearsal can improve imagery without making it consciously effortful forever

The 15-day motor-imagery study you selected is relevant because it shows reported imagery improvements and EEG changes across different hypnotizability groups. It supports investigating imagery as a **trainable activity**, rather than treating every difference as a fixed hypnotic trait. It does not establish that training changed hypnotizability, nor that similar EEG measures mean imagined and executed movements are identical. [source 23](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207144.2026.2669243)

Related hypnosis research complicates the idea that successful responding always requires more deliberate visualization.

In **Comey and Kirsch’s 1999 experiment**, removing instructions to deliberately construct suggestion-related imagery actually increased responsiveness. Spontaneously arising imagery correlated with behavioral responses; deliberately generated imagery did not. Many successful responders nevertheless reported intentionally trying to produce the suggested experience. [source 24](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207149908410023)

That combination is important:

> **Intentionally trying to have an experience is not the same as consciously constructing every detail of it.**

A small **2026 exploratory interview study** found a similar distinction. Moderate responders described goal-directed fantasies, whereas the strongest responses appeared less dependent on consciously reported fantasy construction. The study involved two small samples—seven and six people—and was explicitly intended to generate hypotheses, not establish a general mechanism. [source 25](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207144.2026.2657927)

### The response and the experience of controlling it can dissociate

This is where **Palfi and colleagues’ 2021 cold-control experiment** fits most directly.

Voluntarily imagining words as meaningless and experiencing them as meaningless under posthypnotic suggestion produced comparable reductions in Stroop interference. Yet the voluntary condition felt more like controlled imagination, while the hypnotic condition felt more like perception. Thus, **similar task performance can accompany different experiences of agency and reality**. [source 26](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)

Crucially, the “unconscious” element in cold-control theory concerns **awareness of the intention generating a response**. It does not require that the original instruction was unheard or subliminal.

## The emerging account: selective activation, simulation, and attribution

The research suggests a useful synthesis, but **not one established theory that has already demonstrated the whole chain**.

First, an external cue can activate a word, concept, or memory representation without all aspects of that processing becoming consciously accessible. The depth of processing varies: recognizing a word’s identity, activating associated meaning, and retrieving a personal memory are different achievements. [source 27](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01584.x) [source 28](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2024.103723)

Second, ongoing goals and mental activity influence which representations receive priority. Repetition and imagery can make particular content more available, and that availability can influence what later arises spontaneously. This is more specific than saying that repetition makes a person generally receptive. [source 29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2025.103966) [source 30](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-024-01999-w)

Third, imagery and incoming perception can interact. An internally generated representation is not always processed in complete isolation from matching external information. [source 31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036541/)

Finally, **having an experience and knowing how it was generated are partly separable**. A thought may arise without awareness of the earlier cue that primed it; an experience may feel perceptual rather than imagined; an action may feel automatic rather than deliberately initiated. Those are different forms of limited awareness, supported by different experiments. [source 32](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2024.103723) [source 33](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.05.015) [source 34](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)

**My synthesis is therefore: a person can intentionally establish a mental activity, while subsequent content and responses are generated through processes whose contributions they do not fully notice.** External cues and internal rehearsal can both contribute to those processes.

An illustrative possibility would be a memory emerging during visualization because a preceding cue activated related material, while the person experiences the memory simply as part of their own train of thought. The memory-priming literature makes that a grounded hypothesis. The additional claim that camouflaged speech produces that effect **during a verified absorptive state**, with neither the cue nor its contribution noticed, still requires a study combining those conditions.

Two theoretical distinctions prevent overgeneralization. **Source-monitoring models** address “Was this imagined or perceived?” **Cold-control theory** addresses “Was I aware of intending this response?” They concern related but nonidentical judgments; evidence for one does not automatically prove the other.

The most informative bridge is therefore **not compliance**, but the relationship between **what becomes mentally available, what becomes experienced, and what the person knows about its origin**.
