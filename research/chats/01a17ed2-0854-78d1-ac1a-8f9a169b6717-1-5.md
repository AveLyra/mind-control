**Yes—a phone-only research app is feasible.** The important distinction is between **measuring breathing** and **estimating sleep stage**. Breathing rate alone is unlikely to distinguish sleep stages reliably; a useful model would need patterns over time, such as breathing variability, movement, and acoustic features.

Assuming you mean a phone beside the bed, two approaches have published evidence:

| Native hardware | How it works | Main challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Microphone | Detect breathing sounds and their timing, along with movement sounds | Quiet breathing, fans, bedding, and another sleeper can obscure the signal |
| Speaker + microphone | Emit high-frequency sound and analyze reflections from breathing-related body movement—essentially sonar | Requires device-specific testing and suitable placement |

A sonar-based smartphone system was tested against laboratory polysomnography in **38 subjects**, demonstrating technical feasibility, although that small study does not establish reliability across phones and homes. [Sonar validation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31946344/)

There is also stronger evidence for audio-based inference: a 2023 study tested smartphone recordings against simultaneous home polysomnography in **45 independent test nights**, reporting **76.2% accuracy for wake/REM/non-REM classification**. That model used rich audio information and substantial training data; the result does **not** mean that counting breaths achieves that accuracy or that it reliably distinguishes every sleep stage. [Home validation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37261889/)

For a small scientific prototype, I would start with:

1. **Measure breathing first:** estimate breaths per minute, variability, and signal quality; explicitly report “insufficient signal” when appropriate.
2. **Validate the measurement:** compare it with a synchronized respiratory belt or other suitable reference.
3. **Add experimental staging:** produce stage probabilities, then evaluate against independently scored polysomnography using participants excluded from training.
4. **Test realistic failures:** another sleeper, background noise, position changes, and different phone models.

The strongest objection to the idea is that **different sleep stages can produce overlapping breathing patterns**. Clinical staging relies on brain activity, eye movements, and muscle tone; a phone infers those states indirectly. That makes this a credible research project, but the sensing and validation are substantially harder than building the app interface.

Would the phone sit on a bedside table, or could it be attached to the subject—and do you need stage estimates live or only the next morning?
