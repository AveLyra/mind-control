Yes—but not by detecting brain waves. A phone can potentially **infer REM indirectly** from breathing, movement, and sound.

There are already some surprisingly good research results:

- **Microphone:** A 2023 study used ordinary smartphone audio—mainly breathing and movement sounds—and achieved about **76% overall sleep-stage accuracy**, with **~65% accuracy for REM** in home testing.  [source 0](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46216/)

- **Speaker + microphone as sonar:** Researchers have emitted inaudible sound from a phone and analyzed the reflections to measure respiration and movement, with the phone simply sitting on a **bedside table**.  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31946344/)

- **UWB radar:** A customized smartphone with UWB radar classified wake/light/deep/REM at about **76% overall accuracy** across 509 nights.  [source 2](https://research.samsung.com/research-papers/Ultra-Wideband-Radar-Based-Sleep-Stage-Classification-in-Smartphone-Using-an-End-to-End-Deep-Learning)

So proximity doesn't need to be **head-level**. For microphone-based detection, I'd think **bedside-table distance—perhaps 1–3 feet from the sleeper—rather than inches from the head**. The important signals are breathing and body movement, not electrical brain activity.

The interesting application-development angle is therefore:

**phone microphone + possibly speaker/sonar + ML → breathing/movement patterns → probabilistic sleep-stage estimate.**

A conventional microphone/accelerometer-only app has historically been poor at identifying REM reliably; older validation found ordinary sleep apps couldn't do it.  [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31674096/) But newer deep-learning approaches using the **raw overnight audio** have made this substantially more plausible.

So, yes: **a sleep-quality application requiring nothing but a phone on the nightstand is technically plausible**, including estimated REM. It just wouldn't be equivalent to EEG/polysomnography.
