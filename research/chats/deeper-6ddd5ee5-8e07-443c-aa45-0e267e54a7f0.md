**Subliminal cues could be included as an experimental variable, but they are not an established amplifier of the spelling/wordplay effect.** The relevant hypothesis would be that a cue makes one meaning more accessible, influencing how an ambiguous phrase is interpreted. Research separately supports context-dependent speech perception and some subliminal semantic priming, but those findings do not establish this particular combination.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38383914/)

Three distinctions matter:

**Subliminal is not merely subtle.** An indirect phrase, background sound, or overlooked word is not necessarily below conscious perception. A 2025 analysis of masked linguistic-priming studies found that participants sometimes had measurable awareness of supposedly subliminal words, illustrating why awareness must be tested rather than assumed.  [source 1](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.419)

**Priming is not the same as forming a lasting association.** In one auditory experiment, low-intensity words affected subsequent word-classification speed in slower responders. That supports a constrained processing effect—not a demonstrated change in self-beliefs or a durable name–emotion association.  [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21655277/)

**Not noticing a pairing is different from not perceiving either stimulus.** Someone may consciously hear both words without recognizing their relationship. Studies reporting evaluative learning without conscious knowledge of pairings do not automatically establish learning from entirely imperceptible messages.  [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32980638/)

For your research program, I would compare **ambiguous wording alone, a masked related cue alone, both together, and matched controls**, using fictional names and harmless meanings with consenting participants. Measure what people initially heard separately from what the name evokes later. Include sufficiently sensitive awareness tests; “I didn’t notice anything” alone is inadequate.  [source 4](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.419)

The decisive questions are whether the added cue changes interpretation, whether any association persists, and whether the combination adds more than either component alone. **Those are separate hypotheses—not an established sequence of effects.**
