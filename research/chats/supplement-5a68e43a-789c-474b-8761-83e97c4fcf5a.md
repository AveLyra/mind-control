“Invisible signals” can mean several technically different things, and those differences matter because they determine what the brain actually receives.

One kind is **imperceptible flicker**. The light itself is visible, but it changes so quickly that it looks steady. In experiments around 56–60 Hz, people reported no obvious flicker, yet EEG or MEG showed that their brains were tracking the exact stimulation frequency. Researchers have even used this effect in waking brain–computer interfaces: a person looks at one of several apparently steady targets, each carrying a different hidden flicker pattern, and the computer identifies which one they are attending to from the brain response. This proves the brain can retain information that never becomes an obvious visual experience. It does not prove the hidden pattern is automatically understood as language. A related technique is **invisible spectral flicker**. Instead of making a lamp brighter and darker, researchers alternate between different mixtures of wavelengths that look almost like the same white light. To the person, the light appears nearly unchanged, but the retina and brain still respond differently. Human studies have measured 40 Hz brain responses from this sort of nearly imperceptible modulation. This is especially interesting because the “hidden” part is carried in the spectrum rather than in an obvious on/off flash. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

A second, very different meaning of invisible is **a wavelength outside normal human vision**. Near-infrared light is ordinarily invisible. In 2025, experimental contact lenses containing upconversion materials absorbed infrared and re-emitted visible light. Participants could distinguish infrared flashing patterns, including Morse-like temporal codes. The signals could work through closed eyelids, partly because eyelids block visible background light more strongly than the infrared used in the experiment. But the crucial point is that the contact lens converted the signal into something the wearer could actually see. So the carrier was invisible, but the final message was not subconscious. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

That creates an important distinction between the **carrier** and the **experience**. A radio wave is invisible, but a radio turns it into audible sound. Likewise, an infrared signal can be invisible in the environment while a device converts it into a perceptible signal for the recipient. Calling the carrier invisible does not mean the information bypasses consciousness.

There is also a third category: **signals that remain below awareness but still affect physiology**. Lighting conditions can be engineered to look almost identical while stimulating retinal pathways differently, producing different melatonin or circadian responses. During sleep, flashing light through closed eyelids can shift the body clock without fully waking someone. In these cases, the signal is influencing biology without acting like a consciously understood message. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

Finally, sleep adds another layer. Closed eyelids do not completely block temporal light signals. Researchers have measured stimulus-linked brain responses during non-REM sleep. But sleep changes how the visual system processes timing: some frequencies are weakened, others become relatively stronger, and cortical processing changes substantially. So a hidden signal that works well in an awake person cannot simply be assumed to work the same way in a sleeper. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

The cleanest way to think about all of this is:

**Invisible carrier → detectable signal → distinguishable pattern → understood meaning.**

Research firmly demonstrates the first three under various conditions. What has not been demonstrated is that an ordinary unaware sleeper can reliably receive an invisible encoded signal and unconsciously translate it into arbitrary semantic instructions. The brain can detect far more than consciousness reports, but **detection is not the same as comprehension**.
