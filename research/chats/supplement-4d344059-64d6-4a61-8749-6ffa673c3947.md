**The parallel you are asking about is how deliberately adopting a mental focus can change processes that are not themselves consciously micromanaged.** That is a useful connection between imagery, attention, hypnosis, and some placebo effects. My earlier discussion of concealed instructions diverted from that question.

There is also a wording correction: **absorption is associated with hypnotic responsiveness; it is not interchangeable with responsiveness, and neither is synonymous with general persuadability.** Saying simply “feeling absorbed is not becoming easier to influence” obscured that relationship.  [source 0](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-3514.60.1.144)  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207144.2020.1685330)

## 1. Resolving the apparent contradiction about absorption

Three things need separating: someone’s usual tendency to become deeply immersed, how immersed they are at a particular moment, and how strongly they respond to particular hypnotic suggestions.

The 1991 studies involving **475 and 434 participants** support a relationship between the first and third: people with a greater general tendency toward absorption tended to be more hypnotically responsive. They did not experimentally establish that increasing someone’s momentary absorption causes a corresponding increase in responsiveness. That causal connection is not disproved; it simply was not what those studies demonstrated.  [source 2](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-3514.60.1.144)

The drumming study also needs precise interpretation. Changing whether participants experienced drumming before or after hypnosis affected subjective trance-related measures, but not their scores on the standard hypnotic-response measure. This shows that **feeling more trance-like and performing more hypnotic responses need not move together**. It does not demonstrate that absorption is irrelevant.  [source 3](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029157.1997.10403417)

A separate study of 110 students compared nine measures of suggestibility, including hypnosis, persuasion, conformity, and responses to misleading questions. It found no strong general relationship between hypnosis and the other measures. Thus, a capacity for immersive suggested experiences should not automatically be described as a general tendency to accept other people’s claims.  [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207144.2020.1685330)

The better formulation is:

**Absorption may facilitate engagement with an imagined or suggested experience. Its relationship with the resulting response depends on the person, the suggestion, and the context.**

## 2. What “tasks focused on appearance reduced it” actually means

Here, “appearance” meant **physical features such as shape**, not attractiveness or concern about appearances.

In this research program, participants could encounter the same kind of object picture while answering different questions. One task emphasized meaning—whether the object was living or nonliving. Another emphasized appearance—whether its shape was round or elongated. They then encountered a briefly masked word followed by a visible word, and researchers measured whether the first word influenced processing of the second.  [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22470329/)

A simple illustration is the difference between looking at an apple while asking *“Is this something edible?”* and asking *“Is this shape approximately circular?”* The input can be similar, but the mental operation is different.

Kiefer and Martens’s 2010 experiments found that a preceding meaning-focused task strengthened the subsequent **semantic-priming effect**. A perceptual task weakened it. An important measure was the N400: a brain response sensitive to aspects of meaning processing. The relevant finding was a difference in responses to meaning-related versus unrelated word pairs—not a measurement of belief or agreement.  [source 6](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019561)

The follow-up is especially informative: **appearance-focused processing did not suppress all unconscious processing.** It facilitated a different kind of priming involving shapes and motor responses. Meaning-focused processing favored word meaning; perceptual processing favored visually guided responses.  [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610397056)

An accessible interpretation is that the brain temporarily prioritizes the kind of information relevant to the current task, including information that does not reach reportable awareness. These settings are dynamic: changing the delay between tasks could change—and sometimes reverse—the pattern. It is not a lasting “meaning off” switch. Also, *induction task* in these papers means establishing a task orientation; it does **not** mean hypnotic induction.  [source 8](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2012.00061/full)

## 3. Could an audible word be camouflaged? What semantic relationship matters?

**Yes: detecting a sound and identifying a word within it are different capacities.** There is a study more directly relevant to camouflage than the quiet-word experiment.

Kouider and Dupoux’s 2005 experiment embedded spoken words within an audible stream of speech-like sounds. Participants could not consciously identify the hidden words, yet hearing them influenced later recognition of the same words. This survived a change in the speaker’s voice, suggesting processing beyond the exact acoustic waveform. However, the demonstrated effect was principally **word-identity processing**, not broad comprehension of meaning.  [source 9](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01584.x)

That distinction matters. A masked word can leave a usable trace without the experiment showing that an entire sentence has been understood.

The 2011 Daltrozzo study investigated a different effect: whether a quiet word helped people recognize a subsequent **meaning-related** word. In the slower half of the 24-person analyzed sample, responses were approximately 48 milliseconds faster for related than unrelated pairs. But participants detected the presence of the quiet sound about 87% of the time, and their ability to classify the quiet stimuli retained slight above-chance sensitivity across the full sample. The authors used additional analyses to argue for priming without successful conscious categorization. “Completely imperceptible speech” would therefore be too strong a description.  [source 10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105000/)

**The semantic relationship is between the first word and the later target—not necessarily between the word and the surrounding camouflage.** Illustrative relationships include:

- A shared category, such as **dog → cat**.

- An established association, such as **doctor → nurse**.

- Exact repetition, such as **dog → dog**, which tests repetition priming rather than a relationship between different meanings.

The 2011 study used categorical or associative relationships. The surrounding sound need not carry a related meaning; its role in a masking experiment is to interfere with conscious identification. There is no universal degree of semantic similarity that guarantees an effect, and results from exact-word repetition cannot automatically be treated as evidence for understanding a different, related word.  [source 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105000/)  [source 12](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01584.x)

So, **audible camouflage is experimentally possible, but whether word identity, meaning, or something more complex survives the camouflage must be tested separately**.

## 4. What the tennis study adds—and related imagery research

Rhodes and Leach’s 2026 study compared **38 competitive tennis players over six weeks** across Functional Imagery Training, internal visual imagery, and a control condition without imagery training. Both imagery conditions improved serve accuracy, with greater improvement under Functional Imagery Training. Measured imagery ability and preference did not predict improvement.  [source 13](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.2025-0161)

Functional Imagery Training combines multisensory imagery with personally meaningful motivation. Conceptually, the difference is between seeing a successful serve in your mind and rehearsing a more complete experience involving movement, sound, emotion, and the purpose of the action. This is a description of the approach, not a reconstruction of that paper’s exact script.

Two qualifications matter. First, the intervention was a **package**: the comparison does not isolate whether its advantage came from additional senses, motivation, practice structure, or their combination. Second, with 38 participants, failure to find a relationship with imagery ability is not proof that ability never matters. I verified the abstract and publication record, but could not access the complete report to audit its precise effect sizes and allocation procedures.  [source 14](https://doi.org/10.1123/jsep.2025-0161)

Two related studies sharpen the connection:

**Motivation and endurance.** A 2021 study initially recruited 31 self-described non-runners; 15 later agreed to consider an ultramarathon and were randomly assigned to Functional Imagery Training or motivational interviewing. The imagery group was more likely to finish, with a reported relative risk of 5.25. That is an interesting preliminary result from a very small, selected sample—not a reliable population estimate. Its relevance is to sustained action and coping with barriers, rather than simply picturing victory.  [source 15](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/psy-research/391/)

**Imagery training and hypnotizability.** A particularly relevant 2026 study examined 15 days of motor-imagery training in people with low, medium, and high hypnotizability. Reported imagery effectiveness and EEG measures changed across all groups. This directly connects imagery practice, hypnotizability, and the neural processing of imagined movement. It does not establish that the training increased hypnotizability or improved competitive athletic performance.  [source 16](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207144.2026.2669243)

Together, these findings suggest that the useful skill is not necessarily **“making the sharpest mental picture.”** It may involve learning to engage the representations, feelings, and action plans relevant to the goal.

## 5. Imagining heaviness versus experiencing heaviness “by itself”

Your suggestion about imagined authority comes close to one important theory—but **external authority and the sense of agency are different questions**.

The *sense of agency* is the experience that “I am initiating this.” Someone can generate an action through their own nervous system without experiencing all its production as deliberate. Conversely, someone can knowingly follow another person’s instructions while retaining a strong sense that “I am choosing to do this.”

One account of hypnosis, called **cold-control theory**, proposes that people intentionally produce a response but have reduced or inaccurate awareness of the intention producing it. On this account, the difference is partly **metacognitive**: not necessarily a different underlying capacity, but a different experience of how that capacity is being exercised. An imagined outside authority is not essential to that explanation.  [source 17](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)

A 2021 experiment directly tested this distinction. Highly suggestible participants were asked either to respond to a posthypnotic suggestion that words would appear meaningless, or voluntarily to imagine the words as meaningless. Both conditions reduced interference on a Stroop task. Expectations were comparable, and the analysis supported equivalent performance benefits. Yet participants described the voluntary condition more as something they imagined and controlled, and the hypnotic condition more as something they perceived. **The performance could be similar while the experience of producing it differed.**  [source 18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)

That is a strong parallel with mental rehearsal: conscious participation can organize subsequent processing without every component feeling effortfully generated.

However, it is not the only pattern. In a 2023 study, both imagery instructions and hypnotic suggestions changed representations of body-part length, but the hypnotic effects were larger. Responses to the two conditions were strongly related across participants. Deliberate imagery did not adequately explain the results by itself; response expectations and spontaneously arising imagery also appeared relevant.  [source 19](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2023.103473)

Other theories emphasize changes in monitoring and control, learned response expectations, or the influence of predictions on perception. **There is no agreed single mechanism that explains every hypnotic response.** “Imagining that somebody else is responsible” is therefore one possible strategy or attribution, not an established universal explanation.  [source 20](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2024.103730)

### Is that mostly placebo?

**There is meaningful overlap, but “internally generated” is broader than “placebo.”** Otherwise, learning a skill, changing an interpretation, or practicing an emotional response would all become placebo effects by definition, which would obscure rather than explain their mechanisms.

A useful experiment compared hypnotic, cognitive-behavioral, and placebo-control pain interventions in 124 participants. Expected relief and treatment credibility statistically mediated treatment effects. But hypnotic suggestibility predicted relief specifically in the hypnotic condition. This supports shared mechanisms involving expectation while also suggesting that the interventions were not interchangeable. Mediation analyses do not, by themselves, establish the complete causal mechanism.  [source 21](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02879898)

Your comparison with psychotherapy is therefore useful at the level of **experience, learning, expectation, and attention changing subsequent experience**. But the research does not provide a defensible percentage for how much hypnosis is “mostly placebo,” nor establish that all psychotherapy benefits reduce to expectation.

The better question is: **What does the specific intervention add beyond a similarly credible comparison with matched attention and expectations?** That is more informative than dividing effects into “mental” versus “real.”

## 6. What the movement experiments actually show

**The 2005 arm-heaviness study.** Researchers tracked arm movement in three dimensions while highly hypnotizable participants responded to heaviness suggestions and low-hypnotizable participants simulated the response. The groups differed in reported involuntariness and aspects of movement timing and trajectory. Comparisons with highly hypnotizable participants while awake also suggested that hypnosis amplified some existing movement patterns while changing others.  [source 22](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-004-2112-x)

The authors argued against a straightforward role-playing explanation. The narrower conclusion is that **successful hypnotic responding and instructed simulation were not identical on the measured outcomes**. Movement differences alone cannot rule out expectations or other cognitive mechanisms: those mechanisms can themselves change movement. Nor does a comparison between initially different groups isolate one unique “hypnotic state.”

**The 2009 brain-imaging study.** Cojan and colleagues compared normal performance, hypnotically suggested hand paralysis, and feigned paralysis. Preparatory motor activity remained present during suggested paralysis. Changes instead involved regions associated with imagery and self-monitoring, with a different pattern from ordinary deliberate response withholding. The authors interpreted this as internal representations guiding behavior, rather than the motor system simply being switched off.  [source 23](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2009.05.021)

**The 2026 arm-rigidity study.** Participants who reported similar experiences of inability sometimes behaved differently. Some visibly tried to move and trembled; others described not initiating movement because it felt impossible. The analysis involved 20 selected responders, after three participants whose rigidity response failed were excluded. This is preliminary evidence that **a similar subjective feeling can arise through different cognitive–motor routes**, not a definitive model of everyone’s hypnotic experience.  [source 24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13181418/)

The broader lesson is that “involuntary” is not one simple physical category. It can describe how an action or inhibition is experienced, while the mechanisms producing it differ.

## 7. What the hypnotizability-training study tells us

Gearan, Schoenberger, and Kirsch’s 1995 study compared the original Carleton Skills Training Program with a modified version that **removed instructions to physically enact the suggested response**.

Both versions increased behavioral and subjective responsiveness, with no statistically significant difference in improvement between them. Across conditions, increased use of imagination was associated with stronger behavioral responses and stronger experiences. Increased deliberate enactment was associated only with compliance.  [source 25](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207149508409376)

This matters because it separates two possibilities: learning how to perform the outward behavior, and learning how to generate the corresponding experience. The modified program suggests that improvement did not require teaching people simply to act out the expected result.

But it does not identify imagination as the sole causal ingredient. Training, familiarity, expectations, attention, and practice could all contribute; a correlation between increased imagination and increased responsiveness does not isolate their causal order. Also, “no significant difference” between programs is not proof of exact equivalence.  [source 26](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207149508409376)

**The common thread across these literatures is skilled regulation of experience:** attention determines what gets processed, imagery supplies a representation, expectations influence what is anticipated, and agency-related processes influence whether the outcome feels deliberately produced. That synthesis fits the parallels you asked about without requiring all these phenomena to be the same process.  [source 27](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610397056)  [source 28](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2023.103473)  [source 29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2020.11.006)
