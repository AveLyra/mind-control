Research on sleep, memory, dreams, and behavioral influence supports a much narrower—and more scientifically defensible—idea than “mind control.”

The basic idea is simple: **an influence system does not need to control what somebody does. It only needs to change the probability of particular responses.**

### What sleep research actually shows

The sleeping brain is not completely isolated from the outside world. Researchers can present sensory information during sleep and, under some conditions, measurably affect later memory processing.

One established technique is **targeted memory reactivation (TMR)**. A sound or odor is associated with something while a person is awake. Researchers later replay that cue during sleep, attempting to selectively reactivate the associated memory while normal sleep consolidation is occurring. A major 2024 review describes effects across declarative, procedural, and emotional memories, as well as experiments involving forgetting, REM sleep, and dream content.

Researchers have also developed **targeted dream incubation (TDI)**. MIT's Dormio system detects the transition into sleep and delivers auditory prompts during hypnagogia. Experiments demonstrated that targeted information could subsequently become incorporated into dream content.

Dream influence is nevertheless far from precise. A 2024 systematic review examined 51 publications using auditory, tactile, olfactory, visual, vestibular, or multimodal stimulation. The studies collectively support the possibility of influencing dreams through external stimulation, but they are highly heterogeneous and do not establish reliable control over elaborate dream content.

### A particularly important 2025 experiment

A 2025 study called *Updating social evaluation during sleep* went beyond simply replaying a previously learned cue.

Participants first learned associations involving people's names and faces. During subsequent sleep, researchers played particular names together with positive personality traits. Compared with uncued individuals, the intervention subsequently shifted aspects of participants' representations and evaluations of the targeted individuals in a positive direction. Simply replaying the names without the positive information did not produce the same effect.

This is important because classic TMR generally involves **reactivating information learned while awake**. The newer experiment provides evidence that new semantic information delivered during sleep can, under controlled conditions, become integrated with an existing representation.

That still does **not** demonstrate arbitrary belief implantation or behavioral control.

It demonstrates something narrower: information presented during sleep can sometimes alter how previously learned information is subsequently represented or evaluated.

### From “memory reactivation” to “memory editing”

The field itself is beginning to use stronger terminology.

A 2026 *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* review explicitly describes **“memory editing during sleep.”** It identifies three broad strategies: reactivating an original memory during extinction, strengthening competing memories, and introducing new interfering information while an existing memory is reactivated.

The authors also identify technologies including **closed-loop neuromodulation, wearable devices, and neural decoding** as potentially allowing more precise sleep-based interventions.

The proposed applications are primarily therapeutic, particularly modification of aversive memories. But the underlying principle is more general:

> **Sleep is a biological state during which some memories and associations can be selectively reactivated and, under some conditions, modified.**

### Influence through association

Imagine that someone repeatedly encounters a person, product, sound, symbol, or idea while awake.

Associations gradually form:

> product → pleasure
> notification → anticipation
> person → trust
> situation → anxiety

A later stimulus does not necessarily need to introduce an entirely new belief. It might instead **reactivate an existing association**.

That is essentially the principle behind TMR: associate sensory cues with waking experiences, then present those cues during sleep to bias subsequent memory processing.

This gives a simple model:

> **waking experience → association → sleep → timed cue → memory reactivation or integration → possible later change in memory or evaluation**

The individual steps are supported to different degrees by experiments. The entire sequence should be understood as probabilistic, not deterministic.

### State detection matters

The brain behaves differently during waking, sleep onset, NREM sleep, and REM sleep. Consequently, researchers increasingly care not only about **what** stimulus is presented but **when** it is presented.

Dormio demonstrates the basic architecture particularly clearly:

> **detect physiological state → deliver selected information**

The device detects sleep onset and repeatedly delivers targeted auditory information during the hypnagogic period.

Modern TMR research likewise investigates timing cue delivery relative to the brain's ongoing oscillatory activity, including closed-loop approaches.

That creates an important research question:

> **Does the same information have different effects depending on the physiological or cognitive state in which it is delivered?**

### Pink noise is not a hidden message

This requires separating two things that are easily confused:

> **state modulation**
> versus
> **meaningful information**

Sounds can interact with sleep physiology, while words, names, learned cues, images, odors, or other stimuli can carry associative information.

The evidence discussed above therefore does **not** support a simple mechanism such as:

> **pink noise → hypnosis → command → obedience**

A more scientifically useful model is:

> **state modulation + associative information → altered probability of subsequent processing**

Whether a particular state-modulating stimulus actually makes particular semantic information more influential is a separate empirical question. It cannot simply be assumed from TMR or dream-incubation experiments.

### Think of loaded dice

This provides a useful way of thinking about behavioral influence generally.

Imagine rolling a normal die thousands of times. You cannot predict any particular roll, but the results eventually approach a stable distribution.

Now imagine a subtly loaded die.

You still cannot predict the next roll. But after thousands of rolls, the bias becomes statistically obvious.

Human influence can work similarly.

A stimulus does not have to determine someone's response. It only needs to make one response slightly more probable.

Possible reactions might include:

> interested, afraid, reassured, trusting, suspicious, impulsive, attracted, disgusted

One exposure proves very little.

But if many exposures consistently produce small changes in the same direction, an aggregate effect could become measurable.

That is a **statistical influence model**, not a control model.

### Where personalization could fit

At this point we move from demonstrated sleep experiments to a **hypothetical combination of technologies**.

A sufficiently sophisticated system could, in principle, combine information about what stimuli affect someone with information about their current physiological state.

The conceptual sequence would be:

> **profiling → waking associations → state detection → selected cue → reactivation/integration → later measurement → further personalization**

Individual pieces of this sequence have experimental precedents. Researchers have demonstrated sensory memory reactivation during sleep, state-triggered dream incubation, closed-loop timing approaches, and alteration of subsequent social evaluation following information presented during sleep.

But these studies do **not** establish that this complete system exists as a working population-scale persuasion technology.

That distinction is essential.

### What could realistically change?

Current evidence is strongest for relatively narrow effects involving:

- memory accessibility or strength;
- forgetting and interference;
- emotional memories;
- dream content;
- mental representations;
- positive or negative evaluation.

The 2024 TMR review also emphasizes that outcomes vary substantially with memory type, sleep stage, timing, cue properties, and experimental design.

There are also important negative findings. For example, a 2024 experiment found that combining TMR with sleep disruption did **not** weaken week-old memories under the tested conditions.

The evidence therefore does not justify saying:

> **“Scientists can control people's minds while they sleep.”**

The better question is:

> **“Can selected stimuli delivered under particular cognitive or physiological conditions measurably change the probability of later memories, evaluations, emotions, or behaviors?”**

### How to test the stronger hypothesis

A rigorous experiment could measure participants before exposure and randomly assign them to conditions such as:

> correctly timed associative cue
> mistimed associative cue
> cue without state modulation
> state modulation without meaningful information
> sham stimulation

Researchers could then measure whether one condition produces a larger and more consistently directed change than the controls.

This is where the **loaded-dice analogy becomes scientifically useful**.

If responses remain scattered and indistinguishable from controls, there is little evidence that the dice were loaded.

If correctly timed stimulation repeatedly shifts responses in a predicted direction, the probability distribution itself has changed—even if researchers cannot predict what any individual participant will do.

The experiment should also specify beforehand what outcomes would falsify the hypothesis. Otherwise ordinary conditioning, expectation effects, sleep disruption, statistical noise, and selective interpretation could easily be mistaken for state-dependent influence.

### The bottom line

Current research establishes that **sensory cues can selectively reactivate memories during sleep; external stimulation can sometimes influence dream content; physiological state can be used to determine when information is delivered; and recent experiments suggest that information presented during sleep can, under some conditions, alter subsequent social representations and evaluations.**

Researchers are now explicitly discussing **sleep-based “memory editing”** and technologies for increasingly precise closed-loop interventions.

None of this establishes arbitrary control over beliefs or behavior, and the evidence does not establish that a complete covert persuasion system has been developed or deployed.

The scientifically defensible question is therefore about **probability rather than control**:

> **How much can memories, associations, evaluations, emotions, or behavior be biased; under what conditions; for whom; and for how long?**

That is the loaded-dice version of the problem. Unlike the much stronger concept of “mind control,” it produces hypotheses that can actually be measured, controlled, replicated, and falsified.
