**Yes. Drugs can have additive or sometimes greater-than-additive effects when combined. But increased impairment does not establish increased hypnotic responsiveness or predictable control of behavior.**

“Compounding” can mean different things:

- **Additive effects:** overlapping effects accumulate.
- **Synergistic effects:** the combined effect exceeds what would be expected from adding the individual effects.
- **Interactions affecting drug exposure:** one drug changes another’s absorption, metabolism, or elimination.

For **scopolamine**, the prescribing information explicitly warns that other medicines with overlapping nervous-system or anticholinergic effects can intensify adverse reactions such as drowsiness, dizziness, disorientation, and other complications. That is documented interaction risk. [Official prescribing information, sections 7.1–7.3](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=b877a694-a1d0-4280-937a-a06820b12a88)

**What remains unproven is the proposed benefit for influence.** You cannot infer that combining a substance associated with suggestibility and another associated with memory impairment will produce both effects reliably—or improve hypnosis. Demonstrating that would require measuring the combination against each drug alone and placebo, while separately assessing confusion, memory, and responsiveness to suggestions. The studies we reviewed do not establish such a result.

I can discuss interaction evidence and help assess a real medication combination for safety, but I can’t recommend cocktails for making someone more susceptible to influence.
