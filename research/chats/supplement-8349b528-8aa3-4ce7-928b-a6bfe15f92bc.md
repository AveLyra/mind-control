The research supports a narrow but important conclusion: **sleeping people can be physiologically and neurally influenced by signals they do not consciously notice, but there is no solid evidence that complex thoughts, beliefs, or instructions can be secretly “written” into an ordinary sleeper using invisible or subliminal signals.**

Light can affect the sleeping brain even through closed eyelids. Experiments have shown stimulus-locked visual brain responses during sleep, and timed light flashes can shift circadian rhythms without fully waking the person. Visually subtle or nearly imperceptible light changes can also alter measurable biological responses such as melatonin. This means that **lack of conscious awareness does not mean lack of biological effect**. [upload ancestry 0](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 0a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28098351/) [editorial source 0b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22564396/) [editorial source 0c](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/) [upload ancestry 1](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 1a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29788219/) [editorial source 1b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35998110/)

There is also strong evidence that the brain can detect patterns that the person does not consciously perceive. Rapid flicker around 40–60 Hz can appear visually steady while still producing frequency-specific EEG or MEG responses, and such hidden modulation has been used in waking brain–computer interfaces. Invisible near-infrared signals can also carry temporal information through closed eyelids when specialized contact lenses convert them into visible light. [upload ancestry 2](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 2a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615063/) [editorial source 2b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36669202/) [editorial source 2c](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35570490/) [editorial source 2d](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/) [upload ancestry 3](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 3a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

What is much less established is **meaningful subconscious communication during normal sleep**. A sleeping brain may register that pattern A differs from pattern B without understanding that one means “yes,” “danger,” or “trust this person.” Sleep also changes sensory processing substantially, and higher-frequency signals that work well in awake subjects may be weakened or processed differently during sleep. [upload ancestry 4](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 4a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28334175/) [editorial source 4b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-73997-y)

More complex influence is possible under special circumstances. Lucid dreamers have sometimes received questions during REM sleep and answered them, including one participant interpreting Morse-coded flashes. But those people were consciously experiencing communication within dreams, so this is not evidence of subliminal manipulation. Separately, some sleep-learning studies suggest that previously learned associations or social evaluations can be modestly updated during sleep. That is closer to **nudging or reactivating existing associations** than implanting new detailed ideas. [upload ancestry 5](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 5a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/) [editorial source 5b](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/) [upload ancestry 6](../../sources/upload-recovery.md#receipt-8349b528-8aa3-4ce7-928b-a6bfe15f92bc) [editorial source 6a](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-025-00356-9) [editorial source 6b](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-024-00244-8) [editorial source 6c](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/)

So the research currently supports this continuum:

**invisible stimulus → unconscious neural/physiological response: well demonstrated;
invisible stimulus → limited conditioning or association changes: plausible and partly demonstrated;
invisible stimulus → reliable hidden semantic messages during ordinary sleep: not established;
invisible stimulus → precise control of beliefs, memories, or behavior: not demonstrated.**

The critical distinction is between the brain **responding to a signal** and the sleeper **understanding, remembering, and acting on its meaning**. Those are separate experimental achievements.
