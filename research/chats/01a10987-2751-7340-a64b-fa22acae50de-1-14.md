Yes, but the research is limited, and “hypnotizing someone while they’re asleep” can overstate what was tested.

In small sleep-lab studies from the 1960s, some participants responded to simple instructions given during light sleep—for example, moving a pillow or scratching their nose when they later heard a cue word. Researchers reported responses without EEG signs of waking, and participants often didn’t remember them afterward. The relationship to hypnotic suggestibility was mixed; the researchers cautioned that sleep and hypnosis aren’t interchangeable states. [Evans et al., 1969](https://www.psych.upenn.edu/history/orne/evansetal1969jnmd467476.html)

A 2023 review of 51 studies found evidence that simple forms of learning, such as conditioning or getting used to a sound, can happen during sleep. Whether people can reliably learn complex information or carry it into waking behavior remains unsettled. [Ataei et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37778137/)

There’s also research on listening to a hypnosis recording *before* sleep to influence sleep itself. That’s different from delivering suggestions to someone who is already asleep. [Cordi et al., 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24882909/)

So: sleeping people can sometimes respond to simple cues, but the research doesn’t show that someone can reliably be hypnotized in deep sleep to adopt major beliefs or change habits.
