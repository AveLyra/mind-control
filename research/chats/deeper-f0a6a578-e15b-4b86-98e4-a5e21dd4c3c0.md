# Sleep and digital social experience — proposed research program

The central question would be:

Does encountering related information across phones, computers, and video content change how that information is processed during subsequent sleep—and can later effects be separated into memory, emotion, evaluation, and behavior?

The key is **not to start with an elaborate coordinated environment and see whether “something happens.”** Build several experiments that can distinguish coordination from repetition, sleep-dependent effects from waking persuasion, and memory-specific changes from general sleep disruption.

## A common experimental platform

I would start with consenting adults using a **research phone app, a controlled computer interface, and a prerecorded video stream containing mock commercials**. Use fictional brands, characters, and organizations so that prior preferences and commercial interests do not dominate the results.

Each stimulus would have an identifier linking its appearance across devices. Log actual presentation—not merely scheduled delivery—and match exposure count, duration, spacing, and content between comparison groups. Initially, use **one calibrated, logged sound source during EEG-monitored sleep**, with the other devices silent and screens off. That prevents differences between devices from becoming differences in nighttime stimulation.

Participants should know that the study includes daytime media and possible sleep-time cues, while remaining unaware of the particular target selected for cueing. Any additional concealment would need ethics review and debriefing.

## Twelve candidate experiments

**1. Does cross-device coordination add anything beyond repetition?**

Present the same fictional entities either repeatedly on one device or across phone, computer, and video. Keep the number and timing of exposures equivalent. Within each condition, randomly select some learned cues for sleep replay and leave others uncued.

Measure retention before and after sleep.

The crucial result is an **interaction**: does sleep cueing produce a larger benefit after cross-device exposure than after equally frequent single-device exposure? A simple advantage for repeated content would not establish a special coordination effect.

**2. Can ordinary, incidental media exposure establish a usable sleep cue?**

Compare explicit learning—participants intentionally associate a sound with an object or event—with incidental exposure, where the same association appears within a mock feed or commercial while participants perform another task.

Later, replay selected cues during sleep and test the associated information.

This addresses an important translation gap: **does the effect require deliberate laboratory training, or can an association acquired incidentally be sufficient?** A separate validation group could measure pre-sleep learning without making every experimental participant explicitly rehearse the association.

**3. Does the cue depend on the exact sound, or on its meaning?**

After identical daytime learning, compare sleep presentation of the original recording, a different recording of the same word or melody, and an acoustically matched unrelated cue.

For example, a fictional character’s name could appear in text on a computer, be spoken in a video, and be associated with a distinctive sound in the phone app.

The outcome would distinguish **specific acoustic recognition from broader semantic generalization**. This is more informative than treating all related sounds, words, and images as interchangeable inputs.

**4. Mock-commercial experiment: memory versus preference**

Show matched commercials for fictional, low-stakes products such as notebooks. Associate each product with a different sound. Randomly replay selected sounds during sleep.

Measure product recognition, remembered attributes, liking, and a later choice between otherwise equivalent products. Include a matched waking-replay condition and a no-replay condition.

The distinction is whether cueing changes **what people remember**, **what they prefer**, or both. Keep compensation independent of their choices. A 2025 social-evaluation experiment motivates testing evaluative outcomes, but its authors acknowledged unresolved mechanisms, absent long-term follow-up, and the need for waking controls. It does not establish that sleep-based advertising works.  [source 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

**5. Social approval versus nonsocial reward**

Use a simulated cooperative task in which equivalent successes receive either positive peer feedback or nonsocial feedback such as points. Pair those experiences with counterbalanced cues, then compare cued and uncued memories after sleep.

Measure willingness to reengage, memory for the feedback, and subsequent cooperation—not just ratings of how rewarding the task felt.

This is particularly well aligned with Dahl’s interest in social-emotional development. His experimental work has examined sleep-related changes in emotional functioning using both standardized stimuli and peer-interaction contexts. The proposed study would investigate consolidation of positive social experiences rather than reproduce sleep restriction.  [source 3](https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jcpp.12568)

**6. Reinforcing an emotional-regulation skill across devices**

Teach participants a benign reappraisal exercise: interpreting an ambiguous fictional interaction in a less threatening way. Reinforce the exercise through a computer task, a phone practice session, and a short video, all associated with a cue.

Compare relevant sleep cueing with matched unrelated sounds and no sound. Subsequently present a new, similarly ambiguous scenario.

The important outcome is **transfer of the regulation skill**, separately from remembering the training. This has a closer experimental precedent than generalized persuasion: Recher and colleagues found additional changes in distress, arousal, and vividness after sleep cueing following waking imagery rescripting.  [source 4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-024-03192-4)

**7. Dream-content biasing without interpreting coincidences**

Give participants two distinctive, neutral experiences—such as a navigation task and a music task—each associated with a cue. Randomly replay one cue during monitored REM sleep.

Collect dream reports using identical, non-leading prompts. Have independent raters, blind to cue assignment, score reports against a coding scheme fixed beforehand. Match the awakening schedule across conditions.

Existing work supports testing whether cued task elements become more frequent in reports. The stronger design question is whether that finding survives strict blinding and predetermined scoring—not whether a researcher can retrospectively connect an ambiguous dream to the stimulus.  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

**8. New association formation versus reactivation of old information**

Establish fictional identities during waking, but reserve some **arbitrary identity–attribute pairings** for sleep. Compare correctly paired presentation, shuffled pairings containing the same words, and matched waking presentation.

Test the particular associations afterward, rather than merely asking whether the identity seems more positive.

This would address an unresolved issue in the 2025 evaluation study: whether sleep presentation introduces new associative information or changes the accessibility of already learned information. Arbitrary pairings make it harder for ordinary semantic expectations to explain the result.  [source 6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

**9. Source memory and confidence**

Distribute different harmless facts about fictional events across the phone, computer, and video. After selective sleep cueing, test both factual recall and source attribution: “Where did you encounter this?”

Also measure confidence and recognition of never-presented details.

This separates improved recall from **misattributing a source, increased familiarity, or misplaced confidence**. A participant remembering a fact more readily does not necessarily mean they remember its origin accurately. Use fictional material—not suggestions about participants’ actual autobiographical experiences.

**10. Competition between alternative memories**

Teach two successive versions of a neutral fictional story or two object-location mappings that share a cue. Randomize whether the later version appears on the same device or a different device. Replay selected cues during sleep.

Test recall and recognition of both versions, including which version intrudes when the other is requested.

The question is whether cross-device context helps keep memories separate or changes which version is preferentially retrieved. Define “forgetting” as a measured reduction on particular tests; do not equate it with permanent erasure.

**11. Repeated exposure at home: accumulation, saturation, or interference**

After a laboratory effect is replicated, move the same research materials into an opt-in home study. Randomize participants to relevant cueing, matched control cueing, or no cueing, and test immediate and delayed outcomes.

Ask whether effects accumulate, plateau, disappear, or interfere with other memories. Use fresh stimulus sets where necessary rather than assuming a brief “washout” removes previous learning.

Automated home TMR has a published proof of concept, but the studied EEG-linked system also had timing and generalizability limitations. A home experiment should independently verify delivered cues and sleep-state classification rather than assume the device label guarantees them.  [source 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)

**12. A developmental extension focused on resilience**

Only after adult feasibility and safety work, consider an adolescent study built around **positive learning, emotion regulation, or sleep protection**, with parental permission, adolescent assent, and youth involvement in design.

For example, compare usual routines with a sleep-support intervention while measuring consolidation of identical positive digital experiences. An initial adolescent version need not include nighttime cue delivery.

This would test whether better sleep changes the persistence of helpful digital learning. It fits Dahl’s developmental perspective without presuming that adolescents are simply more susceptible to influence or making commercial preference manipulation the first youth application.  [source 8](https://vcresearch.berkeley.edu/faculty/ronald-dahl)

## Controls that would make these experiments interpretable

**Separate content effects from stimulation effects.** Use learned cues, acoustically matched unassociated cues, and no-sound conditions. Measure sleep continuity, arousals, next-day sleepiness, and adverse experiences alongside the psychological outcome. If all sounds produce similar changes, a memory-specific account is weakened.

**Treat sleep stage and precise timing as separate questions.** First test whether a cue works during a verified sleep stage. Only then compare timing relative to particular oscillations. Verify actual sound onset against the recorded EEG. “The software intended to cue an up-state” is not evidence that it did.

**Do not infer absence of awareness from absence of recall.** Combine sleep recordings with prespecified recognition and awareness assessments. Report waking contamination and cue-related arousals; do not quietly remove inconvenient participants after inspecting outcomes.

**Protect randomization and control analytical flexibility.** Prespecify one primary outcome and the decisive between-condition comparison for each study. Analyze participants and stimulus items appropriately; thousands of presentations do not constitute thousands of independent participants. Directly test differences between conditions rather than claiming a difference because one condition was significant and another was not.

**Explore broadly, confirm narrowly.** Use feasibility work to establish tolerability, delivery accuracy, usable sleep recordings, and task difficulty. Then select a few hypotheses for adequately powered, preregistered replication. Power the cross-device-by-cueing interaction itself, not merely the simpler overall cueing effect.

For the initial program, I would avoid actual ad networks, private-message monitoring, deliberately distressing content, and automated personalization aimed at intensifying emotional responses. A fixed, auditable research schedule is both safer and easier to interpret.
