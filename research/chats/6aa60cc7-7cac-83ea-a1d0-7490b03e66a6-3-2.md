Research on sleep, memory, dream incubation, and behavioral influence points toward a more realistic concept of subconscious influence than “mind control.”

The central distinction is simple: **an influence system does not need to control what a person does. It only needs to change the probability of particular responses.**

## What sleep research actually shows

The sleeping brain is not completely isolated from its environment. Sensory cues presented during sleep can sometimes affect ongoing memory processing. One established method, **targeted memory reactivation (TMR)**, associates information with a sound or odor during waking learning and presents that cue again during sleep. A 2024 review describes evidence that TMR can affect declarative, procedural, and emotional memories, and in some circumstances promote forgetting or influence dream content.

Researchers have also demonstrated **targeted dream incubation (TDI)**. MIT's Dormio system detects the transition into early sleep and delivers auditory prompts during hypnagogia. Experiments have shown that prompted themes can subsequently appear in people's dream reports. A later controlled experiment found that incubating a particular dream theme was also associated with increased post-sleep creativity related to that theme.

Dream influence is nevertheless unreliable. A 2024 systematic review examined 51 publications involving auditory, tactile, olfactory, visual, vestibular, or multimodal stimulation. The literature was highly heterogeneous. The evidence supports the proposition that external stimuli **can influence dream content**, not that researchers can precisely dictate an elaborate dream.

## A particularly important 2025 experiment

In 2025, researchers went beyond simply reactivating previously learned information.

Participants first learned identities associated with faces. During subsequent slow-wave sleep, the researchers presented a person's name together with previously unheard positive personality information.

After sleep, participants evaluated the targeted individuals more positively. The experiment also found changes in implicit evaluation, memory for positive versus negative characteristics, and facial representations—even though participants showed no explicit memory for the positive descriptions presented during sleep.

This does **not** demonstrate arbitrary belief implantation. But it is evidence that new semantic information presented during sleep can, under controlled conditions, become integrated with an existing representation and affect later evaluation.

That distinction is important.

## From “memory reactivation” to “memory editing”

The field itself is beginning to use stronger terminology. A 2026 *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* review describes **“memory editing during sleep.”**

The authors distinguish three broad approaches: reactivating an original memory while extinction occurs, strengthening competing memories, and introducing new interfering information while an existing memory is reactivated. They also identify wearable sensing, closed-loop neuromodulation, and neural decoding as technologies that could make such interventions more precisely timed.

The intended applications are largely therapeutic—for example, modifying distressing or aversive memories. But the underlying principle is more general:

> **Sleep is a biological state during which some memories and associations can be selectively reactivated and modified.**

## Influence through association

Suppose someone repeatedly encounters a person, product, sound, symbol, or idea while awake.

Associations gradually form:

> product → pleasure  
> sound → anticipation  
> person → trust  
> situation → anxiety

A later cue does not necessarily need to introduce an entirely new belief. It may instead **reactivate or alter an existing association**.

That is much closer to established TMR research. TMR normally works by associating a sensory cue with waking information and replaying that cue during subsequent sleep to bias memory consolidation.

The 2025 social-evaluation experiment extends this idea because it suggests that, under some circumstances, **new information can be introduced during sleep and incorporated into an already learned social representation.**

A simplified model is therefore:

> **waking experiences → associations form → sleep state → carefully timed cue or information → selective reactivation/integration → possible later change in memory or evaluation**

Every arrow in that sequence should be treated as probabilistic rather than deterministic.

## State detection matters

Timing is important because the brain behaves differently across waking, sleep onset, NREM sleep, and REM sleep.

Dormio provides a straightforward demonstration of this principle. It detects entry into hypnagogia and then presents auditory prompts. Researchers can therefore couple **state detection** with **information delivery**, rather than simply playing something continuously throughout the night.

Modern TMR research likewise increasingly attempts to time stimulation relative to ongoing brain oscillations rather than treating sleep as one uniform state.

That suggests an important general research question:

> **Does the same stimulus have different psychological effects depending on the physiological or cognitive state in which it is delivered?**

That question is experimentally testable.

## Pink noise should not be confused with a message

There is an important distinction between a stimulus that changes or interacts with a **brain state** and a stimulus carrying **meaningful information**.

Pink noise, clicks, tones, or other sounds can be used experimentally in attempts to interact with sleep physiology. But there is no established evidence that pink noise itself contains some special hypnotic or persuasive property.

A more defensible hypothesis would therefore separate:

> **state modulation + associative/semantic information**

rather than proposing:

> **pink noise → hypnosis → command → obedience**

The meaningful information might instead be a previously learned sound, word, name, image, association, or other cue.

Whether state modulation actually **amplifies** the psychological effect of such information is an empirical question, not an established fact.

## The loaded-dice model

This leads to a useful way of thinking about influence generally.

Imagine rolling a normal die thousands of times. Individual outcomes are unpredictable, but the results converge toward an expected distribution.

Now imagine a subtly loaded die.

You still cannot predict the next roll. But after enough rolls, a statistical bias becomes visible.

Human influence can work similarly.

A message does not have to determine someone's response. It might only make one response slightly more likely.

Possible responses might include:

> interested, reassured, afraid, trusting, suspicious, impulsive, attracted, disgusted

A single exposure tells us very little.

But if repeated exposures systematically move responses in the same direction, their aggregate effect could become measurable.

This is a **statistical influence model**, not a control model.

## Where personalization could fit

This next step is a hypothesis rather than something demonstrated by the sleep experiments.

Modern digital systems can estimate which content generates responses from particular users. In principle, behavioral profiling could therefore be combined with state-dependent stimulation:

> **profiling → waking associations → state detection → selected cue → memory reactivation or integration → later measurement → further personalization**

The individual components have precedents in separate research areas. The sleep literature establishes memory reactivation, dream incubation, state-dependent cueing, and—in the 2025 experiment—alteration of subsequent social evaluation.

What has **not** been demonstrated by these studies is the complete hypothetical system operating as a population-scale persuasion technology.

That boundary is important.

## What might realistically change?

Based on current evidence, plausible experimental outcomes concern relatively narrow psychological variables:

- memory accessibility or strength;
- forgetting or interference;
- familiarity;
- positive or negative evaluation;
- dream themes;
- emotional memory;
- mental representations.

The literature does **not** establish reliable arbitrary belief implantation, obedience, or precise behavioral control. TMR effects also depend heavily on experimental conditions, memory type, sleep stage, timing, and other factors.

The emerging question is therefore not:

> **“Can someone's mind be controlled?”**

A scientifically useful question is:

> **“Can selected stimuli, delivered under particular cognitive or physiological conditions, measurably change the probability of later memories, evaluations, emotions, or behaviors?”**

## How the stronger hypothesis could be tested

A rigorous experiment could measure participants before exposure and randomly assign them to different conditions:

> correctly timed cue  
> mistimed cue  
> cue without state modulation  
> state modulation without meaningful cue  
> sham stimulation

Researchers could then ask whether one condition produces a larger and more consistently directed change than the controls.

Crucially, the experiment should specify in advance what result would **falsify** the hypothesis.

If responses remain scattered and indistinguishable from controls, the proposed effect weakens.

If correctly timed associative stimulation repeatedly produces greater movement in a predicted direction, that would support state-dependent influence.

This framework also forces researchers to distinguish genuine state-dependent effects from ordinary persuasion, expectation, conditioning, sleep disruption, and statistical noise.

## The bottom line

Current research supports something considerably narrower than “mind control,” but also more interesting than the assumption that sleeping people are completely inaccessible to external information.

Researchers have experimentally demonstrated **targeted memory reactivation, modification of some memories, sensory influence on dream content, state-triggered dream incubation, and—in at least one recent controlled experiment—changes in subsequent social evaluation following information presented during sleep.**

Researchers are now explicitly discussing **sleep-based memory editing** and technologies for increasingly precise, closed-loop intervention.

But the evidence does not show arbitrary control over beliefs or behavior, and laboratory findings should not be extrapolated into claims that complete covert influence systems currently exist.

The more defensible question is about **probability rather than control**:

**How much can memories, associations, evaluations, or behavior be biased; under what conditions; for whom; and for how long?**

That is the “loaded dice” version of the problem—and unlike the much stronger idea of mind control, it can be experimentally measured and falsified.
