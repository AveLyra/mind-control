**Recheck:** I found a flaw in the earlier interaction simulation. Its generator makes cue awareness more likely in the “paired + state cue” condition, and awareness itself raises the simulated outcome. That creates an apparent interaction of about **0.018 even when the configured interaction is zero**. In the September 23 run, the analysis flagged the association-only and additive scenarios; its null sensitivity grid flagged **8.75% to 20%** of runs, depending on sample size. I would not use that harness’s current detection rates to plan a human study. The Sleep-EDF run still supports only the feasibility of scheduling cues over recorded sleep stages.

Drugs and hormones could change the **starting state or measured response** in such studies. Their effects depend on the substance, the person, and whether measurement occurs during learning, later cue exposure, or sleep:

| Factor | What the evidence suggests |
| --- | --- |
| **Cortisol and acute stress** | Can alter conditioned responses and extinction, with effects that vary by timing. In one randomized study of 32 men, hydrocortisone before extinction increased conditioned skin-conductance responses. That does not predict a person’s response to an ordinary sound or image. [Human trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23945999/) |
| **Beta blockers, such as propranolol** | Can reduce some bodily expressions of learned fear in controlled experiments, but results are inconsistent. A preregistered replication did not reproduce a proposed memory effect. Lower heart rate or startle also does not prove a memory was erased. [Experimental study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22406658/) · [Replication](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-06119-5) |
| **Sedatives, including benzodiazepines** | Can reduce anxiety and impair formation of new explicit memories. Poor recall afterward therefore cannot establish that a cue was processed subconsciously, or that conditioning became stronger. [Controlled memory study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6122188/) |
| **Antidepressants** | Effects differ across drugs and duration of use. A small randomized escitalopram study found faster extinction of a laboratory fear response; a fluoxetine sleep study found reduced REM sleep. Both could affect what a study measures. [Escitalopram trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23776033/) · [Fluoxetine trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7814830/) |
| **Caffeine, melatonin, and sex hormones** | Caffeine improved vigilance during sleep deprivation in a controlled study. Melatonin and light jointly shifted circadian timing. In a randomized fear-extinction study, estradiol changed skin-conductance results across testing days, while progesterone showed no significant effect on that measure. None provides a simple “more susceptible” setting. [Caffeine study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8935086/) · [Melatonin/light trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24179293/) · [Hormone trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39448569/) |

For the proposed suite, medication use, alcohol and caffeine intake, sleep, and relevant hormone measures belong in the study record as possible confounders. **There is no evidence here that a drug or hormone makes hidden optical messages, weak magnetic signals, or a later frustration cue reliably control complex thoughts or emotions.** These findings also cannot identify the cause of an individual experience. Medication should not be started, stopped, or changed to test the idea.
