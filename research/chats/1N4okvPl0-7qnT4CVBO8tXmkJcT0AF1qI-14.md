**The EEG findings have primary human-study citations.** Two parts of the wording need tightening: the strongest NREM result concerns **slow oscillations below 1 Hz**, rather than the entire 0.5–4 Hz delta band; and changing an EEG rhythm does not by itself show a change in behavior.

| Target | Primary citation | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| **NREM slow oscillations and spindles** | [Ngo et al., *Neuron* (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2013.03.006) | Sounds timed to the up-state of ongoing **<1 Hz** slow oscillations increased that rhythm and coupled spindle activity. Out-of-phase sounds did not have the same effect. |
| **Waking alpha and the transition to sleep** | [Hebron et al., *PLOS Biology* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002651) | Alpha-phase-locked sounds changed alpha power, frequency, and EEG connectivity in a phase- and location-dependent way. One stimulation phase **delayed progression to deeper NREM sleep**; it did not significantly change latency to the *first* sleep epoch. |
| **Alpha during REM** | [Jaramillo et al., *Sleep* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsae193) | In 18 adults, phase-locked sounds produced phase-dependent changes in REM alpha power and frequency. Participants remained in REM without signs of arousal during **most**, though not all, stimulation. |
| **Theta during REM** | [Jaramillo et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsae193); [Harrington et al., *Sleep* (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaa227) | The 2024 study found phase-dependent theta changes. The earlier 16-person study found a brief theta-power increase followed by several seconds of suppression. |
| **Detected sleep spindles** | [Jourde et al., *Sleep Advances* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf007) | In 10 adults, sounds triggered during detected spindles increased subsequent sigma-band activity; sounds delivered early in a spindle could also **end it sooner**. |
| **Sleep-onset insomnia** | [Bressler et al., *Scientific Reports* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-63385-1) | In the 21 participants analyzed, weekly average sleep-onset latency was **10.5 ± 15.9 minutes shorter**, or **29.3 ± 44.4%**, during the stimulation week than during a *silent-sham* week. The silent comparison does not establish how much of the benefit required precise phase timing rather than sound stimulation generally. |

A defensible summary is: **EEG-timed sounds can alter particular measured brain rhythms and, in some experiments, aspects of sleep.** The effects depend on the rhythm, timing, and setting. They do not establish that sounds carry hidden semantic instructions or reliably increase suggestibility. As a useful counterexample, another controlled study increased slow oscillation and spindle activity **without improving its measured memory tasks**. [Human replication study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831893/)
