Using the evidence-falsification lens here: yes, that die analogy is exactly the right statistical move.

Instead of asking, “Did this one ad/post/video make someone do X?”, we ask:

> Across many exposures, do responses drift in a particular direction more than chance would predict?

For the die, the hidden bias is shape or mass distribution. For information curation, the hidden “mass distribution” might be:

- the platform’s ranking objective: engagement, watch time, conversion;
- the user model: inferred personality, anxieties, identity, habits;
- the content supply: what ads/posts are available to serve;
- the timing layer: when the person is tired, bored, angry, lonely, primed;
- the feedback loop: the system learns from every reaction.

So the research question becomes: does repeated curated exposure create a **resultant vector** in attitude/affect/behavior space?

**Lay Version**

Imagine everyone has a cloud of possible reactions: amused, afraid, angry, trusting, suspicious, impulsive, partisan, brand-loyal, risk-seeking, etc.

A fair information environment would still move people around, but the movements should mostly cancel out across many people and many exposures.

A loaded environment would make tiny pushes in the same direction again and again. No single push proves much. But if thousands of little arrows all lean toward fear of immigrants, distrust of institutions, craving a product, hostility toward a candidate, or urgency to buy, the arrows stop canceling.

That is the “loaded dice” detector.

**Research Version**

Define a multidimensional latent outcome space:

- political: left-right, authoritarian-libertarian, institutional trust, threat sensitivity, populism, outgroup affect;
- commercial: brand affinity, price sensitivity, impulsivity, status-seeking, risk tolerance, purchase intent;
- emotional: fear, anger, disgust, sadness, hope, belonging, arousal;
- attentional: dwell time, click-through, return frequency, search behavior.

Each exposure gets represented as a vector in this space. Each user’s before-after movement is another vector. The core statistic is the **mean resultant vector**:

`R = sum(delta vectors)`

Then ask:

- Is `|R|` larger than expected under randomized/sham exposure?
- Does `R` point toward a predicted region?
- Is the direction stronger for modeled-vulnerable users?
- Does it persist after delay?
- Does it translate from affect to behavior?

That cleanly separates “noise” from “biased drift.”

**What We Can Test**

1. **Direct influence**
   Can a specific message reliably move a specific person to a specific belief or action?
   Likely weak except for low-stakes, already-ambivalent, or highly primed choices.

2. **Dice loading**
   Can a system increase the probability of certain emotional or behavioral responses across a population?
   Much more plausible. This is where microtargeting, recommender systems, and ad optimization probably matter most.

3. **Susceptibility matching**
   Do different people move more when the message matches personality, identity, fear, desire, or prior belief?
   This is directly connected to psychological targeting research.

4. **State-dependent amplification**
   Does the same message work better when delivered during fatigue, boredom, social comparison, anger, or sleep-adjacent states?
   This bridges back to our pink-noise/TDI/state-cue framework.

**The Strong Claim To Avoid**

Not:

> The system controls people’s minds.

Better:

> The system may alter the probability distribution of emotional and behavioral responses by repeatedly selecting stimuli that exploit learned user-specific sensitivities.

That is less dramatic, but much more testable.

**Best Experiment Shape**

A proper Faraday test would be:

- baseline participants on political/commercial/emotional measures;
- randomized exposure to neutral, generic persuasive, and personalized persuasive feeds;
- measure movement as vectors, not just single survey answers;
- compare resultant vector length and direction across conditions;
- include sham targeting and wrong-person targeting controls;
- test persistence after 24 hours or a week;
- predefine what would falsify the effect.

If the personalized condition creates a larger, directionally coherent vector than generic or sham conditions, that is evidence of “loaded dice.” If it only creates scattered movement, the scary version weakens.

The beautiful thing about your die analogy is that it gives us a way to investigate population-level influence without needing to prove impossible precision at the individual level. It turns “mind control?” into a measurable question: **how much can the response distribution be biased, in what direction, for whom, and under what conditions?**
