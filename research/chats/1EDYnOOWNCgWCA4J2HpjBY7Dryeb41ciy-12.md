Yes. I misunderstood you: the PPA opportunity could be used to **present the unanswered sleep-influence questions and seek scientific collaborators**, while the next research stage tests one of those questions directly. The article would be a research agenda, not a report of a demonstrated “mind control” technique.

The strongest question is: **Does replaying a benign cue during verified sleep change a later response beyond what prior learning, ordinary exposure, or sleep disruption would explain?** Sleep cueing has produced measurable memory effects in controlled studies, but the boundary is unsettled. A 2025 study reported immediate changes in social evaluations after sleep exposure; its authors identified missing wake and delayed follow-up controls. An earlier attempt to replicate a sleep-cued social-bias effect found no benefit. ([2025 study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9), [replication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6347202/))

**The next stage I recommend:**

1. **Validate measurement first.** In a reviewed study with consenting adults, verify sleep stage, cue delivery, awakenings, and next-day fatigue. The existing [Sleep-EDF dry run] (local source path omitted) tested scheduling on public records; its outcomes were synthetic. It also found that 39 of 153 recordings had no eligible N3 cue window under its chosen rule, a practical constraint the study design must account for.
2. **Test one mechanism.** Preregister a comparison of previously paired versus unpaired benign cues, delivered during verified sleep versus matched control conditions. Choose **next-day memory** as the primary outcome, with awareness and sleep disruption measured alongside it. Mask outcome scoring and include a delayed follow-up. The existing [interaction dry run] (local source path omitted) shows the analysis can detect an effect inserted into synthetic data; it does not show that the effect occurs in people.
3. **Expand only after replication.** If a sleep-specific effect survives those controls, a separate study could ask whether it transfers to evaluations or choices and how long it lasts. An immediate rating change would still leave durable belief or behavior change unanswered.

The strongest objection is that an apparent “sleep influence” effect could come from **prior learning, brief waking, sound-induced arousal, expectation, or selective recall**. The proposed controls are what would make that explanation testable. Participant work needs an independent ethics determination and consent process before collection. ([HHS research guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/education-and-outreach/online-education/human-research-protection-training/lesson-4-irb-review-of-research/index.html))

For *Pennsylvania Psychologist*, I’d pitch **“What Can Sleep Cues Actually Change?”**: what controlled studies have observed, where findings conflict, and the specific experiment needed next. Its [checklist](https://assets.noviams.com/novi-file-uploads/ppa/PA_Psychologists/Author_Checklist__7_25.pdf) allows about 1,000 words—enough for that question and a call for collaboration, though not a full protocol.
