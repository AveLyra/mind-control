The paragraph is accurate. **The strongest conclusion is that sleep provides a limited, state-dependent opportunity to influence memory processing—not that every kind of memory can be predictably strengthened, weakened, or rewritten.** Evidence for selective memory strengthening is substantially more developed than evidence for targeted forgetting or changes in emotional meaning. [1, 2]  [source 0](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00244-8)  [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

The review is **Julia Carbone and Susanne Diekelmann’s “An update on recent advances in targeted memory reactivation during sleep,” published April 15, 2024, in *npj Science of Learning*.** It is a narrative overview, rather than a systematic review or meta-analysis, and includes some findings that were still preprints when it was written. Its sections therefore should not be read as equally established capabilities. [1]  [source 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00244-8)

The assessment below draws on that review, the quantitative meta-analysis, and selected primary studies, including newer positive and negative findings available through September 13, 2026.

## What TMR actually demonstrates

In a typical experiment, participants learn something while awake: object locations, vocabulary pairs, or a movement sequence. Particular sounds or odors become associated with that learning. Researchers then present some of those cues during sleep and compare subsequent performance for cued and uncued material, accounting for pre-sleep performance. The decisive observation is a **selective difference between memories**, not merely better performance after sleeping. [2]  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

An influential 2007 experiment illustrates the importance of the prior association. Participants learned while an odor was present; presenting that odor again during slow-wave sleep improved spatial-memory retention. The odor did not produce the same benefit when it had been absent during learning, when presented during REM sleep or wakefulness, or for the motor task tested in that experiment. These controls argue against a simple “pleasant smells improve memory” explanation. [3]  [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1138581)

Classical TMR is therefore best understood as **cueing previously acquired information while its storage is being reorganized**. That differs from presenting entirely new information during sleep, although newer experiments sometimes combine reactivation with new material. [2, 16]  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)  [source 6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

## How strong is the overall evidence?

The principal quantitative anchor is Hu and colleagues’ 2020 meta-analysis: **91 experiments, 2,004 participants, and 212 effect sizes**, with an overall effect of **Hedges’ g = 0.29**, 95% confidence interval **0.21–0.38**. That is a small average standardized benefit—not a 29% improvement in memory. [2]  [source 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

Several qualifications matter. The studies were heterogeneous, their average sample size was only 22, and publication-bias adjustments reduced the estimated effect to approximately **g = 0.13–0.18**, depending on the method. The clearest pooled support concerned NREM sleep. Separate analyses did not establish overall benefits for emotional-memory retention or fear extinction. The search underlying this meta-analysis ended in June 2019, so those null findings do not settle the newer literature. [2]  [source 8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)  [source 9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

My assessment is that **the phenomenon is established, but its magnitude and reliability depend heavily on the task and procedure**. That judgment is compatible with both repeated positive findings and credible failures to reproduce particular effects.

## Remembering facts and skills

**Declarative memory—facts, associations, and locations—provides the clearest practical examples.**

In a 2025 home study, 24 adults learned associations between pseudowords and Italian words, then slept with an EEG-based system that replayed half the pseudowords. The authors reported an increase in accuracy for cued items and no significant improvement for uncued items; importantly, the statistical comparison of changes between conditions was significant. This demonstrates that selective TMR benefits are not confined to a staffed sleep laboratory. [4]  [source 10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)

However, the engineering claim needs precision. The home system delivered words after detecting slow waves, but the authors explicitly acknowledged that it **did not reliably lock each stimulus to a particular oscillatory phase**. It supports automated, sleep-informed cueing—not the stronger claim that consumer-scale systems already achieve consistently precise control of memory-relevant brain states. [4]  [source 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)  [source 12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12592824/)

Replication is also uneven. Wick and Rasch’s 2023 vocabulary study, with 34 participants across two samples, found neither an overall benefit for cued words nor a difference between cueing during N2 and slow-wave sleep. The authors explicitly called for larger, preferably multilaboratory replications. [5]  [source 13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10547374/)

**Procedural memory—learned skills—also responds to TMR, but the comparison condition can change the interpretation.**

A preregistered 2025 motor-learning experiment compared cues delivered during the up-state of slow oscillations, cues delivered during the down-state, and no cueing. In the main behavioral analysis of 28 participants, up-state cueing outperformed down-state cueing. But **up-state cueing did not outperform no cueing; down-state cueing performed worse than both**. [6]  [source 14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-57602-2)

That is evidence that timing can alter the outcome. It is not straightforward evidence that correctly timed stimulation necessarily adds a benefit beyond ordinary sleep. The distinction is easily lost when a paper’s abstract emphasizes “enhancement.”

## What happens in the brain?

A leading account is that cues interact with coordinated sleep activity: slow oscillations create alternating periods of excitability, sleep spindles provide brief bursts of activity, and medial-temporal or hippocampal ripples accompany memory reactivation. A 2024 experiment combined scalp recordings in healthy participants with intracranial recordings in epilepsy patients and detected reinstatement of previously learned information during sleep. Reactivation was associated with coordinated slow-oscillation/spindle activity and spindle-locked ripples. [7]  [source 15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11187142/)

This is more informative than simply observing that a sound produces an EEG response. It connects sleep stimulation to patterns associated with previously learned material. Nevertheless, associations among oscillations, decoding, and behavior do not by themselves prove every step of the proposed causal mechanism. [7]  [source 16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-49572-8)

A particularly important detail: **the healthy-participant arm of that same study showed worse associative recall for cued than uncued material.** Thus, it supports memory reactivation without supporting the equation “reactivation equals improvement.” [7]  [source 17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11187142/)

That distinction also limits interpretations of wearable-device studies. Detecting a physiological response is not sufficient evidence that a desired psychological effect occurred.

## Emotional memories and “forgetting”

These outcomes must be separated. Remembering fewer details, feeling less distressed by a remembered event, and permanently eliminating the underlying memory are different claims. Studies frequently measure only one or two of them. [8, 11]  [source 18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295023/)  [source 19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655627/)

**Competition between memories can be biased during sleep.** In Xia and colleagues’ 2024 PNAS experiment, participants first learned pseudoword–aversive-image associations. The following evening, some of the same pseudowords were associated with positive images, creating competing memories. During subsequent NREM sleep, researchers replayed selected pseudowords. [8]  [source 20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295023/)

For associations containing that positive interference, cueing reduced later recall of the aversive images and increased reports of positive memories intruding during recall. But the result was not a uniform improvement across every outcome: subjective valence and arousal ratings did not show significant cueing effects, and overall positive-memory recall was not significantly improved in the main analysis. [8]  [source 21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295023/)

The defensible interpretation is that **sleep cueing can alter competition and accessibility among deliberately established memories**. Calling this “erasure” would exceed what the tests establish.

**Attempts to induce forgetting have important limits.** Whitmore and colleagues tested whether pairing memory reactivation with sleep disruption would weaken memories learned a week earlier. Despite disturbing sleep, the intervention did not selectively weaken those memories. This contrasts with earlier effects on memories formed shortly before sleep and suggests that memory age or stability matters. [9]  [source 22](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-024-00276-0)

A 2026 study tested another approach: odor cues previously associated with “remember” or “forget” instructions were presented alongside sounds linked to spatial memories. The predicted selective forgetting did not occur. Instead, the instruction-associated odors interfered with the sound-related memory benefit. This was a different procedure, not a direct replication of every earlier forgetting experiment, but it demonstrates that combining meaningful cues does not make their effects reliably additive or controllable. [10]  [source 23](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13047899/)

**Reducing distress is a separate, promising direction.** In a preregistered 2024 study of 80 healthy participants, researchers first used waking imagery rescripting to modify an aversive autobiographical memory. Replaying individualized words from the updated memory during subsequent sleep produced additional reductions in some measures, including vividness, distress, and arousal. [11]  [source 24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655627/)

That is more relevant to real-life memories than an unpleasant-picture task. But it remains an adjunct to a waking intervention in healthy participants—not proof that TMR alone treats PTSD or removes traumatic memories. [11]  [source 25](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-024-03192-4)

## Influencing dreams

The broad claim that sensory stimulation can influence reported dream content has substantial support, but the degree of influence varies markedly. A 2024 systematic review included 51 publications and found reported frequencies of stimulus-related dream changes ranging from approximately zero to 80%. Because definitions and methods differed considerably, that range is **not a usable general success rate**. [14]  [source 26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11009489/)

A more targeted 2025 experiment studied 20 participants, preferentially recruiting people who frequently remembered dreams. Before sleep, participants completed two distinctive activities—a harmonica task and a bubble-blowing task—each linked to a different sound. One task’s sound was subsequently presented during REM sleep, and blinded raters coded the dream reports. [15]  [source 27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)  [source 28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)

Dreams contained more elements of the cued task: an average of **0.64 versus 0.38 coded elements per dream**. Twelve participants showed more incorporation of the cued task, six showed equal incorporation, and two showed more incorporation of the uncued task. However, the effect was not specifically stronger immediately after REM periods containing sounds than after silent REM periods. [15]  [source 29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)

This supports a **probabilistic bias in reported dream content**, not reliable control over an unfolding dream narrative. The small selected sample and reliance on remembered, interpreted reports remain relevant limitations. [15]  [source 30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)  [source 31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)

There is also clinical proof-of-concept. A 2022 study enrolled 36 people with nightmare disorder. Everyone practiced imagery rehearsal therapy and heard a sound during REM sleep at home; only one group had first associated that sound with the positive rewritten nightmare. That group subsequently reported fewer nightmares and more positive dream emotions, with a sustained reduction in nightmares at three months. The comparison makes the prior association—not merely nighttime sound exposure—particularly relevant. [12]  [source 32](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)

But therapeutic effects do not generalize automatically. A study published September 9, 2026, tested odor-based reactivation of a relaxation exercise in 25 people with frequent nightmares. It found changes in spindle activity but **no improvement in nightmare symptoms or the intended measures of cortical hyperarousal**. This was a different intervention, so it does not overturn the 2022 result; it limits the broader claim that sleep reactivation reliably improves nightmares. [13]  [source 33](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.70443)

## Does this extend to new information or attitudes?

There is emerging evidence, but it should not be folded uncritically into the established TMR findings.

A 2025 study paired previously learned names with **new positive personality traits during slow-wave sleep**. Researchers reported changes in remembered facial representations, trait recall, and explicit and implicit evaluations. A name-only control helped address whether simple repetition of a name explained the results. [16]  [source 34](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

Crucially, the authors acknowledged that their design **could not cleanly distinguish integration of new information from altered reactivation and consolidation of existing associations**. It also lacked a wakefulness comparison, a long-term follow-up, and a name-plus-neutral-trait control. The absence of recalled sleep-presented traits is not, by itself, conclusive proof that every relevant processing event occurred without awareness. [16]  [source 35](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

The study therefore supports further investigation of evaluative updating during sleep. It does not establish unrestricted sleep learning, durable persuasion, or a general ability to bypass someone’s resistance to arbitrary beliefs.

## Overall conclusion

The original paragraph is appropriately cautious. The literature supports a stronger account of the **existence of selective influence**, but not a comparably strong account of **predictable control**.

My evidence-weighted assessment is:

- **Well supported:** cues associated with prior learning can alter subsequent memory performance, particularly in NREM paradigms.

- **Supported under particular conditions:** cueing can change memory competition, emotional responses, and the likelihood of related material appearing in dream reports.

- **Not established as general capabilities:** reliable autobiographical-memory erasure, dependable improvement across people and tasks, or durable implantation of arbitrary beliefs. [2, 8–16]  [source 36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)  [source 37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295023/)  [source 38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12396821/)  [source 39](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9)

**The central scientific distinction is between accessing a memory-processing system and controlling its outcome. TMR establishes the former much more securely than the latter.**
