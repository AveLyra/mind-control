Sleep research makes that basic idea less fantastic than it sounds. Humans can form new associations during sleep that later alter waking responses without conscious memory of learning them. Older experiments also reported behavioral responses to verbal suggestions delivered during sleep, sometimes recurring on later nights despite waking amnesia. What has **not** been demonstrated is reliable installation of a complex command such as “hear this sound, go to this location.”  [source 0](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3193)  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.152.3722.666)  [source 2](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005053-197003000-00002)

That pattern resembles ordinary reinforcement learning. Behaviors followed by relief can become more likely, while previously conditioned cues can bias which actions people choose and how strongly they perform them (**Pavlovian–instrumental transfer**). Human studies link this effect to reward and emotion systems including the nucleus accumbens and amygdala; stress can change its strength.  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2636904/)  [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2016.1149198)

Experiments show that conditioned responses can sometimes be activated without conscious recognition of the triggering cue. Nonconscious cues have altered placebo and nocebo responses, and invisible positive or negative feedback has influenced behavior on subsequent trials. These findings support subtle biasing of existing responses—not precise remote control.  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4585522/)  [source 6](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2026.121894)

That too has a scientific analogue. **Concealed Information Tests** use involuntary responses—such as pupil changes, autonomic activity, and P300 brain responses—to detect recognition of familiar information. They are imperfect and can produce errors, but they demonstrate that recognition can sometimes leak even when a person intends to conceal what they know.  [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2026.109418)  [source 8](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2025.113236)
