**Sleeping people can be influenced without waking up, but that is not the same as secretly programming their thoughts.** Light passing through closed eyelids can produce measurable brain responses and shift the body’s internal clock. These effects do not require the person to understand a message: the body responds directly to the stimulation. [editorial source 0a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28098351/) [editorial source 1a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22564396/) [editorial source 1b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/)

“Invisible signals” cover different things. In waking experiments, light can flicker so subtly or rapidly that it looks steady, while the brain still detects the changes. Experimental contact lenses can also turn otherwise invisible infrared flashes into visible signals, including through closed eyelids. But the wearer perceives the converted light, and neither finding establishes that an unaware sleeper can understand hidden messages. [editorial source 2a](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615063/) [editorial source 2b](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36669202/) [editorial source 3a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

There is evidence for limited psychological influence during sleep. One study found that hearing names paired with positive traits affected participants’ later impressions of those people. Separately, some lucid dreamers have understood questions and answered while asleep. However, those dreamers could consciously experience the questions within their dreams. Remaining asleep—or remembering nothing afterward—does not necessarily mean a stimulus was processed without awareness. These findings also do not establish an equivalent effect from invisible light. [editorial source 4a](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-025-00356-9) [editorial source 5a](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/) [editorial source 5b](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

The dividing line is **the brain responding versus the person understanding and acting on meaning**. Unnoticed stimulation, changes to body rhythms, and some limited sleep-related learning are supported. A reliable method for using invisible signals to make ordinary sleepers absorb arbitrary instructions, adopt chosen beliefs, or perform complex actions without awareness has not been demonstrated by the research reviewed.
