Possibly. There are several mouse studies close to that description, but one is an especially good match.

In a 2013 Stanford/Technion study, researchers first **conditioned mice to associate a particular odor with a mild foot shock**. The next day, once EEG/EMG confirmed that the mice were in **NREM sleep**, they introduced brief pulses of that odor into their cages—at a concentration specifically chosen not to wake them.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24081009/)

The remarkable result was that the sleeping brain wasn't simply ignoring it. Re-presenting the fear-associated odor during sleep **reactivated and strengthened the fear memory**, measured by increased freezing when the mice encountered the odor later while awake. With an additional drug intervention in the amygdala, researchers could instead weaken the reactivated fear memory.  [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5036945/)

There's also a 2017 experiment that used **sounds associated with foot shock**. Researchers played the conditioned sound during either REM or NREM sleep without simply waking the animals. Presenting it during **NREM subsequently weakened the fear memory; REM presentation did not produce that effect**.  [source 2](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep46247)

And another particularly interesting experiment found that an **aversively conditioned sound could push sleeping mice into a lighter sleep state without actually waking them**, whereas an acoustically similar but meaningless sound produced little change.  [source 3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-09457-6)
