The feedback points to a useful distinction: **this pilot can answer a scientific question about whether the method is workable, but it should not claim to establish that sleep cues change dreams.** Its value to backers should be the evidence it produces for deciding whether and how to run a larger, adequately powered study. Pilot-study guidance recommends defining feasibility measures and progression criteria in advance. [Eldridge et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0150205)

TITLE
Can a Sleep-Cueing Study Be Run Reliably? A Pilot for Dream-Report Research

ABSTRACT
This proposed pilot asks whether a sound-cueing protocol can be delivered during monitored sleep with acceptable disruption and produce enough usable, consistently scored dream reports to support a larger study. Before recruitment, we will secure a participating sleep laboratory and the required ethics approval. The pilot will assess protocol delivery, sleep disruption, report availability, cue awareness, and agreement between blinded report scorers. These results will evaluate feasibility, not establish whether cues change dreams. We will use the findings to refine the protocol, estimate key design parameters, and make a documented decision about whether to proceed to a preregistered, adequately powered study of cue-related dream content. If the protocol is not workable, we will report what needs to change or why a larger study should not proceed.

CONTEXT
Published sleep-cueing research has paired sounds with waking tasks and replayed those sounds during sleep to study whether task-related details appear in later dream reports. [Konkoly et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2025.109229). But a larger test depends on practical details: whether cues can be delivered at the intended time, whether they disrupt sleep, whether participants can give usable reports, and whether blinded scorers can apply the scoring rules consistently. This pilot will focus on those methodological questions.

SIGNIFICANCE
A larger study could be costly and inconclusive if the cueing procedure disrupts sleep, reports are too sparse, or scoring is inconsistent. This pilot’s value is to measure those risks and refine the method before a definitive test. If predefined feasibility criteria are met, the results will inform a larger study designed to test whether cueing changes dream reports. If they are not met, the team can revise the procedure or decide not to proceed.

GOALS
We will assess cue-delivery fidelity, sleep disruption, the proportion of awakenings that yield usable dream reports, cue-awareness responses, and agreement between blinded scorers. Before data collection, the team will define how each measure will inform a proceed, revise, or stop decision. We will report these findings, document protocol changes, and use the results to plan—or decline—a larger study. Any cue-related pattern in this small pilot will be treated as exploratory, not evidence of efficacy.
:::
