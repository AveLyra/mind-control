The most audacious **testable** question is: **Can someone acquire a completely new rule during verified sleep, then use it to make choices about examples they have never seen—still doing so a week later?**

That goes beyond strengthening something learned while awake. Studies have reported limited implicit learning of new word associations during sleep and immediate shifts in social evaluation, but they do not establish durable, general-purpose control of beliefs or behavior. The social-evaluation study also lacked a waking comparison and delayed follow-up. ([Sleep vocabulary study](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(18)31672-5), [social-evaluation study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00356-9))

A decisive study would use **consenting adults and harmless fictional material**. Participants would encounter an arbitrary rule only during laboratory-verified sleep. After waking, they would make choices about *new* examples, then repeat the test a week later. Preregistered comparisons would include sham exposure and matched waking exposure; sleep recordings and awareness checks would test whether brief awakenings explain any result. Independent replication would be essential.

The strongest objection is that a positive result might reflect unnoticed waking, familiarity with the sounds, or a bias toward rehearsed examples. Success would require **transfer to genuinely new examples after those explanations are ruled out**. Even then, it would demonstrate a bounded form of sleep learning, not “mind control.”

For our project, that is a **long-range target**. The current [sleep dry run] (local source path omitted) established scheduling feasibility with synthetic outcomes; it has not measured a human cognitive effect. The next practical step is the consented measurement pilot we discussed, followed by a narrower learning test.
