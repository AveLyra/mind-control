The earlier hypnosis-only framing was too narrow. **Some learning during sleep can influence later waking behavior without conscious recollection of the learning.** The important unresolved question is whether this can extend to **learning a novel instruction while asleep and reliably executing it during ordinary life when a hidden cue appears**. The studies below establish parts of that sequence, not the complete system.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22922782/)

## What has actually been demonstrated

**Sleep-learned cues can trigger responses after awakening.** In a 2012 experiment, researchers paired tones with pleasant or unpleasant odors during sleep. Later, the tones alone produced different sniffing responses—including while participants were awake—without their reporting awareness of the learning. This directly demonstrates *sleep-acquired association → later cue → altered waking response*. However, the response was a relatively simple physiological adjustment, and the waking tones were not established as subliminal.  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22922782/)

**Sleep conditioning can affect behavior beyond an immediate reflex.** A 2014 study paired cigarette odor with unpleasant odors during sleep. Among the 12 participants receiving conditioning during N2 sleep, self-reported smoking declined by approximately 34% over the following week. The study included comparison conditions, but participants already wanted to quit, outcomes came from smoking diaries, and the subgroups were small. It supports modification of an existing behavior—not installation of an arbitrary new goal.  [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6608450/)

**There is evidence for learning associations involving meaning, not just sensations.** A 2022 study of 22 participants paired unfamiliar Japanese words with meaning-related sounds during sleep; afterward, participants could select corresponding pictures above chance without apparent awareness of that knowledge. A separate 2024 study found that sleep-presented pseudoword–meaning pairs influenced category judgments 36 hours later. In its successful stimulation condition, accuracy was about 41% against 33% chance. These were limited semantic associations, not detailed instructions, and the subsequent test cues were consciously presented.  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8959773/)  [source 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11045222/)

**Masked waking stimuli can influence the execution of an already established task.** In a monetary-reward experiment, participants squeezed a handgrip harder for higher rewards even when they could not report which reward had been displayed. This concerns action execution and motivational intensity—not merely noticing or remembering something. But participants consciously understood and accepted the task beforehand.  [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17431137/)

## Where N2 and spindles fit

Your reference to spindles is relevant, but **spindles are not themselves carriers of semantic instructions**. They are thalamocortical activity patterns involved in memory processing. Experiments have linked spindle timing to the effectiveness of *targeted memory reactivation*: replaying cues during sleep to strengthen material learned earlier while awake. That is different from teaching an entirely new instruction during sleep.  [source 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5992601/)

New learning is also possible in some paradigms. A 2017 experiment found that repeated acoustic patterns presented during light NREM or REM sleep improved subsequent recognition, with spindle-related activity associated with facilitation. The same exposure during deep NREM impaired subsequent performance. Together with the semantic-learning studies, this indicates that **sleep stage, momentary brain state, and the kind of information all matter**—not that a particular sleep stage provides unrestricted access to learning.  [source 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5548898/)  [source 8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11045222/)

The proposed neural sequence is therefore ordinary sensory processing and memory formation operating under unusual awareness conditions: a stimulus is processed, an association is retained, and a later cue reactivates information that affects responding. In the vocabulary research, later retrieval involved hippocampal and cortical language activity. That supports associative memory mechanisms; it does not establish a separate unconscious instruction-following agent.  [source 9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30713104/)

## The crucial gaps

**Unawareness of learning, unawareness of a later cue, and unawareness of why one acted are three different things.** Showing one does not establish the others. In particular, demonstrating sleep learning with clearly presented waking test cues does not establish retrieval by a masked cue during an unrelated everyday activity.

The evidence also becomes less consistent for flexible, reward-guided learning without awareness. A 2008 study reported subliminal instrumental conditioning. A **2023 registered replication** reproduced the apparent effect using the original approach, but found evidence against unconscious learning when it introduced stronger, trial-by-trial awareness checks. That limits confidence in claims that increasingly complex behavior can be learned entirely outside awareness; it does not negate every simpler conditioning effect.  [source 10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2572733/)  [source 11](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2022.12.003)

Likewise, an EEG response to imperceptible flicker is not evidence that a person has decoded a message or acquired an instruction. A signal could serve as an arbitrary learned cue without containing language—but the cue–response association and its subsequent behavioral effect would still need to be demonstrated.

**The supported concern is unrecognized influence on associations, preferences, physiological responses, and aspects of task performance.** Those effects can matter ethically without amounting to reliable control. I did not find a controlled demonstration of the full combination you describe: novel semantic instructions acquired during verified sleep, subsequently activated by genuinely unperceived cues, producing reliable, complex goal-directed behavior in ordinary life. The missing evidence is for that combination—not for the existence of unconscious influence itself.
