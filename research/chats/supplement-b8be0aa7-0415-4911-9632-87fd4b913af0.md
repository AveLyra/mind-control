### Light can affect the brain even when its modulation is barely noticeable

Optical wavelength and flicker frequency are different variables. Visible light is roughly 380–700 nm; flicker frequency describes how rapidly its intensity or spectral composition changes. A visible light can therefore appear steady even while its modulation continues to produce a measurable neural response. [source 0](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/)

A 2024 study demonstrated this with **40 Hz “invisible spectral flicker.”** Rapid spectral changes were rated as substantially less perceptible than conventional flicker while still producing a measurable 40 Hz steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP). [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

A separate 2024 brain–computer-interface experiment used **58–62 Hz flicker-imperceptible targets**. OPM-MEG detected frequency-specific brain responses, allowing a nine-command BCI to determine which target participants were attending to; five participants were tested, with an average offline accuracy of about 93%. [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38812288/)

The mechanism is:

**hidden light modulation → visual-system response → neural recording → software classification**

This is genuine BCI functionality, but it does not show that flicker inserts information directly into thought. The computer identifies the neural response associated with a known stimulus. The narrower finding is important: **conscious perception of flicker is not required for stimulus-specific neural activity.** [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

### Closed eyelids do not prevent light from affecting the sleeping brain

In 2017, researchers demonstrated SSVEPs produced by flickering light delivered through **closed eyelids during both light and deep NREM sleep**. EEG activity at the stimulation frequency was significantly greater when the light was on. [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28098351/)

Sleep changes how those signals are processed. Fast **8–10 Hz** visual responses were substantially stronger during wakefulness, whereas slower **3–5 Hz** responses were stronger during NREM and REM sleep. A waking flicker-based BCI therefore cannot simply be assumed to work the same way during sleep. [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28334175/)

A 2026 simultaneous EEG-fMRI study found that light responses remained intact in the **visual thalamus during N1 and N2 sleep**, while early visual-cortical responses were profoundly suppressed and could invert into deactivation. The sleeping brain therefore does not simply block optical input; it processes it differently. [source 6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-73997-y)

The key distinction is:

**signal reaches the nervous system ≠ signal is consciously perceived ≠ signal is understood**

### Meaningful communication during sleep has been demonstrated—but awareness matters

A 2021 multicenter study established **real-time two-way communication during verified REM sleep**. Across 36 participants, six people produced 29 correct answers while lucid dreaming, including answering novel questions and simple arithmetic using eye movements or facial-muscle signals. [source 7](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982221000592)

One particularly relevant trial used **red and green Morse-coded light flashes** to transmit the problem “4 minus 0.” The dreamer recognized the flashing code and correctly answered “4” using eye movements while remaining in REM sleep. [source 8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/)

That is genuine optical communication during sleep. But it is not evidence of subliminal communication: the participant later described consciously recognizing the flashing signal inside the dream. [source 9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/)

So these states must remain separate:

**asleep**
≠ **unable to remember afterward**
≠ **never consciously experienced the stimulus**

### Biological effects can occur without an obvious visual difference

Light can also alter physiology without conveying semantic information.

Researchers created displays matched for apparent **color and luminance** while varying melanopic stimulation roughly threefold. Eleven participants reported no significant difference in visual appearance, yet melatonin and subjective sleepiness differed between conditions. [source 10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29788219/)

Light delivered through closed eyelids can also affect circadian physiology. A study using individually calibrated 527-nm light found significant melatonin suppression when stimulation was delivered during both REM and NREM sleep and also observed shifts in dim-light melatonin onset. [source 11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22564396/)

A 2023 placebo-controlled study delivered **2-ms flashes every 15 seconds for one hour during sleep**. Circadian phase shifted substantially more than with placebo, while overall sleep architecture changed relatively little. [source 12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/)

That establishes another pathway:

**optical signal → physiological response**

But physiological sensitivity is not semantic comprehension.

Near-infrared research reinforces that distinction. In a sham-controlled experiment with 20 participants, **1064-nm transcranial photobiomodulation** produced significant increases in EEG alpha- and beta-band power. Invisible light can therefore alter measurable neural physiology under controlled conditions, but the study did not transmit semantic content. [source 13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31259198/)

Infrared can also carry actual information when an engineered receiver is added. In 2025, **upconversion contact lenses** allowed people to discriminate temporal codes and spatial images encoded in normally invisible near-infrared light by converting that radiation into visible light. [source 14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

### The unresolved question: subliminal semantic communication during ordinary sleep

Taken together, the research establishes that:

**Unnoticed optical modulation can produce identifiable neural responses.** [source 15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

**Closed eyelids can transmit temporally structured stimulation during sleep.** [source 16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28098351/)

**Sleep alters rather than completely eliminates neural processing of that stimulation.** [source 17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28334175/)

**Light can influence physiology during sleep without substantially waking the sleeper.** [source 18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22564396/)

**Invisible infrared can carry temporal and spatial information when an appropriate receiver converts it.** [source 19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

**Sleeping people can sometimes understand externally supplied information and respond during lucid REM sleep.** [source 20](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982221000592)

What has **not** been demonstrated end-to-end is:

**an ordinary, non-lucid sleeper receiving a novel optical message, extracting its semantic meaning, retaining or acting on that meaning, and doing so without consciously experiencing the signal.**

That distinction matters because the required processing increases at each stage:

**detecting a flash → discriminating patterns → associating patterns with meaning → decoding sequences → understanding language → retaining the information**

The existing literature demonstrates several earlier links in that chain. The unresolved research question is whether **temporally encoded optical stimulation can transmit specific semantic information to a non-lucid sleeper without conscious perception, with comprehension demonstrated behaviorally or through later memory rather than inferred merely from an EEG response.**
