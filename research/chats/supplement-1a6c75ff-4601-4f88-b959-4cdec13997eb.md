# Invisible Signals and the Sleeping Brain: What the Research Actually Shows

Research on sleep, subliminal perception, imperceptible light, brain–computer interfaces, circadian biology, and lucid dreaming points toward a conclusion that is more interesting than either extreme.

A sleeping person is not neurologically isolated from the outside world. External signals can enter the nervous system without waking someone. Some stimuli can produce measurable brain responses without being consciously perceived. Others can alter hormones or circadian timing. Previously learned information can sometimes be reactivated during sleep, and under unusual conditions sleeping people have even understood and answered novel questions.

But these findings represent different levels of influence. Detecting a signal, distinguishing patterns within it, understanding what those patterns mean, remembering their meaning, and subsequently changing behavior are separate achievements. No research reviewed here demonstrates a reliable system that combines all of them to secretly transmit arbitrary thoughts or instructions into an ordinary sleeping person.

That distinction is the key to understanding the field.

## What “invisible” actually means

Several very different phenomena tend to get grouped together as invisible or subliminal signaling.

A signal can use a wavelength humans ordinarily cannot see, such as near-infrared. Alternatively, the light itself can be visible while its flickering is too rapid to notice. Researchers can also manipulate the spectral composition of apparently steady white light so that two conditions look almost identical despite stimulating the retina differently.

Finally, a perfectly ordinary stimulus can be presented during sleep without awakening the recipient or without being remembered afterward.

These are not equivalent.

For example, rapid visible flicker can be consciously experienced as completely steady while the brain nevertheless follows its modulation. Infrared can be physically invisible but become consciously perceptible after conversion into visible light. And a sleeper can consciously experience an external stimulus inside a dream yet remember nothing about it the next morning.

Consequently, "the person didn't see it" does not by itself establish subconscious processing.  [unresolved source 0]

## The brain can detect visual changes people cannot consciously see

One of the clearest findings comes from experiments using very rapid flickering light.

In a 2022 study, researchers presented visible objects flickering at 56 and 60 Hz. Participants did not perceive the flickering, yet MEG measurements revealed strong responses corresponding to those frequencies.

More importantly, participants could deliberately attend to different apparently steady objects and thereby change their neural responses. Seven of eight participants were able to use those signals to control a Pong game.

A subsequent 2023 experiment developed a four-target EEG brain–computer interface using 60-Hz imperceptible flicker.  [unresolved source 1]

This establishes an important fact:

**Conscious visual experience and neural processing are not identical.**

Something can look completely steady while the nervous system retains information about its temporal modulation.

It does not, however, mean that an arbitrary message hidden in the modulation would automatically be understood. A computer detecting that the brain responded differently to 56 Hz and 60 Hz is fundamentally different from the person's unconscious mind interpreting one frequency as "yes" and another as "no."

## Even apparently identical light can produce different brain responses

Researchers have gone further by developing **invisible spectral flicker**.

Instead of making a light rapidly turn brighter and darker, researchers alternate between different mixtures of wavelengths designed to produce approximately the same apparent color and brightness.

The person therefore sees something resembling steady white light even though its physical spectrum is changing.

A 2022 experiment involving 13 participants found that this produced a measurable 40-Hz neural response. Subsequent experiments found responses across approximately 36–44 Hz while participants experienced substantially less visible flickering than with conventional stimulation.  [unresolved source 2]

Again, this demonstrates hidden **neural stimulation**, not hidden semantic communication.

The brain can respond systematically to a feature of the environment that barely appears in conscious visual experience.

## Truly invisible infrared can carry information

Another line of research removes visible light altogether.

In 2025, researchers reported experimental contact lenses containing upconversion particles. These particles absorb near-infrared radiation, which humans ordinarily cannot see, and emit visible light.

Participants wearing the lenses could distinguish temporally encoded infrared signals. Researchers demonstrated Morse-code-like flashing patterns, and reception could actually improve with the eyes closed because eyelids blocked interfering visible light more strongly than the infrared signal.  [unresolved source 3]

The sequence was essentially:

**invisible infrared signal → eyelid → contact lens → conversion into visible light → retina → perception.**

This is a genuine demonstration of an externally invisible communication carrier passing through closed eyelids.

But it is not subliminal communication.

The recipient ultimately perceives the converted light.

The experiment therefore demonstrates that **an invisible carrier can transmit information to an appropriately equipped person**, not that infrared can silently insert information into an unaware brain.

## The unaided human visual system also has an unusual infrared exception

Human vision is normally described as insensitive to infrared, but there is an interesting physical exception called **two-photon vision**.

Under carefully controlled conditions, two infrared photons can jointly activate a visual pigment in a way that produces a visible sensation.

Researchers have demonstrated the phenomenon at the photoreceptor level, and a 2025 experiment demonstrated a prototype infrared display capable of producing several perceived colors using two-photon stimulation.  [unresolved source 4]

This expands what is physically possible but does not provide a practical subliminal communication system. These experiments require specialized optical arrangements and controlled illumination.

They also demonstrate perception rather than unconscious semantic processing.

## Closed eyelids do not disconnect the visual system

The sleep research is particularly important because closed eyelids are surprisingly poor absolute barriers to temporal visual stimulation.

A 2017 experiment demonstrated steady-state visual evoked responses to flickering light through closed eyelids during both light and deep non-REM sleep.

In other words, the experimenter's signal remained detectable in the sleeper's brain even though the participant's eyes were closed and the participant remained asleep.  [unresolved source 5]

This establishes the first stage of a potential communication pathway:

**external signal → sleeping nervous system.**

But it does not establish:

**external signal → understood message.**

Those are profoundly different claims.

## Sleep changes how those signals are processed

Sleep is not simply wakefulness with consciousness switched off.

Experiments comparing responses across wakefulness and sleep have found that temporal visual processing changes substantially.

Responses to approximately 8–10 Hz flicker became weaker during non-REM and REM sleep, whereas responses to slower 3–5 Hz stimulation became stronger.

A 2026 simultaneous EEG/fMRI study involving 15 participants found another striking effect. During N1 and N2 sleep, light-related responses remained detectable in the visual thalamus, while early visual cortex showed strong negative rather than positive fMRI responses.

The researchers interpreted this as evidence consistent with cortical inhibition contributing to sensory disconnection during sleep. They also cautioned that reduced fMRI activity does not prove that all information processing at that location has stopped.  [unresolved source 6]

This undermines a tempting assumption:

**You cannot simply take an imperceptible signal that works in an awake BCI and assume a sleeping brain will process it in the same way.**

Sleep reorganizes the pathway.

## Invisible or unnoticed signals can alter physiology

The evidence becomes considerably stronger when the question changes from "Can a hidden message be understood?" to "Can an unnoticed stimulus change the body?"

The answer is clearly yes.

Researchers have designed lighting conditions that appear similarly colored and bright while stimulating different retinal pathways.

A 2018 crossover experiment involving 11 participants produced differences in melatonin and reported sleepiness even though participants did not report significant differences in color or glare.

A later experiment involving 29 participants again found differences in melatonin suppression between visually matched conditions, although the hormonal changes were not accompanied by corresponding changes in sleep, vigilance, or basic sensory performance.  [unresolved source 7]

That second result is particularly instructive.

It shows both the power and the limitation of subconscious biological influence:

**A stimulus can measurably alter biology without producing the psychological effect one might intuitively expect.**

## Light can manipulate the sleeping body's clock

Researchers have also delivered light directly through closed eyelids while people sleep.

A 2023 placebo-controlled crossover experiment used brief flashes delivered through goggles during sleep. The stimulation shifted participants' circadian timing while producing relatively little alteration of overall sleep architecture, although there was some evidence of increased sleep instability.

Earlier experiments likewise demonstrated circadian phase shifts using individually tailored flashing light through closed eyelids.  [unresolved source 8]

This is genuine manipulation of a sleeping person's physiology through an external signal.

But the word "manipulation" needs precision.

The body's circadian machinery reacts to environmental light. Researchers are exploiting that biological input pathway.

The sleeper does not have to interpret a message saying:

"Change your biological clock."

The clock responds directly.

This is analogous to adjusting a thermostat rather than persuading someone to change the temperature.

## Invisible infrared can affect tissue without acting as vision

There is another relevant field called **transcranial photobiomodulation**.

Here near-infrared radiation is directed at the head for proposed biological effects rather than being treated as a visual communication channel.

For example, a sham-controlled experiment involving 20 participants reported changes in EEG alpha and beta activity following controlled near-infrared stimulation of the forehead.  [unresolved source 9]

This means it would be incorrect to claim that invisible wavelengths cannot affect neural physiology.

But EEG changes are highly nonspecific.

Demonstrating that infrared changes a measured brain signal does not establish that infrared can encode a particular thought, instruction, memory, emotion, or belief.

Again:

**neural modulation is not semantic writing.**

## Sleeping people can sometimes process meaningful information

The most interesting boundary concerns meaning.

The sleeping brain is capable of more sophisticated processing than older models of sleep suggested.

There is evidence that previously learned associations can sometimes be reactivated during sleep. Rather than teaching the brain an entirely new symbolic language, researchers can exploit associations established while the participant was awake.

The source review also identified a 2025 experiment in which researchers presented names paired with positive personality traits during sleep. Subsequent testing found changes in aspects of participants' social evaluations compared with a control condition involving names without those positive traits.  [unresolved source 10]

This is much closer to psychological influence than changing melatonin.

But it still does not demonstrate unrestricted subconscious persuasion.

The sleeping brain already knows what the spoken words mean. Researchers are potentially modifying or reinforcing relationships among existing representations.

That is different from transmitting an unfamiliar encoded proposition through flickering light and expecting the unconscious brain to discover its meaning.

## Previously learned cues are therefore especially important

Consider two hypothetical situations.

In one, someone learns while awake that a particular sound or light pattern corresponds to something they studied. Researchers replay that cue during sleep and subsequently test whether memory for the associated information changes.

In the other, researchers transmit an entirely novel encoded sentence while someone sleeps and expect them to decode, understand, remember, and believe it.

The first requires the sleeping brain principally to **reactivate an existing representation**.

The second requires:

**signal detection → symbol discrimination → decoding → language comprehension → memory formation → integration with existing knowledge.**

Evidence for the first cannot be treated as evidence for the second.  [unresolved source 11]

This is one reason targeted memory reactivation and related approaches are considerably more scientifically plausible than science-fiction-style "sleep programming."

## Genuine conversations with sleeping people have been demonstrated

Perhaps the most remarkable evidence comes from lucid dreaming.

In a 2021 study involving 36 participants, experimenters successfully obtained 29 correct answers from six individuals during verified REM sleep.

Researchers used auditory, tactile, and visual communication methods.

In one particularly relevant case, an experienced lucid dreamer received a Morse-coded arithmetic problem using flashes, interpreted the question, calculated an answer, and communicated that answer through predetermined eye movements.  [unresolved source 12]

That establishes something extraordinary but quite specific:

**A human being can sometimes receive and understand genuinely new external information while physiologically asleep.**

The experiment crossed the semantic barrier that simple EEG-response studies do not.

But it did not cross the subliminal barrier.

## Lucid-dream communication is not subconscious programming

Lucid dreamers possess some degree of conscious awareness during their dreams.

Participants in interactive-dreaming experiments sometimes reported that experimenters' questions appeared within the dream itself.

The Morse-code example therefore does not demonstrate an unconscious brain silently decoding hidden instructions.

It demonstrates communication with a conscious person who happens to be asleep.  [unresolved source 13]

This distinction also exposes a major problem with using morning memory as a measure of awareness.

Someone can consciously experience something while dreaming and subsequently forget it.

Therefore:

**"They didn't remember it" does not mean "they weren't conscious of it."**

Any experiment claiming truly subconscious sleep communication must measure awareness separately from later recall.

## Subliminal processing exists, but its upper limits remain disputed

Research on masked visual stimuli shows that the brain can process some information under conditions where participants report no awareness.

But researchers continue to debate how sophisticated that processing can become.

A 2026 study of masked visual shapes found evidence consistent with unconscious processing under some analytical definitions but not others. Results depended partly on whether unconscious perception meant the absence of reported subjective awareness or objectively chance-level discrimination.  [unresolved source 14]

That methodological problem matters enormously for claims about manipulation.

If someone says they saw nothing but can nevertheless guess the stimulus correctly 70% of the time, were they truly unaware?

Different definitions produce different answers.

There is therefore legitimate evidence for processing outside reported awareness, but not evidence for an unlimited unconscious language processor capable of absorbing arbitrary hidden messages.

## Brain response, comprehension, and manipulation are different levels

The entire literature becomes easier to understand if it is divided into four stages.

### Physical delivery

Does the signal actually reach the nervous system?

For light through closed eyelids, the answer is clearly yes.

### Neural discrimination

Does the brain respond differently to different signals?

Again, yes. EEG and MEG frequency-tagging experiments demonstrate this particularly clearly.

### Semantic understanding

Does the recipient understand what the pattern means?

This has been demonstrated in some conscious or lucid-dream situations and with already meaningful sensory material. It has not been established for arbitrary subliminal optical codes during ordinary sleep.

### Behavioral manipulation

Does receiving the information reliably change subsequent decisions, beliefs, memories, or actions in a specified way?

Limited effects involving memory, associations, social evaluations, circadian physiology, and other outcomes exist.

Reliable arbitrary behavioral control does not.

These distinctions are the core of the evidence.  [unresolved source 15]

## The most plausible form of subconscious influence is therefore not "mind control"

The research instead points toward several much narrower mechanisms.

A hidden stimulus might change physiology directly.

A previously learned cue might reactivate an existing memory.

A meaningful sound might influence how existing information is consolidated.

A sensory stimulus might become incorporated into a dream.

A person in a lucid dream might consciously interpret an external message.

An imperceptible flicker might generate a distinctive neural response without conveying any conscious meaning.

Each phenomenon is experimentally interesting.

Combining them into the statement that researchers can invisibly program a sleeping person's thoughts would be an unsupported extrapolation.

## Invisible does not mean harmless

There is also an important safety misconception.

Radiation being invisible or stimulation being imperceptible says nothing about whether it is biologically harmless.

Near-infrared radiation can damage the retina under inappropriate exposure conditions. Other infrared wavelengths can injure anterior eye structures. Flickering illumination can provoke adverse symptoms in susceptible individuals. Specialized two-photon experiments use optical conditions that should not be reproduced casually.  [unresolved source 16]

The absence of sensation is therefore not a safety mechanism.

## What would actually prove subliminal sleep communication?

A convincing experiment would have to establish the entire chain rather than merely one component.

Researchers would first need to verify physically that the intended signal reaches the relevant sensory system.

They would then need blinded tests showing that the sleeping brain reliably distinguishes multiple encoded patterns.

Next, the participant would need to demonstrate understanding of their meaning—for example, by correctly responding to previously unseen messages rather than merely exhibiting different EEG responses.

Researchers would also have to demonstrate that the person remained unaware of those messages, rather than consciously experiencing them within dreams and subsequently forgetting them.

Finally, if the claim concerned psychological manipulation, investigators would need preregistered evidence that the messages produced the predicted later behavioral effect compared with carefully matched sham conditions.

The research reviewed here has demonstrated **individual pieces of this chain**.

It has not demonstrated the complete chain for arbitrary subliminal messages during ordinary sleep.  [unresolved source 17]

## The overall picture

The sleeping brain is considerably more connected to its environment than a simple "unconscious and switched off" model would suggest.

External light can penetrate closed eyelids.

The sleeping visual system can track temporal stimulation.

Signals people cannot consciously distinguish can nevertheless generate measurable neural responses.

Apparently identical lighting can produce different physiological effects.

Light delivered during sleep can alter circadian timing.

Previously learned associations can sometimes be modified or reactivated during sleep.

And in the unusual state of lucid dreaming, people have sometimes received, understood, and answered genuinely novel external questions.

Those findings collectively demonstrate that **sleep does not prevent external influence, and conscious perception is not required for every biological or neural effect**.  [unresolved source 18]

But they do not establish a technology for secretly writing arbitrary information into someone's mind.

The evidence currently supports a progression from very strong to increasingly uncertain claims:

**Invisible or unnoticed stimulus → brain response:** well demonstrated.

**Invisible or unnoticed stimulus → physiological change:** well demonstrated.

**Sleep stimulus → alteration or reactivation of existing associations:** supported under particular experimental conditions.

**Sleep stimulus → understanding new information:** demonstrated under special conditions, particularly interactive lucid dreaming.

**Completely subliminal invisible signal → comprehension of arbitrary new messages during ordinary sleep:** not demonstrated.

**Completely subliminal invisible signal → precise control of beliefs, memories, or complex behavior:** not demonstrated.
