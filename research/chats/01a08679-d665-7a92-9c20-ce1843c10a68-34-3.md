Yes, that’s a coherent hypothesis: **cue–state discrimination**.

Instead of “sound affects sleep” generically, the idea would be:

> Different acoustic features could become associated with different sleep states, emotional states, or cognitive sets.

Examples:
- **Pitch**: high tone vs low tone.
- **Timbre/depth**: dull thud vs sharp knock.
- **Rhythm**: single knock vs repeated pattern.
- **Spatial quality**: near/far, left/right, muffled/clear.
- **Intensity envelope**: sudden attack vs soft swell.

Mechanistically, there are two separable questions:

1. **Can sounds be delivered differently by sleep state?**  
   Yes, in principle. Closed-loop sleep work already times sounds to detected sleep stages or slow-wave phase. That is a delivery/timing question.

2. **Can different sounds become paired with different meanings or reactions?**  
   Also plausible in principle. That would be associative learning / targeted memory reactivation: a cue previously paired with a memory, task, or emotional context may later bias recall, salience, or affect when replayed.

The stronger version you’re imagining is:

> Low thud means one conditioned association, higher knock means another, and the system times them to sleep stages where they are less consciously noticed but still processed enough to reinforce or reactivate associations.

For the machine, I’d split it into a clean factorial model:

- **Sleep state:** wake, N1, N2, N3, REM.
- **Cue feature:** pitch, thud depth, knock sharpness, rhythm, volume.
- **Prior pairing:** paired vs unpaired.
- **Outcome:** recall bias, affective salience, dream incorporation, startle/arousal, next-day rumination.

The falsifiers matter:
- If all sounds have the same effect, feature-specific conditioning is weak.
- If only volume/startle matters, then “meaningful signal” is unnecessary.
- If effects vanish when participants are unaware of pairings or when random cues are used, the pathway is weaker.
- If cue effects are explained by awakenings, sleep fragmentation, or annoyance, that’s not subconscious association. It’s ordinary disruption.

So: yes, we should model this as **acoustic feature coding of associative cues by sleep state**, but keep it bounded. It is plausible enough to test; it is not evidence by itself that anyone used it.
