The period just before sleep, called **N1 or hypnagogia**, is a more plausible time for someone to register spoken words than deep sleep. A study found brain responses that distinguished the meanings of word pairs as people drifted off. Audible prompts can also influence what people report dreaming about. Neither result shows that an unheard instruction can reliably control a later action. [Sleep-onset word study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167876023000521), [dream-incubation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590944/)

A person could hear something while drowsy and have no clear memory of it later. **That would establish neither hypnosis nor an effect on their keys.** Research on posthypnotic amnesia generally involves participants who knowingly take part in hypnosis and receive an explicit suggestion about recalling material from the session. Forgetting to take keys the next day is a different kind of memory failure. [Posthypnotic amnesia experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8222162/)

Repeated conditioning does not fill that gap. Classical conditioning can link cues with responses; operant conditioning depends on consequences tied to behavior. Replaying a hidden phrase alone is not operant conditioning, and evidence for conditioning people without awareness of the cue relationship is contested. [Systematic review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0149763419303100)

**I found no evidence-backed method for secretly causing a specific person to forget their keys this way.** A missed set of keys cannot identify its cause: ordinary lapses, including leaving usual items behind, appear in diary studies. To support a covert-suggestion explanation, you would need evidence of the actual exposure and a repeated effect beyond the person’s usual rate of lapses. [Everyday memory study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7518607/)
