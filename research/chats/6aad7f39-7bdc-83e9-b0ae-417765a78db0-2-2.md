**The research supports carefully timed sleep cueing—but does not yet establish a benefit from putting the technology inside a dental crown.** Human studies have demonstrated changes in memory, dream content, and some social evaluations. Separately, dental devices can transmit sound and collect physiological measurements. The missing evidence is that combining these capabilities in a crown improves sleep-based interventions. [source 0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/) [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/) [source 2](https://doi.org/10.1097/MAO.0000000000004664)

The findings below cover literature available through September 18, 2026. They also narrow several claims in my earlier answer.

## Memory: the strongest evidence concerns reinforcing prior learning

**Hu and colleagues’ 2020 meta-analysis, “Promoting memory consolidation during sleep,”** combined 91 experiments involving 2,004 participants. Targeted memory reactivation—replaying a sound or other cue associated with something learned beforehand—produced a small average memory benefit: Hedges’ *g* = 0.29, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.21–0.38. That is a standardized effect size, **not a 29% improvement in memory**. Evidence was strongest for non-REM sleep. [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

This supports a relatively specific capability: influencing which previously acquired memories become better consolidated. It does not establish that someone can learn an unfamiliar subject simply by hearing a recording overnight. That distinction matters for a dental device: transmitting a sound would address only stimulus delivery, not whether the sound has an effective relationship to a memory. [source 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

A useful real-world example is **Salfi and colleagues’ 2025 study, “Promoting vocabulary learning during sleep at home using closed-loop targeted memory reactivation.”** Twenty-four young adults learned translations of 40 invented words while awake. At home, an EEG headband detected sleep slow waves and triggered playback of selected words. Next-morning recall was better for the cued material. Importantly, the researchers delivered the words through **external loudspeakers**, not through the headband’s bone-conduction system. Timing also varied rather than precisely targeting the same brain-wave phase every time. This was a small, encouraging home experiment—not evidence for a dental advantage or learning an unknown language during sleep. [source 5](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.70000)

More stimulation is not necessarily better. In **Subramanian and colleagues’ 2026 experiment**, researchers combined memory-associated sounds with odors previously associated with instructions to remember or forget. The intended selective-forgetting effect did not occur; meaningful odor cues instead interfered with the sound-related memory benefit. The authors interpreted this as competition between reactivated memories. That complicates any proposal to combine numerous cues into a predictable “influence” system. [source 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13047899/)

## Dreams: themes can be encouraged, but complete experiences are not programmable

**Haar Horowitz and colleagues’ 2023 controlled study, “Targeted dream incubation at sleep onset increases post-sleep creative performance,”** analyzed 49 participants across sleep/wake and incubation/no-incubation conditions. Participants prompted to think about trees around sleep onset subsequently reported related dream content and performed better on tree-related creativity tasks. [source 7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-31361-w)

The important limitation is the setting: this involved the transition into sleep, repeated awakenings, and dream reports. It was not an experiment in transmitting a detailed narrative into uninterrupted deep sleep. The creativity outcomes were also related to the prompted theme, rather than demonstrating a general increase in intelligence or creativity. [source 8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-31361-w)

A **June 2026 follow-up pilot** extended the question into later REM sleep. Eleven participants underwent sleep-onset incubation; eight subsequently reached REM sleep, and four of those eight reported the target theme in their first REM dream. However, there was no control group, and the investigators acknowledged limitations in sleep staging and the possibility that participants’ expectations affected reports. This is preliminary evidence of carryover, not a reliable success rate for directing overnight dreams. [source 9](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sleep/articles/10.3389/frsle.2026.1812535/full)

The more clinically relevant evidence comes from **Schwartz, Clerget, and Perogamvros’ 2022 study, “Enhancing imagery rehearsal therapy for nightmares with targeted memory reactivation.”** Thirty-six patients practiced imagining a more positive ending to a nightmare. Half associated that rehearsal with a sound; the other half did not. Both groups subsequently heard the sound during REM sleep at home. The group with the learned sound association experienced fewer nightmares after two weeks, with a sustained reduction at three months. [source 10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)

Crucially for the dental proposal, the documented setup delivered sound by **bone conduction from the forehead**. Bone-conducted sleep cueing therefore already has a human therapeutic precedent without an intraoral device. The meaningful component was the association established during therapy—not an inherently therapeutic sound or a special route around normal auditory processing. [source 11](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.02.17.22270256v2.full)

## Social evaluations: a substantive finding with unresolved limits

**Zhao and colleagues’ 2025 paper, “Updating social evaluation during sleep,”** is particularly relevant to our earlier discussion.

Researchers analyzed 106 participants: 76 in the main experiment and 30 in a separately recruited name-only control. Participants first learned about unfamiliar people while awake. During slow-wave sleep, researchers played one person’s name paired with new positive personality traits. Subsequent measures favored that person, including likability, implicit associations, recollection of positive traits, and reconstructed mental facial images. [source 12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/) [source 13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/)

The findings should not be dismissed as merely repeating information already learned: the sleep-presented traits were new. However, the authors could not distinguish new associative learning from strengthening related, pre-existing memories. There was no long-term follow-up, awake comparison, or matched name-plus-neutral-traits condition. The participants and facial stimuli were also narrowly selected. **The study supports a laboratory change in evaluation, not established, durable control over someone’s real-world attitudes.** [source 14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/)

Replication history warrants caution. **Humiston and Wamsley’s 2019 study** attempted to reproduce an earlier sleep-cueing result involving implicit social biases in 31 participants and found no cueing benefit immediately or one week later. That was a different intervention—not a failed replication of Zhao’s 2025 experiment—but it shows why an initial positive attitude-related result should not be treated as a dependable technology. [source 15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30682167/)

## Brain-wave effects: measurable changes do not guarantee useful outcomes

Closed-loop auditory stimulation, or CLAS, typically uses brief sounds timed to sleep oscillations. Its immediate target is a physiological pattern, rather than a particular memory or proposition. [source 16](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00088/full)

In **Debellemaniere and colleagues’ 2018 study**, researchers evaluated an EEG headband with a forehead bone-conduction transducer. The work included a 20-person validation study and a 90-person home observational component. Stimulation increased measured delta-band power by approximately 43.9% during the four-second window following stimulation. That was a brief EEG response—not 43.9% more deep sleep, better memory, or improved health. [source 17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5853451/) [source 18](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00088/full)

A particularly informative counterpoint is **Baldassarri and colleagues’ study**, published online in November 2025 and in a 2026 journal issue. Among 34 participants, one night of home CLAS increased slow-oscillation amplitude but did **not demonstrate improved memory or vigilance**. The sample cannot rule out every possible benefit, but it directly illustrates why a stronger brain-wave response is not sufficient evidence of a useful intervention. [source 19](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.70247)

This distinction is central to evaluating a proposed crown. Even perfectly recording a delivered vibration—or showing that it elicited an EEG response—would not establish that it improved memory, changed an evaluation, or influenced a dream as intended. Those require separate behavioral measurements.

## What dental-device studies actually establish

**Sound transmission through teeth is real.** In a 2025 prospective study, **Kou and colleagues** evaluated a SoundBite dental bone-conduction device in 38 patients with single-sided deafness following acoustic-neuroma surgery. Hearing and speech-recognition measures improved. However, the study was nonrandomized and unblinded, and it evaluated hearing rehabilitation—not sleeping participants, dream influence, or an embedded crown. [source 20](https://doi.org/10.1097/MAO.0000000000004664)

Earlier SoundBite descriptions explain the mechanism: a removable oral appliance applies mechanical vibrations through the teeth, transmitting sound toward the functioning inner ear. This is an alternative route for hearing, not evidence of bypassing the auditory system to deliver thoughts directly. [source 21](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ajodo.2010.03.027)

**Oral appliances can also collect useful sleep-related data.** In **Mohammadieh and colleagues’ 2025 smart mandibular-advancement-device pilot**, measurements correlated with polysomnography for apnea–hypopnea index and total sleep time: approximately *r* = 0.81 and 0.80, respectively. But correlation is not the same as diagnostic accuracy; *r* = 0.80 does not mean “80% accurate.” The device also used an intraoral multisensor system, not a single crown-mounted strain sensor. These findings support monitoring applications, not precise measurement of brain-wave phase or mental processing. [source 22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12320676/)

**Battery-free dental electronics are feasible—but the demonstrated functions are narrower.** **Shi and colleagues’ 2022 dental patch** used near-field communication to obtain power, measure local acidity, and control fluoride release. It was a tooth-surface patch, approximately 10 × 8 × 1.5 millimeters, rather than an invisible component inside an ordinary crown. Its short communication distance was an acknowledged limitation. It did not demonstrate a self-contained, continuously operating overnight audio system. [source 23](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41528-022-00185-5)

Two corrections to my earlier answer follow.

First, calling a crown a **“stimulus dosimeter”** was too strong. The cited tooth-accelerometer research concerned mechanical head-motion measurement, not a validated method for determining the auditory exposure received by a sleeping person. A sensor’s local vibration reading cannot simply be equated with what reached the inner ear or was understood. [source 24](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/assessment-ear-tooth-mounted-accelerometers-representative-human-head-response-2013-01-0805)

Second, jaw movement could supply a useful additional response channel, but it cannot by itself establish that a participant remained asleep or exclude brief cortical arousals. The successful sleep-cueing systems discussed above relied on EEG-based monitoring; the intraoral pilot demonstrated different, indirect sleep metrics. [source 25](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00088/full) [source 26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12320676/)

The relevant sequence remains:

**A device produced a signal → the person received a stimulus → the brain responded → a meaningful outcome changed.**

Each arrow needs evidence. Measuring the first or second step does not establish the last.

## Where the dental approach could genuinely help

I did not identify a controlled human study demonstrating that an embedded dental crown improves TMR, targeted dream incubation, or CLAS. The direct dental-and-sleep-cueing literature search surfaced a conceptual dentistry article, not a trial of such an integrated device.

My assessment is that the most defensible application is **an optional research instrument**, preferably removable. It could be tested as an additional source of oral-motion data, a mechanically coupled sound-delivery interface, or a means of checking whether an actuator operated consistently. Better comfort, less sound leakage, and more reliable delivery would be hypotheses to test—not established advantages over an existing forehead transducer.

A decisive experiment would compare the oral device with a conventional delivery method while keeping the learned cues and EEG-based timing comparable. An inactive but otherwise similar appliance would help distinguish effects of stimulation from effects of wearing something in the mouth. Outcomes should include delayed memory or clinical benefit, sleep disruption, tolerability, and independently scored results—not merely larger sensor readings.

**The present evidence favors testing whether a removable oral device adds measurable value to an existing sleep-research system. It does not justify permanently modifying a tooth solely for this purpose.**

## News report: Sleep cues can change memories—but “smart teeth” remain an unproven extension

Scientists have shown that carefully chosen sounds can influence what people remember after sleep, encourage particular dream themes, and, in a small clinical study, strengthen treatment for recurring nightmares. But the evidence does not yet show that placing a sensor or sound generator inside a dental crown makes those effects more effective. [source 27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/) [source 28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10185495/) [source 29](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)

The most established technique works more like a reminder than an overnight lesson. A person learns something while awake and associates it with a sound. Researchers replay that sound during sleep, when memories are being consolidated. A large analysis of experiments found a small average improvement in later remembering—not the ability to acquire an unfamiliar subject without studying it. [source 30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7144680/)

Some applications extend beyond remembering words. In a study involving 36 people with recurring nightmares, patients practiced imagining more positive endings. Those who first connected the exercise with a sound benefited more when that sound was replayed during REM sleep. The sounds came through a bone-conduction headband, showing that this delivery method does not require dental hardware. [source 31](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032) [source 32](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.02.17.22270256v2.full)

Other findings remain more preliminary. A 2025 experiment reported changes in evaluations of unfamiliar people after positive descriptions were played during sleep, but did not establish how long those changes lasted. A small 2026 dream study found that a suggested theme sometimes appeared in later REM dreams; without a control group, its reliability remains uncertain. [source 33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/) [source 34](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sleep/articles/10.3389/frsle.2026.1812535/full)

Dental technology supplies a separate piece of the picture. Hearing devices have transmitted sound through teeth, while experimental oral appliances have collected sleep-related measurements. Neither achievement demonstrates that a tooth-mounted device can determine whether a message was understood or a memory changed. [source 35](https://doi.org/10.1097/MAO.0000000000004664) [source 36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12320676/)

There is also a broader warning for the field: changing a measurable brain signal is not necessarily the same as helping someone. A recent home study increased the amplitude of sleep slow waves without demonstrating better memory or alertness. [source 37](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.70247)

For a “smart tooth,” the next scientific hurdle is therefore straightforward: demonstrate a benefit over simpler, removable equipment. Until that comparison is made, the credible prospect is an additional tool for studying sleep—not an established new way to control what a sleeping person thinks.
