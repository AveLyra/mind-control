**Possibly for an isolated word under carefully controlled conditions; there is no good evidence that a person can subconsciously understand a spoken message disguised as modem buzzing.** The distinction is whether recognizable speech information reaches the auditory system.

- **If it is speech mixed under a buzz:** a listener might fail to notice a word yet process some part of it. One experiment found that masked words affected later recognition of the *same word*, without evidence of processing its meaning. Another small experiment found a brief meaning-related effect for low-volume words in slower responders. Those are effects on a subsequent laboratory task, not evidence that listeners understood sentences or followed instructions. [Masked-word study](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2005.01584.x/) · [Meaning-related study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3105000/).

- **If speech has been converted into modem-like tones:** the tones may encode words for a device with the right decoder, but hearing them does not give the brain that decoder. A single tone or buzz has no inherent verbal meaning. For a human to understand it, some speech cues would have to remain audible, or the person would have to learn the code.

- **If “frequency” means sound outside ordinary hearing:** typical airborne human hearing extends roughly from 20 to 20,000 Hz, with much of speech information in a narrower range. Unheard high-frequency sound is not a separate route for delivering intelligible sentences to a “subconscious.” Specialized bone-conduction findings do not demonstrate hidden-message comprehension from ordinary speakers. [NIH hearing overview](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK20366/) · [Ultrasonic hearing experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2063208/).

The best practical check on claims of prolonged subliminal influence is a double-blind study of audio tapes used for a month by 237 people who wanted their advertised benefits. The hidden content did not produce the claimed memory or self-esteem improvements; people’s *belief about the tape’s label* did influence what improvement they reported. [Double-blind study](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1991.tb00112.x/).

So the strongest objection to a flat “no” is real: **some unattended or poorly perceived words can receive limited processing**. But recognizing a trace of a word, understanding a camouflaged message, and being influenced by its instructions are three progressively stronger claims. The evidence here supports the first under some conditions, not the latter two. A modem buzz itself could become a conditioned cue if repeatedly paired with an experience, but that would be learning a response to the *sound*, not decoding a hidden verbal message.
