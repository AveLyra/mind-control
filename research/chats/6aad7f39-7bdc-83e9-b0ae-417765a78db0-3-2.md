**Yes for several vital signs. Oral EEG is an experimental possibility—but it is not an established capability of a sensor inside a crown.** The strongest evidence concerns larger, removable oral appliances. [source 0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12266342/) [source 1](https://med.stanford.edu/biodesign/news---events/health-technology-showcase/an-oral-retainer-could-alleviate-the-need-for-an-extensive-hospital-stay-to-accurately-diagnose-epilepsy.html)

**Pulse and blood oxygen are already demonstrated.** A 2025 study tested an oral appliance with an optical sensor facing the inner cheek in 12 healthy adults. Against arterial blood measurements and ECG, its root-mean-square errors were approximately **2.94 percentage points for oxygen saturation and 2.08 beats/minute for pulse**. Those results came from controlled, motionless testing—not unrestricted overnight use. Importantly, it measured blood-containing cheek tissue, not the tooth itself. [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12266342/)

**Temperature is feasible too.** A 2022 instrumented-mouthguard study demonstrated temperature measurement, although it involved bench testing and one volunteer, and readings took considerable time to stabilize. It supports monitoring temperature trends, not detecting instantaneous physiological responses. [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9343656/)

**EEG requires a different measurement system.** A strain sensor records mechanical deformation; EEG records electrical voltage differences between electrodes. Mouthguard projects have demonstrated or explored **EMG—muscle electrical activity—which must not be mistaken for brain activity**. An EEG-capable design would need suitable electrodes, a reference, and validation that the recorded signal actually contains usable cerebral activity. [source 4](https://sites.uci.edu/mengprojects/glidewell-dental/) [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5606130/)

There is a relevant **Stanford oral-retainer EEG concept**, using electrodes against the roof of the mouth. However, the reported testing used a pig-head model with an artificial signal generator—not validated EEG recordings from sleeping humans. That establishes an engineering investigation, not demonstrated sleep monitoring. I did not find validated single-crown EEG in this search. [source 6](https://med.stanford.edu/biodesign/news---events/health-technology-showcase/an-oral-retainer-could-alleviate-the-need-for-an-extensive-hospital-stay-to-accurately-diagnose-epilepsy.html)

**For your sleep experiments, my recommendation is to separate the measurements:** oral pulse/oxygen sensing for physiological context, and a validated EEG system for brain-state measurement. Ear-mounted EEG already has human studies comparing sleep staging against polysomnography, including a recent 20-participant study. Crucially, successful sleep-stage classification still does not automatically establish sufficiently accurate, real-time brain-wave timing for stimulation. [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1109/JBHI.2025.3599874)

The useful distinction is **“the body responded,” “the brain changed state,” and “memory or behavior changed.”** Vitals, EEG, and behavioral testing answer those different questions; none substitutes for all three.
