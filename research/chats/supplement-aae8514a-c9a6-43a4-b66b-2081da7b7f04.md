The easiest way to understand the abuse risk is to separate “what is actually demonstrated” from “what people might fear.”

The most realistic abuses are not science-fiction mind control. They are subtler: manipulating attention, collecting neural data without meaningful consent, influencing physiology, or using ambiguous brain signals as if they revealed a person’s thoughts.

A few concrete abuse scenarios:

- **Hidden attention steering.** If imperceptible flicker can produce measurable brain responses, a display could potentially be designed to influence visual attention or arousal without obvious flashing. What is demonstrated is neural entrainment and stimulus tagging—not insertion of beliefs.
- **Covert physiological influence.** Light can affect circadian timing, melatonin, alertness, and some measurable brain rhythms without being very noticeable. Abuse would look more like sleep disruption, fatigue manipulation, or altered arousal than “programming” someone.
- **Neural surveillance.** The strongest privacy concern comes from people already wearing or implanted with neural sensors. If raw neural data are collected, a company, attacker, employer, or state actor could try to infer more than the user intended to reveal. The danger is especially serious because imperfect decoding may still look convincing.
- **Unauthorized stimulation of implanted devices.** Future neural implants that receive wireless power or commands create an ordinary cybersecurity problem with unusual consequences: unauthorized commands could affect movement, sensation, or other physiological functions if authentication and hardware safety limits failed.
- **Manipulation during sleep.** Sleepers can process some external stimuli, and lucid dreamers have communicated with experimenters. A malicious application could plausibly aim to disturb sleep, bias arousal, or repeatedly cue previously learned associations. Evidence does not currently show that an unaware sleeper can be fed arbitrary new beliefs or instructions.
- **False “mind-reading.”** This may be one of the most immediate harms. Weak or probabilistic neural signals could be presented as proof that someone intended, remembered, or believed something. A decoder can be wrong while still producing plausible-looking output.

The core risk ladder is:

**hidden stimulus → physiological response → behavioral influence → semantic communication → deliberate thought control**

The evidence is reasonably strong at the first two levels, much more limited at behavioral influence, and does not currently establish the last step.

So the practical abuse concern is less “someone secretly uploads a sentence into your brain” and more **covert stimulation, sleep/arousal manipulation, invasive neural-data collection, unauthorized device control, and overclaiming what uncertain brain signals mean**.
