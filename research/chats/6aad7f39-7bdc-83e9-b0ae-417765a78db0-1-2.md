Yes. For the sleep-influence work we discussed, an intraoral device becomes substantially more interesting if you treat it as a combined **stimulus dosimeter + physiological sensor + possible stimulus actuator**, rather than as a way of measuring cognition directly. 

The highest-value uses are:

1. **Verify what stimulus actually reaches the sleeper.**  
A tooth is unusually well mechanically coupled to the skull. Tooth-mounted accelerometers have tracked skull acceleration reasonably closely, although the tooth introduces a transfer function/amplification that must be calibrated. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] This means an accelerometer or piezoelectric element could timestamp extremely small mechanical events at the dentition while an external microphone records airborne sound.

That would be useful for our earlier question of whether an observed response follows a particular sound/vibration event. Instead of merely recording “speaker played cue at 02:14:32,” you could determine:

> stimulus emitted → airborne field → skull/tooth vibration → jaw/arousal response → EEG response → subsequent behavioral effect.

It would not establish source, intent, or psychological influence by itself.

2. **Add an unusually good arousal/response channel.**  
Jaw movement contains much more sleep information than I initially gave it credit for. Mandibular-movement research shows relationships with sleep/wake transitions, respiratory effort and cortical/subcortical arousals; machine-learning systems can extract sleep-state information from these signals. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] A 2025 smart mandibular device also showed substantial agreement with PSG for total sleep time, sleep position and apnea-related measurements, although it was a small pilot and does not replace polysomnography. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

So for TMR/TDI experiments, an oral sensor could answer a crucial confound:

**Did the cue alter processing while the person remained asleep, or did it simply cause a microarousal?**

Bruxism, swallowing, jaw opening, clenching and other movements could also serve as precisely timestamped response markers. Modern IMU work has classified mandibular movements with high accuracy in small proof-of-concept datasets. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

3. **A tooth can also be an acoustic input channel.**  
This may be the most interesting extension. Dental bone-conduction hearing systems such as SoundBite have already demonstrated that a piezoelectric transducer acting on the teeth can deliver intelligible sound through the skull. Clinical work showed usable auditory transmission through an intraoral appliance; earlier engineering characterization reported operation over roughly 250–12,000 Hz. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

That creates a potentially valuable experimental comparison:

**air-conducted cue vs tooth/bone-conducted cue vs sham.**

For TMR or TDI, this could provide a highly localized cue, reduce room sound and interference with a sleeping partner, and give extremely reproducible stimulus timing. It does not create a fundamentally new mechanism of influence—the cochlea still receives the mechanical signal—but it gives you a different and controllable route into the auditory system.

This is particularly relevant because TMR effects depend heavily on cueing and sleep state. The large TMR meta-analysis found a modest overall effect, Hedges' \(g≈0.29\), concentrated in NREM sleep. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] The 2024 review continues to characterize TMR as a way of biasing endogenous memory reactivation rather than inserting arbitrary information. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] And the 2025 social-evaluation experiment we discussed actually presented name-plus-positive-trait audio during slow-wave sleep and observed changes in later representations of the cued people; simple name exposure was insufficient. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

4. **It could improve closed-loop experiments, but it cannot replace EEG.**  
For CLAS, the critical variable is instantaneous brain-wave phase. Modern CLAS detects slow oscillations from EEG and triggers sound at particular phases. A crown cannot infer that phase sufficiently precisely from tooth movement. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

So the useful architecture would be:

**EEG → determines state/phase**  
**tooth/mouthguard → verifies physical stimulus + jaw/arousal response**  
**external microphone → records ambient acoustic field**  
**tooth transducer or speaker → delivers cue**  
**behavioral testing → determines whether anything subsequently changed**

That gives you much better causal resolution than EEG + speaker alone.

There is also an important reason to measure the response rather than assuming stimulation “worked.” CLAS memory effects have been inconsistent; a 2023 meta-analysis actually found a pronounced decline in reported memory benefits over time and raised issues involving blinding and outcome reliability. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The permanent **crown**, though, is probably not the best first implementation. A removable instrumented mouthguard can already support accelerometers, gyroscopes, pressure sensors, wireless electronics and multiple sensing sites; recent aligner work has demonstrated continuous occlusal-force monitoring and detection of behaviors such as grinding. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat] A crown becomes attractive only if you eventually need much stronger tooth/skull coupling, unobtrusive long-term monitoring, or very repeatable geometry across many nights.

The strongest novel direction for the project, therefore, is probably **not “smart crown detects influence.”** It is a calibrated intraoral node that lets you experimentally separate:

**stimulus generated → stimulus physically received → sleeper remained asleep/aroused → neural state changed → memory/evaluation/dream outcome changed.**

That addresses one of the largest measurement gaps in the sleep-influence experiments we had been discussing.
