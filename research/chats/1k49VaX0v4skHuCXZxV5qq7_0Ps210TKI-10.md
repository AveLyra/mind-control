**A person can learn to respond to a cue associated with hypnosis, but the evidence falls far short of a *Manchurian Candidate* trigger.** A familiar word or gesture might help a willing person become focused or relaxed again. A *posthypnotic suggestion* can also prompt a specific response after the session ends. Neither finding establishes that a cue can automatically put someone under another person’s control.

The strongest evidence for a cue working outside the session comes from a study of 17 people selected for high hypnotic responsiveness. They were told to touch their forehead on hearing a particular word over the next 48 hours. **Five responded consistently even when the experimenter was absent.** That is a real effect, but it was a simple, harmless action in a small, selected group; the study did not show that they re-entered hypnosis or lost the ability to choose. [Original study](https://www.psych.upenn.edu/history/orne/orneetal1968jpsp189196.html).

Context and the requested action matter. In another experiment, a straightforward social request produced *more* of an everyday behavior—mailing postcards—than a posthypnotic suggestion did. A newer study found that a cue linked to a suggestion made highly responsive volunteers answer a memory task faster and more confidently a week later, **without improving their accuracy**. These results show influence, not a dependable switch. [Postcard experiments](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00052) · [Memory-cue experiment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2024.104240).

There is also a measurement problem: “entered a hypnotic state” is less definite than “performed this action” or “reported this experience.” A response to a cue alone cannot tell us whether hypnosis returned, an ordinary learned association operated, or the person acted on an expectation.

So my answer is **yes, in the limited sense of a learned cue or posthypnotic response; no demonstrated evidence for an automatic, durable trigger that reliably compels complex acts against someone’s wishes**. The hidden-audio and drug scenarios from your earlier questions do not close that evidentiary gap.
