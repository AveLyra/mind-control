**There is evidence that people can perform a simple action while failing to remember the instruction that prompted it. Evidence that this can be achieved through speech below their hearing threshold is much weaker.** Those are separate claims.

First, I should correct my earlier wording: **“physically below the hearing threshold” was a misleading heading for the tone study.** Its conclusion concerned tones *slightly above* hearing threshold. It measured electrical brain responses in ten sleeping participants; it tested neither word meanings nor compliance with instructions. A brain response to a faint sound establishes auditory processing, not understanding a directive. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21886362/)

The closest evidence to your specific question comes from **posthypnotic responding with amnesia**. In a small, highly selected study, some volunteers performed a previously suggested movement while reporting no memory of the suggestion. Responses became less consistent outside the experimental setting. These participants knowingly participated in hypnosis and received audible instructions; the study did not demonstrate covert hypnosis of an unsuspecting person. [Sheehan & Orne, 1968](https://www.psych.upenn.edu/history/orne/sheehanetal1968jnmd209220.html)

A later experiment involving 38 highly hypnotizable volunteers found that 94% responded to a simple suggestion, compared with 16% given a complex suggestion. Those percentages cover participants with and without amnesia; they are **not success rates for unnoticed influence**. An accompanying suggestion to forget did not improve responding. [Barnier & McConkey, 1999](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/posthypnotic-suggestion-response-complexity-and-amnesia/)

There is also evidence for **learning during sleep without remembering the learning experience**. After researchers paired tones with odors during sleep, participants’ sniffing responses to the tones changed, including after waking, without subsequent awareness of the learning process. That demonstrates a narrow conditioned physiological response. No verbal directive was involved, and the tones were not established as below the hearing threshold. [Arzi et al., 2012](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.3193)

The strongest limitation is that **forgetting the source afterward does not prove the instruction was unconscious when received**. To support the particular mechanism you’re considering, a study would need to establish all three: speech was undetectable under the actual listening conditions, its meaning affected a specified action, and participants could not identify its source. The studies above establish portions of that chain, not the whole mechanism.
