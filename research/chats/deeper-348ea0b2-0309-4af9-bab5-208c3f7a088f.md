## What the science actually supports

*Research and product snapshot: September 17, 2026.*

Three findings are especially relevant:

| Research | What was demonstrated | What it means for this idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Continuous semantic decoding, 2023 — Tang and colleagues, Nature Neuroscience** | Functional MRI signals supported reconstruction of meaning from heard speech, imagined speech, and silent videos. Cooperation was needed for training and use. | Neural signals can contribute meaningful information beyond ordinary speech, but this is not a verbatim recording of someone’s thoughts.  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37127759/) |
| **Mind Captioning, November 2025 — Horikawa, Science Advances** | Generated descriptions of videos people were viewing **and recalling**. Across six participants, descriptions identified the recalled video among 100 candidates about **30%** of the time, versus 1% chance. | A particularly relevant demonstration of converting recalled visual content into language. However, it involved previously viewed videos—not childhood memories—and approximately **17 hours of fMRI recording per participant**.  [source 2](https://group.ntt/en/newsrelease/2025/11/17/251117a.html) |
| **MIRAGE, May 2026 — PLOS Computational Biology** | Generated images from activity associated with prompted mental imagery, using a dataset involving eight participants, 18 imagery targets, and 7-tesla fMRI. | A genuine step toward visual reconstruction. But the authors explicitly do **not** claim to recover what is unique and idiosyncratic about an individual’s imagery.  [source 3](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1014263) |

That **30% result is identification accuracy**, not “30% of a memory recovered.” Likewise, an attractive reconstructed image is not a measure of how accurately a childhood scene has been recovered.

A particularly important counterweight is the peer-reviewed study **“Spurious reconstruction from brain activity.”** It found that some impressive-looking reconstructions can arise from recognizing broad categories and letting an image generator invent plausible details, with poor generalization beyond the original dataset. **Visual realism and recovered information are different things.**  [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40499302/)

For your product, that suggests a precise role for neural technology:

**Use brain-derived information as another uncertain constraint—not as an authority that overrules the person or the historical evidence.**

Also, the relevant “scan” would be functional recording **while the person is remembering or imagining**, not simply an anatomical MRI taken once. The studies above measure ongoing activity during particular tasks.  [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37127759/)

## The major danger: changing what you are trying to preserve

This deserves a central place in the design.

A preregistered **CHI 2025 study with 200 participants** found that AI-altered visual material could increase false recollections. The strongest condition—AI-generated videos based on AI-edited images—produced **2.05 times as many false memories as the control condition**. This was a controlled experiment, not a measured harm rate for your proposed product, but it directly supports concern about suggestive visual reconstruction.  [source 9](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/synthetic-human-memories/)

The response should be more substantial than a disclaimer.

Preserve the original interview unchanged. Distinguish details supplied before and after exposure to generated scenes. Do not pressure the narrator to choose a plausible alternative. Keep family disagreements rather than averaging them into a supposedly definitive history.

Most importantly, **a person saying “that feels right” should count as approval of the representation—not independent verification that every detail happened.**

Offer room for subjective authenticity. Someone may deliberately prefer an impressionistic reconstruction of how their childhood home felt. That is legitimate when presented as such.

The product should preserve both **“what I can support”** and **“how I remember it,”** without pretending those are interchangeable.

## How to investigate the brain-assisted version seriously

I would establish a separate collaboration with a neuroimaging laboratory around one narrow question:

**Does neural information improve reconstruction beyond an equally well-designed interview and visual-editing session?**

That is a much stronger research objective than “make impressive images from brain scans.”

Begin with experiences whose details can be independently checked. Participants experience documented scenes, later recall them, and help reconstruct them. Hold back some evidence from the reconstruction system so it cannot merely reproduce supplied photographs.

Compare three main approaches:

| Condition | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Interview and source material | Establish the ordinary reconstruction baseline. |
| The same inputs plus iterative visual editing | Measure the value of your core interface. |
| The same workflow plus neural information | Measure the scanner’s **additional** contribution. |

Include shuffled-signal or no-signal model ablations to test whether improvements actually depend on brain data. Keep session time comparable, and separate recall recordings from recordings made while viewing candidate images; otherwise the system might decode the displayed proposal rather than the remembered scene.

Measure **correctly recovered details, unsupported additions, correction effort, narrator fatigue, and subjective resemblance separately**. A more moving or beautiful result is not automatically a more accurate one.

Only then extend to older autobiographical memories, where external ground truth is incomplete.

The plausible early neural contribution is modest but useful: suggesting a broad feature, ranking competing scene interpretations, or helping express something the person cannot readily verbalize. The commercial case depends on whether that benefit justifies the burden and cost.

MIRAGE’s authors themselves identify individual calibration, computational requirements, training-data biases, and generalization to imagery as remaining obstacles. That argues for a research partnership—not installing a “memory scanning booth” in the first business.  [source 10](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1014263)
