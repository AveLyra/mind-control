## Layman summary

This 1991 study examined **absorption**—the tendency to become deeply immersed in imagination or an experience—and its relationship to hypnosis.

Some researchers suspected the connection came from testing conditions: asking about imagination immediately before hypnosis might shape people’s expectations and responses.

Across two experiments, the authors found that **absorption remained associated with hypnotic responsiveness even when measured separately beforehand**. Testing context had small, inconsistent effects. However, absorption was a **weak predictor**: being easily absorbed does not reliably identify someone as highly hypnotizable. [source 0](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1991/Nadon_AbsHypContext91.pdf)

## Technical summary

Nadon and colleagues studied **475 and 434 university students**, using the **Tellegen Absorption Scale** and **Harvard Group Scale of Hypnotic Susceptibility, Form A**. They compared questionnaire administration in an independent survey versus immediately before hypnosis, approximately two weeks later.

Behavioral hypnotizability correlations with absorption were approximately **r = .14–.25**, corresponding to roughly **2–6% shared variance**. Regression analyses found weak context effects in Experiment 1 that did not replicate consistently in Experiment 2. The proposed gender moderation also lacked consistent support. [source 1](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1991/Nadon_AbsHypContext91.pdf)

**Interpretation:** The findings support absorption as a modest correlate of hypnotizability. They do not establish causation, a brain mechanism, or susceptibility to coercive control. [source 2](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/PDFs/1990s/1991/Nadon_AbsHypContext91.pdf)
