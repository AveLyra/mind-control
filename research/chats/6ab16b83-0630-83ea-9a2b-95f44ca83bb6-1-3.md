**Yes—but there are several different ways this could work, and the evidence is much stronger for some than others.**

The clearest established finding is that **light can produce measurable brain responses even when its flicker is not consciously perceived**. Separately, experimental contact lenses have enabled people to receive infrared flashing signals through closed eyelids. Neither result, by itself, demonstrates that an ordinary sleeper can unconsciously understand an encoded message.  [source 0](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/acb50e)  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.04.019)  [source 2](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/05/250522124556.htm)

For the particular possibility you are investigating—**transmitting information through closed eyelids without conscious visual awareness**—the crucial distinctions are between:

**Light reaching the brain, the brain distinguishing a pattern, the person understanding that pattern, and the person retaining or acting on its meaning.**

Those are separate experimental achievements. The studies below establish different parts of that sequence, not an end-to-end system.

## 1. “Invisible” can mean four different things

| Meaning | What is actually hidden? | Why the distinction matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **An ordinarily invisible wavelength** | The radiation itself, such as much of the near-infrared spectrum | Penetrating the eyelid does not guarantee that retinal photoreceptors can use it for vision. |
| **Imperceptible flicker** | Rapid changes in otherwise visible light | A light can look steady while producing a frequency-specific brain response. |
| **Subliminal visual content** | A visible-spectrum stimulus presented under conditions that prevent reliable awareness | Some processing can occur, but the extent depends heavily on how awareness is measured. |
| **Stimulation during sleep** | The person may not awaken or later remember the stimulation | That does not establish that the stimulus was never consciously experienced within a dream. |

Each category has supporting research, but they cannot be substituted for one another. In particular, waking experiments with imperceptible flicker do not automatically establish subliminal communication during sleep.  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10561787/)  [source 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615063/)  [source 5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2026.104018)  [source 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

There is also a basic terminology issue: **optical wavelength and flicker frequency are different variables**. A red light can flash slowly or rapidly without changing its color. Making its flicker imperceptible does not require moving its wavelength into the infrared.

## 2. The most directly relevant discovery: infrared signals through closed eyelids

### Experimental contact lenses have already demonstrated an optical version of this

In **2025**, Ma and colleagues published *Near-infrared spatiotemporal color vision in humans enabled by upconversion contact lenses* in **Cell**. They incorporated particles into contact lenses that absorb near-infrared light and emit visible light. Human participants wearing the lenses could discriminate temporally encoded infrared signals; the study also explored spatial information and wavelength-to-color conversion.  [source 7](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.04.019)

The publisher’s account gives the particularly relevant result: participants detected **Morse-code-like flashing signals**, and receiving the flickering information could improve with their eyes closed. The explanation was that eyelids reduced interfering visible light more strongly than the infrared signal used by the apparatus.  [source 8](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/05/250522124556.htm)

The physical sequence was:

**Infrared signal → transmission through eyelid → conversion by contact lens → visible light reaching the retina.**

This is an important proof of concept. It shows that an optical signal can be invisible to an unaided observer yet readable by a suitably equipped recipient, including through closed eyelids.

**But it does not demonstrate subliminal reception.** The wearer was able to perceive the converted signal. The human experiments were not demonstrations of unconscious message comprehension during sleep. They also did not establish that detailed images could simply be projected through sleeping eyelids: the researchers used an additional wearable optical system to improve spatial resolution beyond that of the contact lenses alone.  [source 9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.04.019)  [source 10](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/05/250522124556.htm)

So, for this route:

- **An externally invisible optical carrier:** demonstrated.

- **Reception through closed eyelids with a conversion device:** demonstrated.

- **Unconscious semantic decoding during ordinary sleep:** not demonstrated by that work.

### Can the unassisted eye detect infrared?

There is a genuine exception to the usual limits of human vision: **two-photon vision**.

Under specialized conditions, two infrared photons can jointly activate a visual pigment. The resulting sensation resembles a visible color associated approximately with half the infrared wavelength. Research using adaptive optics has demonstrated this effect at the photoreceptor level and substantially increased its perceived brightness.  [source 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10561787/)

This is not merely an old laboratory curiosity. A **2025** study demonstrated a prototype **two-photon infrared RGB display**, producing a range of perceived colors, including white, using infrared light.  [source 12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12698097/)

However, these experiments use carefully controlled optical arrangements, including concentrated light and, commonly, short laser pulses. They demonstrate **visual perception under specialized stimulation**, not a naturally available, low-intensity infrared channel for unconscious communication. Neither study establishes message reception through sleeping eyelids.  [source 13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10561787/)  [source 14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12698097/)

**My assessment:** ordinary invisible infrared is not the most defensible starting point for a natural retinal communication channel. Upconversion provides a demonstrated engineering workaround; two-photon vision provides a specialized physical exception. Neither eliminates the separate problem of comprehension during sleep.

Ultraviolet is not a demonstrated solution to that problem either. Much of it is absorbed by the front of the eye, and unnecessary exposure introduces photochemical injury risks rather than a useful hidden visual pathway.  [source 15](https://ehs.berkeley.edu/node/515)

## 3. Imperceptible flicker: a stronger route for unnoticed neural signaling

Here the light remains in the visible spectrum, but its modulation is too rapid—or otherwise too difficult—to perceive as flickering.

### Rapid invisible frequency tagging

In a **2022** study, Brickwedde and colleagues used visible objects flickering at **56 and 60 Hz**. Participants did not perceive the flicker, but magnetoencephalography, or **MEG**, detected strong responses at the corresponding frequencies.

Participants could change those responses by directing attention toward different objects. Seven of eight participants could use the resulting signal to control a Pong game.  [source 16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615063/)

A **2023** study by Ming and colleagues implemented a four-target EEG-based brain–computer interface using **60 Hz imperceptible flicker**, with individualized spatial and phase coding to improve performance.  [source 17](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/acb50e)

These findings establish something significant:

**A temporal property of light can be absent from conscious visual experience while remaining detectable in the brain’s response.**

But the direction of communication matters. In these interfaces, the person deliberately attends to a visible target, and a computer identifies that selection from the neural response. The result does **not** show that the brain unconsciously translates an unfamiliar flashing code into words or instructions.

For example, an EEG classifier might distinguish a response to pattern A from a response to pattern B. That is evidence that the nervous system responds differently to the patterns. It does not establish that the participant interprets A as “turn left” and B as “turn right.”

### There is no universal “invisible above this frequency” threshold

Perceived flicker depends on brightness, contrast, stimulus size, visual-field position, and individual sensitivity. Research on rapid frequency tagging finds measurable responses at high frequencies but also considerable individual variability.  [source 18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10577447/)

Consequently, a stimulation frequency described as imperceptible in a paper should be understood as **imperceptible under that study’s conditions**, not universally invisible to everyone.

## 4. Invisible spectral flicker is especially relevant to your question

This is a different approach from simply flashing faster.

**Invisible spectral flicker** alternates between mixtures of visible wavelengths that look very similar. The light’s spectral composition changes while its apparent color and brightness remain comparatively steady.

In the implementation described by Hansen and colleagues, the system alternated between two phases of white light with similar color temperature but different spectra. “Invisible” was not absolute: the researchers measured perceived flicker and described it as nearly imperceptible.  [source 19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606973/)

### What the human studies show

A **2022** study involving **13 healthy volunteers** found that invisible spectral flicker produced a measurable **40 Hz** neural response. The response was weaker than that produced by ordinary stroboscopic flicker but stronger than the response under continuous-light control conditions.  [source 20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9277695/)

A **2024** study found that invisible spectral flicker, luminance flicker, and chromatic flicker could all elicit a 40 Hz response, while invisible spectral flicker was rated more comfortable and less visibly flickering.  [source 21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606973/)

A **2025** study involving **20 volunteers** extended this to frequencies between **36 and 44 Hz**. All tested frequencies generated measurable responses; there was no clear population-wide preference for one particular frequency.  [source 22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12091823/)

This supports a qualified “yes” to a central part of your question:

**Light can undergo changes that people barely perceive, yet the brain can respond systematically to those changes.**

### What remains unproven

Those studies do not establish that the hidden modulation conveys understood messages, or that the same effect survives in a useful form through closed eyelids during sleep.

There is also an optical complication. Eyelids filter different wavelengths unequally. A pair of spectra that look similar when viewed directly may no longer look similar—or stimulate the same retinal mechanisms—after passing through an eyelid. Adult eyelid measurements show substantial wavelength dependence and individual differences.  [source 23](https://doi.org/10.1117/1.3593151)

**My inference:** an invisible-spectral-flicker system intended for closed eyes would need separate calibration and validation. Its open-eye performance cannot simply be assumed to carry over.

Likewise, **40 Hz stimulation does not imply 40 bits per second of communication**. Repeating one modulation pattern forty times a second is not the same as conveying forty independently meaningful symbols.

## 5. Physiological effects without obvious visual changes are better established than hidden messages

### Identical-looking light can have different biological effects

In **2018**, Allen and colleagues used a specially designed display to present images matched for apparent color and luminance but differing in their stimulation of the melanopsin-related retinal pathway.

In an **11-person** crossover experiment, the conditions produced differences in melatonin and reported sleepiness despite participants reporting no significant difference in color or glare.  [source 24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6093320/)

This is a particularly useful distinction:

**A person need not notice a visual difference for that difference to matter biologically.**

But the effects are not interchangeable. A **2022** study involving **29 participants** found that visually matched lighting conditions produced different melatonin suppression without corresponding differences in sleep, sleepiness, vigilance, or basic sensory processing.  [source 25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9644120/)

Thus, even a demonstrated hormonal effect does not automatically establish a predictable behavioral or psychological effect.

### Light delivered during sleep can shift circadian timing

A **2023** placebo-controlled crossover study delivered brief light flashes through goggles while participants slept. The intervention shifted circadian timing, with little change in overall sleep architecture, although there was some evidence of increased sleep instability.  [source 26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475014/)

Earlier work also demonstrated circadian phase shifts using individually tailored flashing light delivered through closed eyelids during sleep.  [source 27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790124/)

These are strong examples of **physiological input without ordinary waking visual engagement**. They are not examples of the sleeper understanding a message.

A useful analogy is that a clock can be reset by an input without “reading” that input as language. In the same way, a circadian response establishes biological sensitivity, not semantic comprehension.

### What about infrared acting directly on brain tissue?

That is a separate research area: **transcranial photobiomodulation**.

For example, a sham-controlled study of **20 participants** reported changes in EEG alpha and beta activity following controlled near-infrared stimulation of the forehead. This was not a visual communication experiment and did not convey encoded content.  [source 28](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/transcranial-photobiomodulation-with-1064-nm-laser-modulates-brai/)

Such findings prevent an overly broad claim that “invisible light cannot affect the brain.” They do not support the much stronger claim that modulated infrared can write particular thoughts, memories, or instructions into an unprepared person.

## 6. What changes when the person is asleep?

### Closed eyelids do not completely block temporal signals

The **2017 Norton, Umunna, and Bretl study** you cited involved eight participants and demonstrated steady-state visual evoked potentials through closed eyelids during both light and deep non-REM sleep. That establishes stimulus-linked neural activity during sleep. It did not test message comprehension.  [source 29](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12807)

### Sleep changes which temporal patterns are effectively represented

Sharon and Nir found that responses to **8–10 Hz** flicker were substantially weaker during non-REM and REM sleep than during wakefulness. In contrast, responses to **3–5 Hz** stimulation were stronger during sleep.  [source 30](https://doi.org/10.1093/cercor/bhx043)

That matters because it undermines a tempting shortcut:

“Use faster flicker to hide it from awareness, then assume the sleeping brain will decode it.”

The evidence shows that sleep changes the response to temporal stimulation. It does not establish that the high-frequency methods effective in waking brain–computer interfaces will remain effective sleep communication channels.

Also, a stronger response at a slow frequency may reflect interaction with sleep-related dynamics rather than better recognition of a code. **Response amplitude is not a measure of understanding.**

### The 2026 study supports suppression, but the quoted explanation needs narrowing

The study published on **June 11, 2026**, *Cortex-specific inversion of visual responses during sleep*, recorded simultaneous EEG and fMRI in **15 participants**.

During N1 and N2 sleep, light-related responses remained present in the visual thalamus, while early visual cortical regions showed strong negative rather than positive fMRI responses. The authors interpret this as evidence consistent with cortical inhibition contributing to sensory disconnection.  [source 31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13402605/)  [source 32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13402605/)

However, the paper explicitly cautions that reduced fMRI activity **does not prove that information processing itself was impaired at that location**. It also did not establish the same response pattern for every sleep stage.  [source 33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13402605/)

The defensible conclusion is:

**Sleep profoundly changes the processing of incoming visual signals, and cortical suppression is a plausible part of that mechanism. The study does not prove that all visual information becomes unusable.**

## 7. The lucid-dream experiment is real—but it was not subliminal communication

The **2021 Konkoly and colleagues study** is stronger evidence for meaningful communication than an EEG frequency response because some participants actually answered questions during verified REM sleep.

Across **36 participants**, the researchers obtained **29 correct answers from six individuals**, using several sensory and response methods. In one visual example, an experienced lucid dreamer received a Morse-coded arithmetic problem and responded with eye movements.  [source 34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)  [source 35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

Three qualifications are essential.

First, participants practiced the stimulation and response methods while awake, although the particular questions presented during sleep were novel.

Second, the 29 correct answers were **not all visual or Morse-code trials**. They pooled multiple methods.

Third, lucid dreamers can consciously experience something while asleep. Participants often reported receiving the experimenter’s questions within their dreams. Therefore, the study demonstrates communication **during sleep**, not necessarily communication **without awareness**.  [source 36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

The full text also reports substantial failures and distorted later recollections. That gives us another important distinction:

**Not remembering a stimulus after waking does not establish that the stimulus was never consciously experienced.**

## 8. Could subliminal signals carry meaning, rather than just cause a brain response?

### Some below-awareness visual processing is possible

A **2026** study of masked visual shapes found evidence for unconscious processing under some conditions, but different analytical approaches disagreed under others. The disagreement depended partly on whether “unconscious” meant absence of subjective awareness or chance-level discrimination.  [source 37](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2026.104018)

This supports neither extreme: it would be wrong to say that all information outside awareness is ignored, but equally wrong to treat all subliminal-processing claims as evidence of unrestricted message comprehension.

There is an additional problem for the closed-eyelid case. A masked word viewed with open eyes and a diffuse flash transmitted through an eyelid are not equivalent stimuli. If the spatial pattern identifying a word does not survive transmission, the brain cannot recover that word merely because the remaining glow is subliminal.

### A learned cue is a more defensible hypothesis than an unfamiliar message

Consider two proposed experiments.

In the first, a participant learns while awake that a particular light rhythm is associated with a previously studied item. Researchers later replay that rhythm during sleep and test whether it changes memory for that item.

In the second, researchers transmit an unfamiliar, temporally encoded sentence during sleep and expect the sleeper to understand and retain it.

The first asks whether a signal can reactivate an existing association. The second additionally requires reliable symbol decoding, sequence integration, comprehension, and memory formation.

**My assessment:** previously learned optical cues are a more plausible initial research target. That is a hypothesis about experimental tractability, not evidence that subliminal optical memory reactivation is already a reliable technique.

### New learning during sleep should not be dismissed categorically

For example, a **2025** study found that auditory presentation of name–positive-trait pairs during sleep changed aspects of participants’ later social evaluations. A control condition using names without the positive traits did not produce the same effect.  [source 38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/)

That is relevant evidence that some sleep-related updating can occur. But spoken words already have a learned sensory and linguistic representation; the study does not establish an infrared or subliminal optical equivalent.

The evidence therefore supports a narrow distinction: **limited learning or updating in one sensory paradigm does not validate arbitrary learning through another channel.**

## 9. What would establish the specific capability you are asking about?

A convincing demonstration would need to distinguish four outcomes, rather than calling all of them “information transfer.”

**Physical delivery.** The intended modulation must actually reach the eye, with its spectrum and timing measured. An apparent EEG response must not be electrical interference from the light-driving electronics.

**Neural discrimination.** Brain recordings should distinguish different transmitted patterns under blinded, held-out testing—not merely show increased activity whenever the light turns on.

**Use of meaning.** The participant must respond appropriately to the pattern’s meaning, or show a prespecified, cue-specific learning effect. A classifier identifying the stimulus is insufficient.

**Absence of awareness.** Researchers must separately examine awakenings, dream awareness, subjective perception, and later recall. “The participant stayed asleep” and “the participant remembered nothing” do not establish the same thing as “the participant processed the message without awareness.”

This is a proposed evidentiary framework. It follows directly from the separation between neural tagging, sleep physiology, awareness measurement, and interactive dreaming in the studies reviewed above.  [source 39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615063/)  [source 40](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2026.104018)  [source 41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

A responsible initial study would use consenting participants, calibrated non-laser stimulation, matched sham conditions, sleep-stage and arousal monitoring, and a simple prelearned association. Success would then justify progressively stronger tests—not an immediate claim of unnoticed sentence transmission.

**Safety cannot be inferred from invisibility.** Near-infrared radiation can reach and damage the retina, while other infrared bands can injure anterior eye structures. Flicker can also provoke symptoms in susceptible people. Experimental eye illumination, two-photon laser stimulation, and overnight use of novel contact lenses are not appropriate DIY tests.  [source 42](https://ehs.berkeley.edu/node/515)  [source 43](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606973/)

## Overall assessment

**Yes: a person’s visual system can respond to modulation they do not consciously see.** Waking imperceptible-flicker interfaces and invisible spectral flicker provide direct human evidence.  [source 44](https://doi.org/10.1088/1741-2552/acb50e)  [source 45](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9277695/)

**Yes: an externally invisible infrared signal can be received through closed eyelids with an optical conversion device.** Experimental contact lenses have demonstrated that physical route, but the signal becomes perceptible to the wearer.  [source 46](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.04.019)  [source 47](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/05/250522124556.htm)

**What remains unestablished is the combined capability:** an ordinary non-lucid sleeper, without appropriate sensory-conversion equipment, reliably understanding and retaining meaningful optical messages while having no conscious experience of them.

The best-supported formulation is therefore:

**Unnoticed optical stimulation is real. Physiological influence during sleep is real. Meaningful communication during some lucid dreams is real. A reliable, fully subliminal optical message channel during ordinary sleep has not yet been demonstrated by the evidence reviewed here.**
