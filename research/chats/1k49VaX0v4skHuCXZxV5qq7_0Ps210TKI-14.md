**Repetition can change attention, familiarity, and confidence in memory. There is research connecting some forms of repetition to increased acceptance of suggestions—but no established finding that repeating an action puts the mind into a generally vulnerable “loop.”**

Your examples—retrying a button, repeating yourself over a poor connection, and repeating a word—could involve different mechanisms. They do not, by themselves, establish conditioning or deliberate influence. **“Madness” has no established special property as a hypnotic trigger.** Calling it a trigger would require evidence of a specific, reproducible response to it.

## 1. What the repetition research actually shows

### Repeated checking can undermine confidence in memory

This is the closest match to “I already did that, but now I need to do it again.”

A meta-analysis of **28 experiments involving 1,662 participants** found that repeated checking substantially reduced memory confidence, vividness, and detail, with smaller reductions in accuracy. Someone can become less certain about completing an action even while remaining largely correct about it. These laboratory studies often used repeated checking of simulated appliances; they do not establish that a second button click has the same effect. [Meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jocrd.2017.11.006).

**What this supports:** Repetition can sometimes make individual instances harder to distinguish.

**What it does not establish:** That this uncertainty causes hypnosis, general obedience, or susceptibility to an unrelated instruction. Those would require separate measurements.

### Repeating a word can make it temporarily feel strange

*Semantic satiation* is the temporary experience of a familiar word seeming meaningless after repetition. Experiments have investigated changes in access to meaning, although results and proposed mechanisms vary. [Experimental study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2882703/).

That unfamiliar feeling is not evidence that judgment has switched off. In particular, **a word feeling strange and a person becoming more suggestible are different outcomes**.

### Repeated claims can become more believable

The *illusory truth effect* is well supported: previously encountered statements tend to receive higher truth ratings than new statements.

A 2026 meta-analysis covering **182 studies and 31,184 participants** estimated a small average effect after correction for small-study effects: **g = 0.37, 95% CI 0.30–0.44**. That is a difference in ratings—not a 37% chance of accepting a claim, and not a measure of obedience. [Meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-70041-x).

This concerns **repetition of the claim itself**. It does not show that repeating an unrelated action makes a subsequent claim persuasive.

### Repeated misleading suggestions can distort memory

In eyewitness experiments, repeatedly encountering misleading details increased participants’ later reports that they remembered those details from the original event. Some effects persisted for a week. [Original experiments](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1996.tb00377.x).

However, repeating an *experience* does not necessarily increase vulnerability to misleading questions. A 2025 study found no difference in memory suggestibility between participants who watched a single event and those who watched repeated events. [Study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-92903-y).

**The distinction matters: repeating misinformation, repeating a memory retrieval, and repeating a physical action are not interchangeable.**

### Mental overload does not consistently increase hypnotic responsiveness

The intuitive explanation—“repetition occupies the conscious mind, allowing suggestions through”—is too simple.

An experiment comparing hypnotic suggestions with and without a competing cognitive task found that cognitive load **interfered with several types of hypnotic response**. It impaired ordinary recall as well, illustrating how a general performance deficit can be mistaken for a special hypnotic effect. [Experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10616253/).

Also, “suggestibility” is not one uniform characteristic. A 2025 meta-analysis found only a very weak association between responsiveness to direct suggestions and measures such as interrogative suggestibility. Being responsive to hypnosis does not automatically imply being easily misled in questioning. [Meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886925001436).

**My assessment:** The evidence supports several specific effects of repetition. It does not currently support the proposed chain:

> Repeating an action → a special mental loop → general vulnerability to suggestion.

The first missing link is evidence that **neutral repetition itself increases an independently measured form of suggestibility**.

## 2. The gap to *The Manchurian Candidate*

The film’s premise combines several capacities that must each be established—and then shown to work together.

| Scientific observation | Additional claim needed for the film’s scenario |
|---|---|
| A cue can evoke a learned response. | A cue reliably initiates a complex sequence of actions. |
| A response can feel involuntary. | The person cannot stop it when they actively try. |
| Some selected volunteers respond strongly. | The effect is dependable in the intended individual and circumstances. |
| Some effects persist beyond a session. | They remain reliable across long delays, changed settings, and competing priorities. |
| Suggested amnesia can affect recall. | Complex actions occur with dependable, enduring concealment from memory. |
| Drugs can alter memory, inhibition, or suggestion responses. | They reliably produce precise behavioral control without impairing the required performance. |

For example, the study previously discussed found consistent cue responses outside the experimenter’s presence in **5 of 17 highly responsive participants**, for a simple action. That is relevant evidence for posthypnotic responding, but it leaves nearly all the stronger claims untested. [Original study](https://www.psych.upenn.edu/history/orne/orneetal1968jpsp189196.html).

**The strongest objection to dismissing the scenario entirely:** Cooperative laboratory studies cannot prove that every conceivable coercive combination is impossible. But that limitation supplies no positive evidence that the combination works. Findings from separate studies cannot simply be added together into a demonstrated capability.

## 3. Studies that could test the missing links

The appropriate research program would test competing explanations using consenting participants and harmless tasks. These are **proposed studies**, not established results.

| Stage | Question and comparison | What would count as meaningful evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Isolate repetition** | Compare neutral repeated actions, repeated words, and varied activities matched for duration and effort. Measure suggestibility separately from tiredness, frustration, and memory confidence. | A reproducible change in a prespecified suggestibility measure—not merely feeling strange or uncertain. |
| **2. Establish specificity** | Assess memory misinformation, belief ratings, and responses to harmless suggestions independently. | Evidence identifying which outcome changes. A memory-confidence effect alone would not establish increased hypnotizability. |
| **3. Separate expectation from repetition** | Compare repetition with and without an expectation that it will affect responsiveness; use assessors unaware of the condition. | An effect exceeding what expectations, ordinary practice, and experimenter cues explain. |
| **4. Test cue effects and retained control** | Use harmless, agreed-upon responses and assess whether participants can interrupt them or follow a changed instruction. | Distinguish “felt automatic,” “started automatically,” and “could not stop.” These are different findings. |
| **5. Test durability and transfer** | Repeat assessments after delays and in different settings, with irrelevant cues as controls. | Reliable persistence and cue specificity, with failures and unintended responses reported. |
| **6. Replicate independently** | Preregister outcomes, recruit beyond highly responsive volunteers, and repeat across laboratories. | Effects with useful precision and predictable individual outcomes, rather than a few striking cases. |

A positive result at one stage would justify testing the next claim; it would not establish it. Null results would also need enough statistical precision to distinguish “little effect” from “we did not measure well enough.”

**There is an unavoidable endpoint:** Demonstrating a voluntary, interruptible laboratory response cannot establish involuntary control over consequential real-world actions. Research deliberately defeating someone’s ability to withdraw would be ethically unacceptable. No sequence of harmless proxy studies automatically resolves that gap.

For your own observations, the most grounded interpretation is that **repetition may affect how familiar, effortful, or certain an experience feels**. Establishing a change in suggestibility would require additional evidence; the recurring need to repeat something is not itself that evidence.
