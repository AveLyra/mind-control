The revision below adds them and states the abuse risks more plainly. The archive records one citation error in the supplied text: the absorption study’s correct [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1995834/) is different from the linked [posthypnotic-durability study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2001895/).

**Executive summary, in everyday terms.** There is a serious reason to study these techniques together: people can be influenced without clearly recognizing *what* influenced them. A light can appear steady while the brain tracks its flicker. Light delivered through closed eyelids can affect the body clock. Sounds can strengthen memories learned before sleep, and prompts near sleep onset can steer dream themes. Hypnotic suggestions can change pain and perception, sometimes with effects measurable weeks later. None of those findings, separately or combined, proves that an unaware sleeper can be made to understand hidden orders. But dismissing them as “nothing” would miss real capacities to alter sleep, experience, recall, and attention. [Flicker study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38812288/); [sleep-light study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/); [sleep-cue meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/); [dream-incubation study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37188795/). [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38812288/), [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/), [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/), [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37188795/)

The **more immediate manipulation risk is information control**. Someone can repeatedly present a false claim, select which political messages a person encounters, or offer biased writing suggestions while leaving the person unaware of the selection or bias. Experiments have found changes in perceived accuracy, stated attitudes, and some political opinions. Effects vary, and they do not amount to dependable control of a particular person. Yet even modest effects matter when systems operate repeatedly and at scale. [False-headline experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30247057/); [AI writing-assistant experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12978215/); [X-feed experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10098-2). [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30247057/)

**Technically, the first boundary is between receiving a signal and interpreting it.** Rapid flicker and nearly imperceptible spectral changes can produce frequency-specific EEG or MEG responses. In a waking brain–computer interface, software can identify which known flickering target someone attends to. That is an externally measured response to a known choice; it is not evidence that flicker is silently translated into sentences. Likewise, experimental contact lenses can carry patterns using near-infrared light because the lenses *convert it into visible light*. The environmental carrier is invisible, but the wearer can perceive the resulting signal. [40 Hz spectral-flicker study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/); [infrared-lens study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/). [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

Light presents a different, consequential risk even when it conveys no words. [Metameric-display experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29788219/) found different melatonin and sleepiness responses to light that appeared similar. [Flashes delivered during sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37660233/) shifted circadian timing in a controlled study. Unwanted exposure could therefore interfere with someone’s biological timing. It would be an error to infer from a hormone change that a person had decoded a message or formed a belief. [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29788219/)

**Sleep is receptive in selective ways.** Light through closed eyelids can evoke measurable visual responses, although sleep alters frequency processing and suppresses some cortical responses. The striking [lucid-REM communication study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33607035/) showed that some sleepers could answer questions, including one delivered through coded light. Successful communication depended on particular conditions and included conscious experience within a dream. Sleep cues can also modestly strengthen *previously learned* material; timed spoken word pairs have produced limited implicit learning. [Closed-eyelid response](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28098351/); [sleep-cue meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/); [implicit word-learning study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30713104/). [source 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8162929/)

The attachment also contains findings I previously underplayed. Sleep-onset prompts have changed **dream themes** and performance on a related creativity task. Precisely timed pink noise improved recall in one small study, while another increased sleep oscillations without improving its tested memory outcomes. An odor-pairing study reported reduced smoking in people who already wanted to quit. These results raise legitimate concerns about conducting sleep interventions without consent. They do not yet support a general technique for installing complex ideas or directing later behavior. [Dream study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37188795/); [pink-noise study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28337134/); [null memory result](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31604814/); [odor study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25392505/). [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37188795/)

**Hypnosis can produce clinically useful and sometimes striking effects.** The [IBS trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30473202/) found adequate relief at three months in about 41% receiving individual hypnotherapy, versus 17% receiving educational support. A [sham-controlled hot-flash trial](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2841242) found a 53.4% reduction in symptom scores with self-hypnosis versus 40.9% with sham treatment at six weeks. Evidence also supports some procedural pain and anxiety uses; results for smoking cessation, depression, insomnia, and other applications are more mixed. The comparison groups matter: the full improvement seen during treatment cannot automatically be credited to hypnosis itself. [source 6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30473202/)

The abuse concern around hypnosis is less like an irresistible command and more like **exploiting altered experience and confidence**. Posthypnotic responses persisted across testing over eight weeks in a small study, with durability varying by task. In a separate memory experiment, participants led to expect better recall made more *confident errors* without gaining accurate memories. A compelling recollection, especially one elicited through leading suggestions, must not be treated as proof that an event occurred. [Durability study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2001895/); [memory experiment](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ch.146). [source 7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2001895/)

**Drugs could heighten vulnerability without producing programmable obedience.** Small human experiments found increased imaginative suggestibility with [LSD](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25242255/) or [nitrous oxide](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19057896/); an [11-person ketamine feasibility study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29856288/) suggested increased hypnotizability in some participants. The attachment also covers context-dependent oxytocin results and weaker or older findings for alcohol, cannabis, and methylphenidate. These studies measure different outcomes and do not establish that adding any drug makes hypnotherapy reliably more effective. Sedation, confusion, or amnesia may make exploitation easier while making a person’s behavior *less* predictable. [source 8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25242255/)

**The strongest documented path to abuse does not require invisible light or hypnosis.** Repeated false headlines can feel more credible through familiarity; notifications can disrupt concentration even when ignored; biased AI suggestions can move stated attitudes without many users recognizing the bias. Feed-ranking experiments show changes in some opinions and perceptions of political hostility. These tools could be used to exploit existing fears, distort perceived social consensus, discourage participation, or make selected voices seem untrustworthy. That is a substantial risk even if no one can dictate exactly what an individual will think. Outcomes are not uniform: a large [Facebook intervention](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06297-w) changed exposure without measurable effects on eight political-attitude measures. [Notification study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/); [AI study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12978215/); [X study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10098-2). [source 9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/)

Historical misconduct makes consent and oversight more than abstract concerns: the [1977 Senate MKULTRA record](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/hearings/95mkultra.pdf) documents abusive experimentation. It does **not** establish that a modern combination of drugs, hypnosis, sleep cues, and hidden light can program beliefs or actions. Establishing that stronger claim would require evidence of each link: delivered exposure, detection, understood meaning, lasting change, specified behavior, and the responsible actor. The studies reviewed here do not demonstrate that full chain or identify such an operation against any particular person. [source 10](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/hearings/95mkultra.pdf)
