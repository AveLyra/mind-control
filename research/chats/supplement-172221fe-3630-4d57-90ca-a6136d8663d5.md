**The related literature supports a stronger conclusion than “unnoticed stimulation can produce a brain response,” but a narrower one than general-purpose subconscious control.** Researchers have demonstrated new associations formed during sleep, changes in later behavior, and selective modification of existing memories. Some studies also report limited acquisition of semantic information during non-lucid sleep. These findings make nonconscious influence a substantive research subject—not merely a speculative extension of flicker experiments.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25392505/)

The most important recurring pattern is this:

**A weak or unnoticed signal often does not supply an entire new thought. It activates, strengthens, weakens, or changes the emotional significance of information the brain already possesses.**

There is also evidence for genuinely new learning, but it is more constrained. Below, I distinguish those mechanisms and the implications that follow from them.

## 1. The closest missing connection: new learning during ordinary sleep

Your optical summary leaves a gap between sensory registration and semantic comprehension. **Auditory sleep-learning research partially bridges that gap**, although it does not establish the equivalent optical pathway.

### New associations can survive into wakefulness

In a 2012 *Nature Neuroscience* study, Arzi and colleagues paired tones with pleasant or unpleasant odors during sleep. Subsequently, the tones alone elicited different sniffing responses, including after awakening. Participants did not need to consciously recall the sleep-training episode for the learned response to appear. This was new associative learning, not simply preservation of something learned before bed.  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3193)

A 2014 extension moved beyond a laboratory reflex. Across a study involving 66 people who wanted to quit smoking, cigarette odors were paired with unpleasant odors during sleep. The researchers reported reduced subsequent cigarette consumption, with the largest and more persistent reduction following stage-2 sleep conditioning. Waking conditioning and several sleep-control conditions produced different outcomes.  [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25392505/)

The limitations are important: participants already wanted to quit, individual experimental groups were small, consumption was recorded in diaries, and follow-up covered approximately a week. **This supports sleep-based modification of a motivated behavior—not reliable induction of behavior contrary to someone’s goals.** It nevertheless demonstrates something more consequential than an EEG response.  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6608450/)

### Some sleep learning reaches meaning, not just sensory familiarity

Three studies are particularly relevant:

| Study | What participants encountered during sleep | What was demonstrated afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Züst et al., 2019** | Unfamiliar pseudowords paired with familiar translation words during slow-wave sleep. | Above-chance judgments about a semantic property of the associated meaning, related to the timing of words within slow oscillations.  [source 4](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.12.038) |
| **Koroma et al., 2022** | Japanese words paired with meaningful sounds, such as a word paired with barking. | Twenty-two participants could select corresponding **pictures** after awakening, demonstrating transfer from auditory learning to a different sensory format.  [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35356055/) |
| **Schmidig et al., 2024** | Pseudowords and translations presented during selected phases of slow-wave sleep. | Semantic-category decisions were above chance at the 36-hour test for material presented in the successful stimulation condition.  [source 6](https://elifesciences.org/articles/89601) |

The cross-modal result is especially informative. Recognizing a picture corresponding to a sleep-presented word is harder to explain as mere familiarity with an acoustic pattern. It suggests that learning accessed an existing representation of meaning. However, the task remained a constrained choice between alternatives, not spontaneous translation or conversational comprehension.  [source 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8959773/)

The 2024 study also deserves careful wording. Its public peer-review assessment supported the learning result while questioning the authors’ use of **“episodic memory,”** which normally carries stronger implications about recollecting personal experiences. Categorizing a word correctly is not equivalent to remembering an episode in which it was learned.  [source 8](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.89601.2.sa2)

**Implication:** It is too strong to say that non-lucid sleepers cannot acquire meaningful new information. The defensible distinction is between **limited associative or semantic learning** and **flexible comprehension of novel messages, explanations, or instructions**.

## 2. The larger connection: influencing existing memories without transmitting a new message

The most developed related field is **targeted memory reactivation**, usually abbreviated TMR.

In a typical experiment, participants learn something while awake, with an associated sound or odor. Re-presenting that cue during sleep can selectively affect subsequent memory. For example, a study of object pairs found that replaying an associated sound benefited later spatial memory for multiple objects linked to that sound. The stimulus did not need to reproduce the entire original experience.  [source 9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2018.12.017)

A useful conceptual distinction is:

**A message carries information to be decoded. A reminder can retrieve information already stored.**

This matters for your question because a simple cue can have a content-specific consequence without carrying much content itself. As an analogy—not a literal account of neural storage—the cue can function more like an address than a document.

### Emotional meaning can change, not merely memory strength

Xia and colleagues’ 2023 study paired positive spoken words with reminders of previously learned aversive pictures during NREM sleep. Afterward, participants were more likely to classify the associated cues as neutral rather than negative, compared with control conditions. This goes beyond detecting a word: the intervention affected a later evaluative response linked to a particular memory.  [source 10](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.12.004%20)

However, the full results are narrower than “negative memories were rewritten.” The categorical judgment changed, but the study did **not** find corresponding significant condition differences in graded valence and arousal ratings or overall measures of recalled identification, gist, and detail. Some EEG measures correlated with individual memory outcomes, which is not the same as demonstrating a group-level forgetting effect.  [source 11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979073/)

A preregistered 2024 study involving 80 healthy participants provides another connection. Participants first changed the interpretation of an aversive autobiographical memory through **awake imagery rescripting**. Subsequently presenting associated words during sleep produced additional improvements in some measures, including distress, arousal, and vividness. The sleep intervention reinforced a therapeutic change already initiated consciously; it did not supply a new autobiographical narrative from scratch.  [source 12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11655627/)

### Reactivation can also promote forgetting or competition

Sleep cueing is not uniformly a memory-strengthening tool. In a 2020 experiment, replaying a sound previously associated with instructions to forget increased forgetting of designated spatial information. Another study found that reactivation could strengthen one association while weakening an overlapping association.

The important pattern is therefore not “stimulus makes memory stronger.” It is:

**The effect depends on which memory is activated, what other information is active, and the learning history surrounding the cue.**

For subconscious influence, this suggests that **selectively changing what becomes accessible or emotionally salient** may be a more realistic mechanism than covertly inserting a fully formed belief. That is an inference from the memory findings, not a demonstrated general-purpose persuasion system.

## 3. Dream influence is real, but it belongs to several different states

Dream-incubation research offers another nearby connection. The 2020 **Dormio** study used auditory prompts around sleep onset to influence subsequent reported dream content. That demonstrates externally guided incorporation into hypnagogic experience—the transition into sleep—not necessarily subliminal processing during consolidated deep sleep.  [source 14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590944/)

A more clinically oriented 2022 study involved 36 people with nightmare disorder. Participants rehearsed a more positive version of a nightmare while awake. For one group, a sound was associated with that positive rehearsal; both groups subsequently heard the sound during REM sleep. The group with the prior positive association experienced fewer nightmares and more positive dream emotions, with a sustained reduction in nightmares at follow-up.  [source 15](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.09.032)

That design is particularly revealing: **the same sleeping stimulus had different implications depending on what had previously been associated with it.**

Neither study demonstrates precise control over a dream’s entire narrative. They instead support influence over themes, emotional associations, or the probability that particular material becomes incorporated. And an externally influenced dream may be consciously experienced as a dream even when the sleeper does not recognize the external source. The original lucid-communication research similarly cannot be treated as evidence that meaningful processing occurred without experience.

Thus, “influenced during sleep,” “unaware of the influence’s source,” and “unconscious of the resulting experience” remain different claims.

## 4. Distant but important connections from waking research

These studies help identify which mechanisms might generalize across sleep and wakefulness—and which require very different equipment.

### Masked cues can influence learned value and actions

In a 2008 study, Pessiglione and colleagues used masked visual cues in an instrumental-conditioning task. Participants developed a preference for cues associated with monetary rewards rather than punishments, with neural activity consistent with reward learning. This supports learning about value when contextual cues are not consciously identified under the experiment’s conditions.  [source 17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2572733/)

Other research has tested whether consciously learned instructions can later operate through masked reminders. In **“Unconscious memory suppression”** from 2018, participants first learned associations between visual cues and remembering or suppressing particular material. Masked presentations of those cues subsequently influenced recall. The relevant “instruction” was already learned; the masked stimulus functioned as a trigger for an established task.  [source 18](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.06.023)

That distinction prevents an important overgeneralization:

**Evidence that a learned cue can activate an established response does not establish that an unfamiliar, unnoticed command can be understood and obeyed.**

### Conditioned cues can change bodily experience

Jensen and colleagues’ 2012 placebo/nocebo experiments are relevant because they concern how a person **feels**, rather than which button they press.

After participants learned associations between visual cues and different pain experiences, masked presentations of the cues could elicit changes in reported pain. The triggering cue did not have to be consciously recognized in the same way as during initial learning.  [source 19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3465419/)

This illustrates a potentially consequential form of nonconscious influence: **a cue can affect an experience without delivering a proposition about it**. The finding does not imply that arbitrary sensations can be produced at will, or that an unexplained sensation identifies a hidden external cause. It shows a specific effect of experimentally established associations.

### A 2026 study extends masked-memory interventions—but with qualifications

Yang and colleagues’ March 2026 *PNAS* paper, **“Reducing PTSD symptoms through unconscious intervention,”** combined masked trauma-related reminders with bilateral eye movements. Its experiments included a clinical sample of 60 participants and reported reduced intrusive memories.  [source 20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12994179/)

The full text substantially qualifies the word *unconscious*. Eleven of the 30 treated clinical participants reported detecting trauma-related cues; an exploratory analysis examined the remaining 19. The clinical comparison was a no-intervention control, and the two-month follow-up was conducted in returning intervention participants rather than a matched follow-up of both groups. These features leave uncertainty about specificity, expectations, and complete absence of awareness.  [source 21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12994179/)

Nevertheless, it is relevant evidence that **visual reminders designed to minimize awareness may contribute to changing existing intrusive memories during an active, consented intervention**. It is not evidence that unnoticed light can implant new autobiographical events.

### Decoded neurofeedback provides a different route

Koizumi and colleagues’ 2016 experiment used real-time fMRI to identify activity patterns corresponding to a previously fear-conditioned visual stimulus. Participants earned rewards when the targeted pattern occurred, without being told which representation was being reinforced.

In 17 participants, the intervention reduced subsequent physiological responses to the targeted stimulus relative to a control stimulus. Participants were consciously engaged in a feedback task, but did not know the relevant neural target or the intended association.  [source 22](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5628620/)

This is a stronger demonstration of **representation-specific influence without conscious knowledge of the target** than ordinary flicker entrainment. But its prerequisites are radically different: an MRI scanner, individualized neural decoding, prior conditioning, repeated feedback, and participant engagement.

It cannot be substituted into an optical-flicker argument as though all of those functions were supplied by the light itself.

### Invasive animal experiments establish another boundary

A 2015 animal study triggered intracranial reward stimulation when particular hippocampal place cells fired during sleep. Afterward, behavior shifted toward the corresponding location. This demonstrated a causal link between a recorded spatial representation, reinforcement during sleep, and later navigation.  [source 23](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.3970)

The connection is scientifically important: sleep-associated neural representations can support new learning that affects later behavior. The limitation is equally important: **direct intracranial access to identified cells is not equivalent to delivering an unnoticed sensory stimulus through the eyelids.**

## 5. Why sleep sometimes permits meaningful processing and sometimes blocks it

The research does not support a simple switch between “fully receptive” and “completely disconnected.”

### Non-lucid sleepers can occasionally respond appropriately

A 2023 *Nature Neuroscience* study asked sleeping participants to distinguish words from pseudowords using facial-muscle responses. Appropriate responses occurred during several sleep stages, including N2 sleep. Responses during non-lucid REM were also demonstrated, particularly in participants with narcolepsy. The researchers excluded conventionally scored micro-arousals and found that responsiveness was associated with transient higher-processing states.  [source 24](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-023-01449-7)

This revises the claim that meaningful behavioral responses during sleep are restricted to lucid dreamers. However, it does **not** settle whether a given response occurred without any conscious experience: non-lucidity means not recognizing that one is dreaming, not necessarily having no experience. The study’s sleep-stage and awareness measurements must therefore remain separate.  [source 25](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-023-01449-7)

### Preserved input does not mean preserved integration

Intracranial auditory recordings published in 2022 found substantial neuronal and high-frequency responses to sounds during sleep, alongside reduced activity patterns associated with feedback processing. This suggests that some initial processing survives while broader integration changes.  [source 26](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-022-01107-4)

The June 2026 visual EEG-fMRI study in your summary complements this: responses persisted in the visual thalamus during N1 and N2, while early visual-cortical responses were strongly suppressed or inverted into negative BOLD responses. The authors interpret this as evidence for cortical inhibitory mechanisms, although fMRI deactivation is not itself a direct measurement of every neuron’s inhibition.  [source 27](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-73997-y)

**The inference is not “critical thinking is switched off, therefore messages enter unopposed.”** Reduced integration can also make messages harder to understand, connect to other information, or retain.

Sleep can therefore preserve some associative effects while disrupting the processing needed for complex semantic communication.

## 6. A recent development: decoding sleep-related semantic activity

A 2026 *Neuron* paper, **“Interpreting human sleep activity through neural contrastive learning,”** is a significant adjacent development.

The researchers collected approximately 1,000 hours of sleep EEG and 400 hours of waking EEG from 135 participants, using 15 semantic categories. Their model achieved a peak classification accuracy of approximately **40% on unseen participants**, compared with a 6.7% chance level, under particular NREM conditions. Additional nap experiments examined generalization, and decoded reactivations correlated with subsequent memory performance.  [source 28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41999754/)

This advances the ability to distinguish sleep-related representations beyond merely detecting a stimulation frequency. But it remains classification within a defined experimental framework—not unrestricted access to dreams, private reasoning, or arbitrary thoughts. It also does not demonstrate that a participant understood a newly transmitted message.  [source 29](https://www.ukdri.ac.uk/publications/interpreting-human-sleep-activity-through-neural-contrastive-learning)

Its research implication is that future studies may be better able to distinguish **sensory registration, reactivation of existing meaning, and later behavioral consequences**. Decoding alone still cannot substitute for demonstrating comprehension.

## 7. The patterns—and what they imply about subconscious manipulation

### Influence can occur without semantic communication

Your proposed chain runs from detecting a flash to understanding language. That is the appropriate chain for **message transmission**, but it is not necessary for every kind of influence.

Circadian light interventions can affect physiological timing without a message being understood. Conditioning can alter responses without a sentence being decoded. Memory cues can affect which previously learned information is retained. These are distinct causal routes.

Consequently, failure to demonstrate subliminal optical language would **not** establish that all unnoticed optical influence is impossible. Conversely, a physiological light effect does not establish directional control over beliefs or decisions.

### A low-information cue can have a relatively rich consequence

A reminder’s consequence can be much richer than its physical signal because the recipient supplies the associated memory.

This is the strongest conceptual connection between your optical hypothesis and memory-reactivation research. **An optical cue would not necessarily need to encode a whole message to influence a previously associated representation.** That is a testable inference—not evidence that a particular unnoticed optical cue already works during non-lucid sleep. Existing sound-cue studies demonstrate the general reminder principle, not automatic interchangeability of sensory channels.

### Lack of awareness does not mean lack of constraints

Across the positive studies, effects depended on prior associations, a restricted task, a suitable sleep state, reinforcement, or specialized measurement. Those conditions are part of the mechanism, not incidental laboratory details. The vocabulary studies likewise used constrained learning and retrieval tasks rather than open-ended language acquisition.  [source 32](https://elifesciences.org/articles/89601)

A reasonable synthesis is that **nonconscious influence usually changes the probability or strength of a response within an existing system**. The studies reviewed do not establish dependable control over arbitrary goals or behavior.

### Reduced awareness is not necessarily greater influence

Some reported subliminal effects do not survive close replication. Two highly powered, preregistered replication attempts failed to find subliminal anchoring effects. That does not invalidate every masked-priming result; it means that evidence from a narrow perceptual or motor task cannot simply be promoted into a broad claim about judgment.  [source 34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7567524/)

Similarly, a randomized study applying sleep reactivation alongside treatment for social anxiety did not find the predicted overall between-group anxiety benefit. Positive memory findings therefore do not guarantee therapeutic—or manipulative—effectiveness across contexts.  [source 35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9281560/)

### “Unaware” must identify what was not experienced

There are several different possibilities: a person did not notice flicker; did not identify the stimulus; did not recognize its meaning; did not know why it was presented; or did not remember it afterward.

The 2026 masked-memory study illustrates how participants can differ on these measures even within one intervention. The non-lucid responsiveness study illustrates why sleep scoring cannot settle subjective awareness. Neither “masked” nor “asleep” is a sufficient stand-alone label for unconscious processing.  [source 36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12994179/)

For your optical proposal, **unnoticed modulation and unnoticed semantic content must be tested separately**. A visible target with invisible flicker is not the same stimulus as an invisible word, and neither is automatically equivalent to an unexperienced message during sleep.

## 8. What remains unresolved specifically for light

After reviewing the related human literature, **I did not locate a convincing end-to-end demonstration of a novel, temporally encoded optical message being semantically decoded during ordinary non-lucid sleep, with meaningful later use and adequate evidence against conscious perception.**

What I found instead is a set of partially connecting results: optical input can reach the sleeping nervous system; auditory associations can sometimes be learned during sleep; reminders can selectively modify memories; and some sleeping people can intermittently respond to external information. Those results constrain the question but do not complete the optical demonstration.  [source 37](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12807)  [source 38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35356055/)

The most informative next distinction would be between **reactivating an optical association learned while awake** and **learning genuinely new meaning from an optical code during sleep**. The first tests whether light can serve as an effective reminder. The second requires additional decoding and learning capabilities.

A persuasive result would need content-specific behavioral evidence—such as delayed recognition or transfer to a different task—not merely an EEG change. It would also need to distinguish meaningful learning from altered alertness, brief awakenings, partial awareness, and unintended sensory cues.

**Overall, the evidence supports genuine nonconscious influence over associations, memory accessibility, emotional responses, and some later behavior. It also supports limited new learning during sleep. It does not establish a general channel for covertly transmitting complex instructions or overriding autonomous judgment.**

The important ethical implication does not depend on proving “mind control”: an intervention that changes someone’s memories, emotional responses, or habits can warrant explicit consent even when its effects are partial, probabilistic, and highly context-dependent.
