**Treat disclosure of the hypothesis as an intervention, and awareness of it as a changing participant state.** That separates three things: what was actually presented, what participants think is happening, and what they subsequently report or do. Research on demand characteristics addresses precisely this possibility.  [source 0](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311951827_The_Perceived_Awareness_of_the_Research_Hypothesis_Scale_Assessing_the_influence_of_demand_characteristics)

### Experimental design first

For a consenting-adult study using harmless sounds and memory tasks, independently randomize **stimulus versus sham** and **hypothesis disclosure: absent, indirectly suggested, or explicit**. Keep required safety information identical. Randomizing when disclosure occurs would help distinguish its effects from ordinary changes over time.

This lets you estimate the stimulus effect, the disclosure effect, and whether they interact. A relevant precedent is a blinded infrasound experiment in which negative-expectation information increased symptom reports during **both actual and sham exposure**. That demonstrates why the explanation given to participants deserves its own experimental condition.  [source 1](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031760)

### Add a state-transition model

I would propose three awareness states:

**U:** Has not identified the hypothesis.
**S:** Suspects a possible hypothesis.
**K:** Can identify the hypothesis.

Measure **belief in the hypothesis separately**: someone can know what researchers predict without believing it.

Let HtH_t describe disclosure and XtX_t describe actual stimulus delivery at time tt. Define:

Pij(Ht,Xt)=Pr⁡(At+1=j∣At=i,Ht,Xt).P_{ij}(H_t,X_t)
=
\Pr(A_{t+1}=j\mid A_t=i,H_t,X_t).

Each row of PP contains probabilities of moving from one awareness state to the others and must sum to one. The model estimates whether explicit disclosure, hints, or the stimulus itself change those transition probabilities. Covariate-dependent hidden Markov models provide an established framework for this structure.  [source 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14139)

If awareness is measured imperfectly, use a **hidden Markov model**: answers to awareness questions are noisy observations of the state, not definitive labels. Do not infer awareness solely from the outcome you are trying to explain—that would make the reasoning circular.

For a row-vector distribution of possible states:

πt+1=πtP(Ht,Xt).\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}P(H_t,X_t).

**Consecutive exposures involve matrix multiplication, not addition.** A weighted sum of matrices can represent a mixture of conditions; it does not automatically represent their combined psychological effect.

### Two safeguards matter especially

**Measuring awareness can itself disclose the hypothesis.** Direct questions can reveal what participants had not previously inferred. Randomize probe timing or include an endpoint-only assessment group, and log every probe as another input. General awareness questionnaires exist, but perceived awareness is not the same as correctly identifying the hypothesis.  [source 3](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311951827_The_Perceived_Awareness_of_the_Research_Hypothesis_Scale_Assessing_the_influence_of_demand_characteristics)

**Do not simply exclude people who guessed, or “control away” their awareness.** Awareness may be caused by the stimulus or disclosure. Conditioning on such post-treatment variables can bias causal estimates. Keep randomized-group comparisons as the primary analysis; use the transition model to investigate the process, with its additional assumptions made explicit.  [source 4](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12357)

A useful event log would record **actual stimulus delivery, disclosure wording and timing, awareness probes, hypothesis guesses, confidence, expected effects, and outcomes**.

The resulting question becomes: **“Does disclosure change awareness or expectation, and does the randomized disclosure condition change the measured effect?”** The Markov model tracks the first process; the experiment establishes the causal comparison. Neither treats growing belief as evidence that the underlying hypothesis is true.
