## Bottom line

**Political influence belongs in this research, not just commercial advertising.** The strongest evidence points to a chain of data collection, selective exposure, repetition, social cues, and persuasive messages. People may see the content but remain unaware of *why* they saw it or how it affected their judgment. That is a credible route to influence without requiring an invisible signal or literal “mind control.” The evidence does **not** establish a system that can reliably program a particular person’s emotions, beliefs, or actions.

## Connections worth investigating

- **Privacy choices can feed targeting.** In a randomized field trial, changing a cookie banner increased consent by 17 percentage points. That creates a possible feedback loop: interface design obtains more data, which can support more selective advertising. This is an inference about how the mechanisms could connect, not a finding that the trial tested the whole loop. [Cookie-banner field trial](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106729).

- **Profiling is real; its persuasive advantage varies.** Facebook Likes predicted personality scores in one study, and three commercial-ad field experiments reaching over 3.5 million people found that personality-matched ads produced up to 40% more clicks and 50% more purchases *relative to comparison ads*. But a preregistered political experiment with 8,587 people found no statistically detectable advantage for individually tailored AI messages over strong untargeted ones. Knowing something about a person is not the same as being able to control their response. [Personality prediction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25583507/), [ad experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29133409/), [political targeting experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848300/).

- **Repetition can make a claim feel more credible.** In experiments, even one prior encounter with a false headline increased later accuracy ratings, including a week later. That is a plausible connection between propaganda and a person’s sense of what counts as legitimate information. It measures *perceived accuracy*, not a guaranteed change in voting or conduct. [Illusory-truth experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30247057/).

- **Curation can change perceived social reality.** A randomized eight-week study found that an engagement-based feed amplified moralized and hostile content and increased *perceived* partisan animosity; an alternative ranking improved the accuracy of some social-norm perceptions. A separate X experiment found shifts in some policy and current-event views after seven weeks, but no significant change in partisanship or affective polarization. Conversely, a large Facebook experiment changed exposure substantially without measurable effects on eight political-attitude outcomes. Platform, outcome, and timing matter. [Feed-ranking trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10536-1), [X trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10098-2), [Facebook trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06297-w).

- **Emotional and social signals can alter expression without demonstrating control of inner feeling.** Facebook’s News Feed experiment changed the emotional language users subsequently posted. An observational study found moral-emotional words associated with greater diffusion within political networks; that association alone does not prove the words caused it. Neither result shows a dependable way to summon a specified emotion in a specified person. [News Feed experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24889601/), [diffusion study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28652356/).

- **AI assistance is an especially pertinent newer case.** In two preregistered experiments involving 2,582 people, biased autocomplete suggestions shifted later stated attitudes toward the suggestions’ position; most participants did not recognize the bias or its influence. The suggestions were visible, not subliminal, and the study did not establish durable real-world political effects. Separately, AI-generated political messages and overt chatbot conversations have changed measured attitudes in controlled studies. [Writing-assistant experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12978215/), [message experiments](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61345-5), [voter-dialogue experiments](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41345316/).

- **Sleep cueing is a narrower phenomenon.** A meta-analysis found a modest average benefit of targeted memory reactivation during non-REM sleep for *previously learned* material. It does not demonstrate that arbitrary ideological messages can be installed in an unaware sleeper. This is a particularly important gap in the proposed combined mechanism. [Sleep-cueing meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32027149/).

## Espionage and propaganda: what the record establishes

The historical concern is not imaginary. A [Senate account of COINTELPRO](https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/church-committee.htm) documents covert efforts to disrupt and discredit political and civil-rights groups. The [1977 MKULTRA hearing record](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/hearings/95mkultra.pdf) documents unethical drug experiments, including administration to unwitting subjects. The [Justice Department’s Internet Research Agency indictment](https://www.justice.gov/archives/sco/file/1035477/dl?inline=) alleges concealed identities, false personas, and political ads. These records establish activities or allegations, **not** successful subconscious conditioning. In one study linking 2016 Twitter exposure to survey responses, researchers found no meaningful relationship with changes in attitudes or voting; it does not settle effects on other platforms. [IRA exposure study](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/exposure-to-the-russian-internet-research-agency-foreign-influenc/).

US intelligence agencies also acknowledge using [commercially available information](https://www.intelligence.gov/commercially-available-information). That raises a concrete privacy question, but **data access, access to a platform’s proprietary recommendation model, and an operation to influence someone are three separate claims**.

## Where this leads

My assessment is **high confidence** that opaque curation and ordinary persuasive content can influence attention, judgments, and sometimes attitudes; **mixed confidence** that fine-grained personalization adds much beyond a well-chosen general message; and **low confidence** in the proposed leap to precise, clandestine, multisensory conditioning of an individual. A strong objection to an all-powerful model is that many well-controlled political interventions have small or null effects, including a synthesis of [49 campaign-contact field experiments](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433). Effects from different techniques cannot simply be added together.

The best next research would test the *connections*, not assume them: preregistered, consent-based comparisons of generic versus tailored messages; repeated versus single exposure; ranking and social-proof cues; and immediate versus delayed **behavioral** outcomes. A separate audit could document who paid for messages, who received them, and how feeds ranked them. Those designs could reveal population-level “loaded dice” while also giving the hypothesis a fair chance to fail. None of the studies above, alone or together, identifies a covert campaign against you.
