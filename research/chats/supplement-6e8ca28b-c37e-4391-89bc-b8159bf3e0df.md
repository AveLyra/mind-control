**Your “autopilot” idea captures something important: an action can feel as though it is happening without your deliberately initiating it. But “the body defers to a metacognitive controller—or a superego” is not the best explanation.** A central hypothesis is almost the reverse: the brain continues producing a goal-directed response, while the person’s awareness of having initiated that response becomes less accurate. That is one influential theory, not a settled account of all hypnosis.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30792903/)

There is also an important distinction in your appearance question: **the research we discussed showed that focusing on physical form rather than meaning can reduce certain unconscious semantic-priming effects. It did not establish that this prevents hypnosis or reverses absorption.**  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21245492/)

## In layman’s terms

### What absorption might contribute

Absorption means becoming deeply engaged in an experience: the story, image, sensation, or task occupies the foreground, while competing information receives less attention. In a hypnotic setting, this may help a person remain engaged with a suggested experience instead of continually comparing it with other thoughts and possibilities. Brain-imaging research is consistent with changes in how attention, bodily experience, and self-related processing coordinate—but it does not establish a single causal mechanism called “absorption.”  [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248753/)

A useful working model is that a suggestion does more than tell someone *what to do*: it offers a way to **experience what happens**. Consider the difference between “raise your hand” and the suggested experience of a hand becoming light and rising. In the second case, attention may become organized around anticipated lightness, movement, and the feeling that the movement is occurring by itself. Predictive accounts of hypnosis propose that these expectations help shape both the response and its interpretation.  [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604989/)

These changes need not be merely verbal reports. In a small brain-imaging experiment, highly hypnotizable participants given suggestions to experience color differently showed corresponding changes in color-processing regions. That supports the possibility of changes in perceptual processing—not just saying what the experimenter expects. It does not establish that imagination and perception become identical.  [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10910791/)

However, **absorption is not a general “openness to incoming instructions” setting**. What occupies attention matters. Being absorbed in a competing task can leave fewer resources for responding to suggestions. An experiment that added a simultaneous mental task found that this interfered with some hypnotic responses, although not all categories of suggestion.  [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10616253/)

The absorption study you previously asked about also measured an association between people’s tendency toward absorption and hypnotic responsiveness. It did **not** demonstrate that making someone absorbed causes them to execute instructions.  [source 6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1995834/)

### Why an action can feel automatic without a separate controller

It helps to distinguish **producing an action** from **experiencing yourself as its author**.

Ordinarily, those seem inseparable: “My hand moved because I decided to move it.” In some hypnotic responses, the movement or other response occurs, but the experience is closer to “It happened by itself.” The *cold-control* theory proposes that an intention can guide the response without an accurate accompanying awareness of that intention. On this account, the person is not necessarily pretending; the altered experience of authorship is the phenomenon to explain.  [source 7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23083914/)

“Autopilot” is therefore useful only with qualifications. It can mean that something **feels unbidden**, but that does not automatically mean it requires no attention, uses a completely different motor system, or is impossible to interrupt. The finding that an additional mental task can disrupt some hypnotic responses is particularly important: **subjective effortlessness is not the same as absence of cognitive work.**  [source 8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10616253/)

There is also evidence against a simple “the normal controller switches off” account. In a study of suggested hand paralysis, preparation-related activity remained in motor cortex even though participants did not make the movement. The researchers instead found changes involving regions associated with imagery and self-monitoring. The brain was still organizing behavior, but under an altered representation of what the hand could do.  [source 9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19555654/)

### Is that metacognition—or something like a superego?

**Metacognition is assessment of your own mental activity:** whether you know something, how confident you are, or whether you initiated an action. It is not simply a higher-ranking system that issues orders to the body. In the relevant hypnosis theory, what changes is the accuracy of the person’s representation of their intention—not the installation of a more powerful internal supervisor.  [source 10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23083914/)

The *superego* is a psychoanalytic concept concerning conscience, internalized standards, and ideals. That is a different question from awareness of intention. “Was this morally acceptable?” and “Did I initiate this movement?” are not the same judgment.

So the more useful contrast is:

Not “my ordinary mind steps aside and an inner authority takes over,” but “the processes generating a response and the processes identifying it as self-generated do not line up in their usual way.”

That is an explanatory proposal about action and awareness, not evidence for a second agent inside the mind.  [source 11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30792903/)

### Why focusing on appearance could work against meaning-based effects

Here, **appearance means physical features of a stimulus—its shape or visual form—rather than what it signifies**.

Imagine two ways to examine the same picture: determining whether it depicts a living thing, or determining whether its outline is round or elongated. The first task emphasizes meaning; the second emphasizes form. Experiments found that these preceding tasks changed how subsequently presented, masked stimuli influenced processing. A meaning-focused task favored semantic priming; a perceptual task reduced that effect.  [source 12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21245492/)

The proposed explanation is not that attention to appearance “wakes up the conscious mind.” It is that **attention changes which kinds of information receive preferential processing**. You can be deeply absorbed in either task. The difference is what your attention is organized to extract.  [source 13](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jepge/fulltext/10.1037/a0019561~attentional-sensitization-of-unconscious-cognition-task-sets)

Crucially, the perceptual task did not shut down unconscious influence generally. In the same research program, it favored **visuomotor priming**—effects linking visual features to a response—while the semantic task favored meaning-based priming. It changed the *kind* of processing that was facilitated.  [source 14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21245492/)

The possible connection to hypnosis is therefore limited but interesting: **a competing perceptual task might interrupt engagement with a suggested meaning or imagined experience.** That is an inference from attention research, not a demonstrated universal anti-hypnosis effect.

## In technical terms

### 1. Separate absorption, hypnotic responsiveness, and agency

Three constructs need to remain distinct:

**Absorption** concerns the concentration and experiential engagement of attention. **Hypnotic responsiveness** concerns responses to particular suggestions. **Sense of agency** concerns the experience or judgment that one generated an action. They can covary, but evidence about one does not automatically identify the mechanism of another. The absorption–hypnotizability study was correlational, while intention-timing research examined a particular aspect of agency-related metacognition. Neither alone demonstrates the complete sequence “absorption causes altered metacognition, which causes greater responding.”  [source 15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1995834/)

This also separates *trait* from *state*: a person who habitually reports strong absorption is not equivalent to a person whose momentary attention has been experimentally narrowed. A causal claim about inducing absorption requires a manipulation, not merely a correlation between questionnaire scores and hypnotizability.  [source 16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1995834/)

### 2. The imaging evidence suggests selective reorganization, not global shutdown

Jiang and colleagues studied participants selected for very high or low hypnotizability. Their findings included the following patterns:

| Finding | Cautious interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Reduced low-frequency BOLD-signal amplitude in the **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex**, or dACC, during hypnosis among highly hypnotizable participants | A change in processes involved in attention and contextual monitoring—not proof that critical judgment has been switched off. |
| Increased functional connectivity between **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**, or DLPFC, and **insula** | Changed coordination between task-related control and bodily or subjective processing. |
| Lower DLPFC–default-mode-network coupling associated with greater reported hypnotic intensity | Task engagement may become less coupled to some forms of self-referential processing. |

These are functional-imaging associations. “Connectivity” here means statistical relationships between signals, not literal disconnection of brain regions or proof of which region controls another.  [source 17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248753/)

A complementary finding comes from Cojan and colleagues’ hypnotic-paralysis experiment: motor-preparatory activity was preserved, while precuneus activity and its coupling with motor cortex increased. The authors proposed that suggestion-generated representations guide behavior through altered self-monitoring, rather than through direct motor inhibition. **Some monitoring may therefore increase during a hypnotic response.** A blanket claim that hypnosis “reduces self-monitoring” is too crude.  [source 18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19555654/)

A useful distinction is between monitoring **whether the experience matches the suggestion** and monitoring **one’s own contribution to generating that experience**. Those are different functions, even when everyday language calls both “self-awareness.”

### 3. Cold-control theory: intact first-order control, altered higher-order representation

Cold-control theory distinguishes:

**First-order intention:** the action-guiding process that produces a response.

**Higher-order representation:** a representation of oneself as having that intention.

The theory proposes that hypnotic responding can involve the first without an accurate version of the second. Thus, a response can remain intentional in the theory’s technical sense while being experienced as involuntary. “Higher order” means *about another mental state*; it does not mean morally superior, more intelligent, or more powerful.  [source 19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23083914/)

One empirical line of support comes from studies using a Libet-style timing task. More highly hypnotizable participants reported becoming aware of an intention to move later than less hypnotizable participants. This is consistent with differences in access to intention-related information. But these were retrospective timing judgments, not direct measurements of an intention’s onset, and the results do not establish a global metacognitive deficit.  [source 20](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30792903/)

Under this account, your proposed “deference to metacognition” would be better described as **reduced accuracy of metacognition about an otherwise implemented intention**. The claim remains theory-dependent; it should not be treated as an established explanation of every sensory, motor, or memory response to hypnosis.

### 4. Predictive-processing accounts: expectations shape both response and attribution

An alternative computational account treats the brain as continually predicting sensory input and updating those predictions in light of evidence.

In Martin and Pacherie’s model of hypnotic motor responses, a suggestion can influence both predictions about bodily movement and the explanation attached to that movement. Attention changes the weighting of relevant sensory information; action can then change bodily input toward the predicted state. The suggestion also supplies an interpretation of reduced agency: the movement is experienced as occurring without one’s authorship.  [source 21](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604989/)

This separates two explanatory problems: **why the movement occurs** and **why it feels involuntary**. An account that explains only motor activation has not yet explained the altered experience of agency.

Predictive processing and cold control overlap in distinguishing response production from experienced authorship, but they are not identical theories. The predictive account is a proposed model, not a demonstrated neural algorithm or evidence that any sufficiently strong expectation can override sensory reality.  [source 22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30604989/)

### 5. Appearance-focused tasks: attentional sensitization of processing pathways

The relevant framework is **attentional sensitization**. A *task set* configures processing for a current goal—for example, extracting conceptual meaning rather than discriminating shape. The hypothesis is that this configuration also affects processing of stimuli that are not consciously identified.  [source 23](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jepge/fulltext/10.1037/a0019561~attentional-sensitization-of-unconscious-cognition-task-sets)

Kiefer and Martens’ experiments examined masked semantic priming using event-related potentials. They measured modulation of the **N400**, an electrical response associated with semantic processing. A preceding semantic task produced stronger N400 priming than a preceding perceptual task. Comparable patterns across task variations argued against the explanation being simply that one task was harder.  [source 24](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jepge/fulltext/10.1037/a0019561~attentional-sensitization-of-unconscious-cognition-task-sets)

An attenuated N400 priming effect should not be translated into “the brain processed no meaning whatsoever.” It means that the measured difference between experimental conditions was reduced. Nor does semantic priming establish comprehension and execution of an instruction: facilitating the processing of a related word is a much narrower outcome.

Later experiments reproduced behavioral differences following actual semantic versus perceptual tasks, but also found that presenting a task cue *without performing the task* could reverse the pattern. That makes actual engagement and timing important; merely telling someone to attend to appearance is not equivalent to the experimental manipulation.  [source 25](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363711007_Task_cue_influences_on_lexical_decision_performance_and_masked_semantic_priming_effects_The_role_of_cue-task_compatibility)

The broader implication is that unconscious processing can be **goal-dependent and selectively regulated**, rather than constituting a separate, unrestricted command system.

## Putting the pieces together

**The most useful synthesis is selective attention plus suggestion-consistent processing plus a possible change in experienced authorship—not a body handed over to an unconscious authority.** Different theories explain the relationships among those components differently, and the entire causal chain has not been established.  [source 26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6248753/)

For appearance, the crucial distinction is **how strongly you are absorbed versus what you are absorbed in**. Focusing intensely on form can favor different processing from focusing intensely on meaning. That can counteract a particular semantic-priming effect without counteracting absorption itself—and it is not evidence for a general method of blocking hypnotic instructions.  [source 27](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21245492/)
