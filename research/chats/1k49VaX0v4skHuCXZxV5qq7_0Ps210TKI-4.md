## What the evidence supports

Humans can be **strongly conditioned to respond to cues**, and some people can respond strikingly to hypnotic suggestions. Drugs can alter both processes. The evidence does **not** show a reliable way to turn someone into an obedient, programmable person. The clearest drug effects concern bodily responses, fear learning, perception, memory, and inhibition; effects on complex choices are much less predictable.

| Question | What has been demonstrated | Main limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Can a cue acquire a bodily or emotional effect?** | Yes. A cue paired with an event can later evoke fear, craving, nausea, pain relief, or a measurable immune response. | The response depends on the cue, person, learning history, and context. It is not a general command to behave. |
| **Can hypnosis change experience or behavior?** | Yes. Suggestions can alter pain, attention, perception, and some actions, especially in responsive people. | Responsiveness varies, and suggestion can also work outside a formal hypnotic induction. |
| **Can drugs amplify these effects?** | Sometimes, on specific experimental measures. | Results are mixed, often based on small studies, and do not establish dependable control. |

### How far conditioning can go

Classical conditioning links a cue to an event: for example, a sight or smell repeatedly associated with nausea may eventually evoke nausea on its own. In one study of 55 chemotherapy patients, 10 reported anticipatory nausea before a later infusion; its pattern was consistent with conditioning. That is a substantial, involuntary effect, though the study cannot prove conditioning was its only cause. [Patient study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8318445/).

Medication can itself be the event that establishes the association. A placebo controlled human experiment paired an unusual drink with an immunosuppressant; later, the drink paired with placebo was associated with reduced laboratory measures of immune activity. A systematic review found evidence for conditioned immune and endocrine responses across 16 studies, while noting that success depends on the training and test conditions. These findings concern **specific measured responses**; they do not mean a cue can replace necessary medication in routine care. [Original experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12468450/) · [Systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28183096/).

Conditioning is also flexible. Responses can weaken when a cue stops predicting the event, change when contingencies reverse, or return in another context. In a large set of human fear-conditioning experiments involving 895 participants, only those who became aware of which cue predicted the aversive event showed the measured conditioned response. That finding bears on **those fear paradigms**, not every kind of human conditioning. [Fear-learning study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40569728/) · [Review of extinction and return of fear](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2692002/).

A crucial boundary is that **learning a response to a cue is different from obeying an instruction**. Fear at a sound, relief after a familiar treatment cue, or craving near a drug-associated place can influence a decision. None specifies exactly what a person must decide.

### How far hypnosis can go

Hypnosis is best assessed by testing responses to particular suggestions, rather than assuming a uniform “trance.” One relatively well-studied outcome is pain relief: a meta-analysis of 85 controlled experimental trials, with 3,632 participants, found moderate average effects on experimentally induced pain. Effects were larger when suggestions directly addressed pain and among people who scored higher on hypnotic suggestibility; benefits were minimal among low scorers. Those experiments do not establish the same effect for every clinical pain condition. [Meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30790634/).

Suggestions can also have effects after a session. The most consequential caution is **memory**: in an experiment with 27 highly hypnotizable participants, 13 later reported that a suggested event from the previous week had actually happened. This shows a risk of confident false reports, not an ability to recover accurate hidden memories. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6623094/).

Does hypnosis make someone act against their wishes? The honest answer is narrower than either “yes, mind control” or “never.” People have performed apparently dangerous acts in hypnosis experiments. In a classic controlled comparison, people *simulating* hypnosis also performed them, so the result could not be attributed specifically to hypnosis; trust in the experimenter and the setting mattered. Real-world coercion is hard to reproduce ethically, so these studies cannot set an absolute upper bound. They do **not** establish reliable, hypnosis-specific control over a person’s values or complex actions. [Experimental comparison and discussion](https://www.psych.upenn.edu/history/orne/orneholland1968ijp282293.html).

### What drugs and medications change

There are **four distinct possibilities** that are often conflated:

1. **A drug effect becomes conditioned.** The immune-response experiment is an example: the drug helps establish an association, and a cue later evokes part of a related response.

2. **A drug changes learning or the expression of a learned response.** A 2026 systematic review of 100 publications found evidence that some compounds affect particular stages of human aversive memory. Its clearest conclusion concerned propranolol and *reconsolidation*—the modification of a memory after reactivation. Evidence for many other drug effects was weak or unreplicated. This is modulation of a defined memory measure, not general control over learning. [Systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41513054/).

3. **A drug changes responsiveness to suggestion.** Small placebo controlled studies found increased scores or responses with [alcohol (32 participants)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23958795/), [LSD (10)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25242255/), and [ketamine in a feasibility study (11)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6181123/). Oxytocin produced increased responses in some experiments, but in another placebo controlled experiment it **impaired** the effect of a posthypnotic attention suggestion. These studies measured particular tasks in selected volunteers. They do not identify a dependable “hypnosis enhancing” medication. [Oxytocin behavior study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3618033/) · [Contrary oxytocin result](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4090953/).

4. **A drug impairs judgment or memory independently of hypnosis.** That can make a person more vulnerable or less able to give a reliable account afterward, without making them more hypnotizable or their statements more truthful. In a randomized study of 36 volunteers, two benzodiazepines impaired recognition of words actually presented but did not comparably reduce false recognition. Historical assessments of drug-assisted interrogation likewise found no reliable “truth serum”; drugged speech could contain fantasy, error, and deception. [Memory experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11401011/) · [Historical assessment](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Truth-Drugs-in-Interrogation.pdf).

One useful clinical test of the “drug makes learning powerful” idea is D-cycloserine added to exposure therapy. Across individual data from 21 randomized trials, its added benefit was **small at the end of treatment and not established at follow-up**. Even when a medication affects learning, translating that effect into a durable real-world outcome is difficult. [Individual participant meta-analysis](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2599177).

**Conclusion, with confidence:** Confidence is high that humans can acquire powerful, sometimes involuntary cue responses, and that hypnosis can change specific experiences in responsive people. Confidence is moderate that particular drugs can shift particular learning or suggestion measures. Confidence is low that any known drug-and-hypnosis combination can reliably impose complex beliefs, extract truth, or direct sustained behavior. The strongest objection to a simple “no control” conclusion is real: suggestions can produce actions and false memories, while intoxication can reduce resistance. Those risks warrant seriousness, but they are not evidence of precise or universal mind control.
