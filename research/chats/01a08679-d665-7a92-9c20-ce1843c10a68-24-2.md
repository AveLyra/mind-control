**Summary For Psychologists**

We reframed “mind control” as a measurable population-level influence problem.

The core idea is that personalized information systems may not need to directly control individuals. Instead, they may slightly shift the probability of emotional, political, or consumer responses across many people. Each individual effect could be small, noisy, and deniable, but if many tiny shifts point in the same psychological direction, the population as a whole can drift.

We modeled this using a “loaded dice” analogy. A fair die produces results that cancel out over many rolls. A biased die does not need to always land on one face; it only needs a subtle physical bias that makes one region more likely. Likewise, a recommender or ad-targeting system does not need to determine what a person thinks. It only needs to make fear, anger, distrust, purchase impulse, or partisan hostility slightly more likely in enough people.

The proposed experiment represents each person’s response as a vector across psychological dimensions such as:

- institutional distrust
- threat salience
- anger or arousal
- outgroup coldness
- purchase impulse
- risk tolerance

The question is whether repeated curated exposure causes these response vectors to cancel out, as random noise would, or whether they leave a coherent “resultant vector” pointing in a predicted direction.

In synthetic tests, this framework could distinguish a targeted “loaded dice” condition from null, generic-broadcast, and wrong-direction controls. It also showed that small effects would require large samples. Under the current assumptions, moderate population drift became detectable around 500-1000 participants.

The important implication is that influence can be socially powerful without being individually deterministic. If a system can shift millions of people’s affective probabilities by even a small amount, it may meaningfully alter public sentiment, consumer behavior, and political dynamics.

The most pernicious possibilities are not cinematic control, but ambient statistical steering:

- increasing polarization by repeatedly amplifying anger, threat, and outgroup hostility;
- suppressing collective action by making people cynical, distracted, exhausted, or distrustful;
- nullifying partisan efforts by selectively demobilizing persuadable groups rather than converting them;
- fragmenting shared reality by giving different psychological profiles different emotional versions of events;
- intensifying consumer impulses by timing emotionally resonant stimuli when people are most susceptible;
- making political persuasion asymmetric, where one side’s organizing efforts are diluted by targeted demoralization, outrage cycles, or attentional diversion.

So the psychological claim is not “a platform controls minds.” The better claim is:

> Personalized curation may bias the distribution of emotional and behavioral responses at population scale, producing measurable directional drift even when individual-level effects are weak or inconsistent.

That is both more scientifically testable and, in some ways, more troubling.
