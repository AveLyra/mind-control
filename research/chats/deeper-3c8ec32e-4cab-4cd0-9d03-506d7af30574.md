Yes, that is a much sharper question. You are asking whether you could **habituate to the sound's tendency to wake/startle you while somehow retaining resistance to its subtler phase-locked effect once asleep.**

The evidence doesn't give us a reliable way to do that.

Repeated sounds can become less arousing through habituation. In fact, monotonous repeated tones have experimentally become less disruptive and can eventually coexist with falling asleep.  [source 0](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0301051173900021)

But habituation is **not equivalent to becoming neurologically insensitive to the sound**. During NREM sleep, auditory evoked responses themselves show some short-term habituation, yet auditory processing continues.  [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7800161/) More strikingly, modern closed-loop experiments find that repeated quiet sounds can continue producing measurable changes in sleep oscillations even when they don't wake the sleeper.  [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9788124/)

So, hypothetically:

**Loud knock → startle/arousal → habituation can reduce this response.**

But that does **not establish**:

**habituation → protection against quiet, precisely phase-locked stimulation during sleep.**

Indeed, those may partly dissociate. Closed-loop stimulation is specifically designed to stay **below the awakening threshold** while exploiting the timing of an auditory response that remains present. Alpha-phase stimulation has altered sleep-onset dynamics without simply preventing subjects from sleeping.  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11185466/)

There is one potentially useful clue: changing the sound can *reduce* habituation to closed-loop stimulation. Researchers found pink noise produced stronger responses than narrower-spectrum sounds and that varying sounds could partly counteract habituation.  [source 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9788124/) That implies habituation probably does weaken at least some of the desired neural effect.

But I don't know of evidence for a trainable defense that gives you the desirable combination you're asking about: **“I can sleep through this sound, but my sleeping brain becomes resistant to its phase-specific neural effects.”** If anything, training yourself to sleep through it could remove the most obvious protective response—awakening—without guaranteeing that the subtler EEG response disappears.
