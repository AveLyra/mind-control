**We can already decode some intended actions, words, and even internally imagined speech. But a wearable EEG headset that reliably transcribes whatever you happen to think is not an established capability.** The strongest speech results still come from implanted electrodes; EEG is more mature for selecting options, recognizing particular mental tasks, and measuring aspects of brain state. 

The important research now concerns **how much information is genuinely present in the recording, how much the AI supplies, and whether the result works outside its training experiment.**

## How we are actually “detecting thoughts”

### The sensor measures activity—not words

EEG electrodes measure tiny voltage differences at the scalp, reflecting the combined electrical activity of neuronal populations. They do not record individual neurons saying particular words. Signals from different sources overlap, and recordings also contain eye movements, facial-muscle activity, electrical interference, and movement artifacts.

Implanted electrodes get closer to the source. **ECoG** records from the brain’s surface; **intracortical arrays** enter the cortex and record much more localized activity. Successful speech systems use these recordings to distinguish patterns associated with producing or imagining speech. 

The basic process is:

**Known task → synchronized brain recording → trained pattern decoder → predicted action or content → feedback.**

For example, someone repeatedly imagines moving their left or right hand. A decoder learns differences in the associated sensorimotor activity. Those patterns can then operate a cursor. The computer does **not** have to discover the abstract meaning of “left”; it learns a relationship between recorded activity and a labeled task. MNE’s public motor-imagery example demonstrates this using spatial filtering and a relatively simple classifier—not a giant language model. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

Speech decoding adds another layer. A neural decoder may predict speech sounds, characters, or semantic representations. A language model then helps turn uncertain predictions into plausible language. Both intracortical speech systems and recent noninvasive systems use versions of this approach. **That language-model contribution is useful, but it complicates the claim that a system has “read a thought.”** Grammatically convincing output is not necessarily faithful output.

### “Thought reading” actually covers several different experiments

| What researchers decode | What the experiment establishes |
|---|---|
| **Attention to a displayed target** | Which option someone selected from an interface—not their unrestricted thoughts. |
| **Motor imagery or attempted movement** | A particular imagined or intended action can serve as a control signal. |
| **Attempted speech** | Neural activity associated with trying to speak can produce text or audio. |
| **Instructed inner speech** | Internally saying specified words or sentences can sometimes be decoded without attempting overt speech. |
| **Perceived or recalled content** | A model can recover aspects of something viewed, heard, or deliberately recalled under the tested conditions. |

These distinctions matter: spelling by attending to flashing letters, decoding attempted speech, and describing a recalled video are all real achievements, but they solve different problems. 

## The research breakthroughs most worth knowing

### 1. Genuine inner-speech decoding—not merely attempted speech

A **2025 Cell study** examined four people with implanted microelectrode arrays. It found overlapping neural representations for attempted speech and inner speech, while also identifying a “motor-intent” dimension that helped distinguish them. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The researchers demonstrated real-time decoding of deliberately imagined sentences. With a **125,000-word vocabulary**, word error rates ranged from **26% to 54%** in the two participants tested in that large-vocabulary condition. They also recovered aspects of uninstructed inner speech during counting and sequence-recall tasks. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The important boundary: **they did not accurately recover complete, intelligible sentences during unrestricted free-form thinking.** Outputs from prompted autobiographical reflection were mostly unintelligible, with occasional plausible phrases; the authors explicitly declined to treat those phrases as reliable reports of participants’ thoughts. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

My interpretation: this is strong evidence that some internally generated verbal content is accessible to implanted recordings. It is not evidence of a general-purpose mind transcript.

### 2. Speech interfaces are moving from text toward natural voice

Two important 2025 studies pushed beyond displaying decoded words.

In *Nature Neuroscience*, researchers used high-density surface recordings to generate continuously streaming speech personalized to a participant’s preinjury voice. Neural decoding operated in **80-millisecond increments**—which should not be confused with the system’s total end-to-end delay. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

In *Nature*, another system used **256 implanted microelectrodes** in one person with ALS to synthesize voice with immediate auditory feedback. It decoded not only phonemic content but features supporting changes in intonation and short sung melodies. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The advance is not simply “more words correct.” It is giving users a more responsive, expressive communication channel. These remain small clinical demonstrations, not evidence that comparable performance is available from consumer EEG.

### 3. Less calibration is becoming a major frontier

A **July 2026 preprint**, *A generalizable speech neuroprosthesis*, pooled intracortical recordings from **six participants** into a shared transformer-based decoder.

The authors reported more than a 50% average relative reduction in word error rate compared with individually trained models. For a held-out user, fine-tuning on **fewer than 200 sentences** achieved a word error rate below **7%**. This has not yet passed peer review. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

This matters because a spectacular result requiring extensive person-specific training is difficult to scale. A shared model that needs much less new-user data could make clinical deployment more practical. It still requires calibration; it is not a universal decoder that works immediately on anyone.

A separate, peer-reviewed **March 2026** study demonstrated attempted bimanual typing in two people with paralysis. It reported performance reaching **22 words per minute**, with a **1.6% word error rate**, and calibration using as few as 30 sentences. The decoded behavior was attempted finger movement—not an abstract thought being translated directly into prose. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

### 4. Noninvasive sentence decoding is improving—but MEG is not EEG

**Brain2Qwerty v2**, described in a 2026 preprint, used **MEG**, which measures magnetic fields associated with neural activity. Nine healthy volunteers contributed approximately ten hours of recording each while **physically typing** sentences. The system reported an average **39% word error rate** without needing individual keystroke timings as input. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

This is significant, but it does not demonstrate silent, spontaneous thought transcription from a wearable headset. The recording equipment and task differ substantially from consumer EEG. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The paper also illustrates the AI problem vividly: for one participant, the target **“cars are not allowed on this road”** became **“had she not fallen down the stairs.”** Fluent output can be entirely wrong. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

### 5. Researchers can recover aspects of nonverbal mental content

A **November 2025 Science Advances** study, *Mind captioning*, generated descriptions from fMRI activity during video viewing and recall.

It studied **six participants**, trained mappings between brain activity and semantic representations, and tested descriptions of new videos and recalled content. Test measurements were averaged across **five repetitions** of each viewing or imagery item. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The result is closer to **constructing a caption from a partial semantic representation** than recording an internal voice. It suggests a route to communication that need not depend entirely on speech-motor commands, but the controlled setting and repeated measurements are central to interpreting the achievement.

### 6. BCI research also includes restoring movement and sensation

A **2026 Nature Medicine** case study connected an intracortical BCI with spinal and cortical stimulation to restore aspects of hand movement and sensation in a person with complete tetraplegia. The participant performed activities including self-feeding and delicate-object manipulation; the study also reported some persistent improvements when the system was off. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

This is a different frontier: **reading movement intentions and supplying feedback or stimulation**, rather than decoding language. Because this was a single-person study, the recovery findings should not be generalized broadly.

## EEG: what is genuinely working, and what remains fragile?

### The strongest practical approaches do not require reading sentences

| EEG approach | What you do | What the system detects | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SSVEP** | Attend to a visual target flickering at a particular frequency or pattern. | The corresponding visually evoked response. | A brain-operated selection interface. |
| **P300** | Attend to a target among other presented items. | A target-related event response. | Select letters or commands. |
| **Motor imagery** | Imagine a trained movement, such as moving a hand. | Changes in sensorimotor activity. | Control a limited command set or cursor. |
| **Imagined speech** | Internally produce trained words or characters. | Patterns potentially associated with that imagery. | Promising, but substantially less established and reproducible. |

The first two have well-established experimental foundations and public datasets. They should not be mistaken for discovering a word that someone thought without any interface or constrained task. 

### A positive 2026 result: better motor-imagery training

A **July 2026 Nature Communications** study combined tactile guidance with adaptive decoding in **31 BCI-naïve participants**. It reported average online discrete accuracies of **86.0% for one-dimensional** and **77.5% for two-dimensional** motor-imagery tasks. Continuous-control results were lower. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

The interesting idea is that **the person and decoder learn together**. Better instructions, feedback, and adaptation may matter as much as a more elaborate classifier.

There are also positive EEG text experiments. A **2026 IEEE study** combined visual, speech, and motor imagery in a **29-character task with ten participants**. It reported online character accuracy of **64.70% before language correction** and **77.39% afterward**. That is a constrained character-imagery system, not unrestricted inner-monologue transcription. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

### The newest warning: imagined-speech results often do not reproduce

A particularly relevant paper appeared on **September 15, 2026**: *Decoding speech imagery or just noise?: a symptom of the replicability crisis.*

The researchers attempted to reproduce published speech-imagery pipelines and compared decoding across several datasets. Reproduced performance was consistently lower than originally reported, and methodological details were often missing. In their replication analysis, only **36% of speech-imagery participants** exceeded statistical significance thresholds, compared with **91% in the motor-imagery datasets examined**. These are results for those datasets—not universal success rates for all users. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

A separate study of **EEG-to-text evaluation** found that several models performed similarly, or even better, when given noise instead of EEG. That raises the possibility that apparent success came from language priors or memorized patterns rather than useful neural information. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

**My assessment:** EEG imagined speech remains a legitimate research direction, but demonstrations of impressive sentence output deserve much more scrutiny than demonstrations of a small, well-controlled command set.

## How I would investigate this without fooling myself

**Start with an existing dataset before buying hardware.** BrainFlow supports playback and synthetic sources, and MNE provides an executable motor-imagery decoding example. That lets you learn the acquisition/analysis pipeline and establish a baseline before introducing electrode-contact and hardware problems. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

For a first live experiment, I would choose **two trained motor-imagery commands plus an explicit “no command” state**, rather than open-ended imagined words. That is a proposed starting design, not a promise that it will work reliably for every person.

The acceptance test should answer four questions:

1. **Does the decoder depend on brain data?** Compare against shuffled labels, noise inputs, and any language-only baseline.
2. **Does it work on another day?** Keep complete sessions—and, where relevant, people and sentences—separate between training and testing.
3. **Is it detecting the intended signal?** Monitor eye and muscle activity; check whether blinks, jaw tension, or movement explain performance.
4. **Does it behave usefully when nothing is intended?** Measure false activations, latency, calibration time, and correction burden—not just accuracy during preselected trials.

These priorities follow directly from the reproducibility and noise-control findings. A model that classifies prepared trials well but triggers continuously during ordinary behavior is not a reliable interface. 

For communication systems, also distinguish **word error rate, character accuracy, and closed-set classification accuracy**. A four-choice classifier, a 29-character system, and a large-vocabulary speech decoder are not comparable merely because each reports a percentage. 

## Two practical boundaries: EMG and mental privacy

**Some of the most useful “neural interfaces” are not brain interfaces.** A 2025 *Nature* study used a wrist-worn surface-EMG device to decode muscle electrical activity. Models trained on thousands of participants generalized across people and supported handwriting input at a median **20.9 words per minute**. That is important interface research, but it is not EEG or private-thought reading. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

For building a useful hands-free interface, I would evaluate EMG alongside EEG rather than assume that going directly to the brain is always the best engineering choice.

**Conversely, implant privacy should not be dismissed simply because unrestricted mind reading does not exist.** The Cell inner-speech study demonstrated potential unintended decoding and tested protective strategies. An internally spoken unlock keyword worked with **98.75% accuracy in one participant’s real-time test**; another strategy trained an attempted-speech decoder to suppress inner speech, but was evaluated offline. Neither is a blanket security guarantee. [citation marker unresolved in recovered chat]

My design recommendation would be explicit activation, visible recording status, local processing where practical, limited retention, and a separate confirmation step for consequential actions.

Clinical development is expanding: the registry lists Neuralink’s **VOICE** communication-restoration study as an early-feasibility trial, while Synchron has ongoing and new studies of its implantable interface. These records document research programs; they do not establish consumer availability or general-purpose thought decoding.
