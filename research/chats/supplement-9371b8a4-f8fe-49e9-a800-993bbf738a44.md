**I have not found a study that cleanly demonstrates that exact combination: a person remains immersed in visualization, processes the meaning of an external signal, and does not realize that any external signal occurred.** There are relevant findings on either side of that question, but the distinction between *not attending to something* and *not noticing its presence* matters here.

## What the closest research shows

**Meaning can be processed while attention is directed elsewhere.** In *Attentional Modulation of Hierarchical Speech Representations in a Multitalker Environment* (2021), people listened to overlapping stories while attending to only one speaker. Brain-imaging analyses found representations of the **unattended story’s meaning**, not just its acoustic characteristics, in part of the auditory cortex. This supports semantic processing outside the main focus of attention. However, participants were not absorbed in visualization, and the experiment did not establish that they were unaware another voice was present. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

**Some sensitivity to external meaning persists during internally directed thought.** In *Scene meaningfulness guides eye movements even during mind-wandering* (2022), participants’ eyes continued to prioritize meaningful parts of scenes when they reported that their thoughts had wandered elsewhere. A reanalysis of an independent dataset produced a similar result. This is closer to being mentally “elsewhere,” but it did not establish that participants had no awareness of the scene or that they were engaged in immersive visualization. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

**A particularly relevant hypnotic-deafness experiment did not find the expected word-processing effect.** Nash and colleagues’ *Subjectively complete hypnotic deafness and auditory priming* (1987) presented spoken words during suggested deafness and then tested whether those words influenced subsequent word decisions. It compared nine hypnotized participants, 15 people simulating hypnosis, and 20 baseline controls. Although the hypnotized group’s response pattern appeared different from baseline, **the expected priming effect was not demonstrated**. This small study is not proof that such processing cannot occur, but it cannot serve as positive evidence for your proposed phenomenon. I could verify its abstract, not the complete report. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

**Sound processing can persist despite a report of not hearing—but sound processing is not necessarily meaning processing.** A 2022 hypnotic-deafness case study found preserved early brain responses to tones, including responses to an acoustic change, while a later response associated with conscious access—the P3b—disappeared. The participant reported complete deafness. That is a relevant dissociation between neural processing and reported experience, but it involved **one person and elementary sounds, not words or sentences**. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

The missing evidence is therefore quite specific. The studies above do not combine a verified absorptive-visualization state, a response that depends on an external signal’s **meaning**, and sufficiently sensitive tests showing that its presence went unnoticed. A person’s later statement that they do not remember hearing anything would not, by itself, establish that they never noticed it.

This also distinguishes two versions of your question: **not noticing the signal at all**, versus noticing something but not recognizing its contribution to one’s imagery. Evidence for the second would not automatically establish the first.

## What changed when drumming came before hypnosis?

My earlier wording was too compressed. **The subjective difference concerned how participants experienced the drumming—not how deeply they subsequently experienced hypnosis.** [citation marker unresolved in reader]

In Maurer and colleagues’ 1997 study, **206 participants** experienced 15 minutes of monotonous drumming and a standardized hypnosis procedure in one of two orders: drumming followed by hypnosis, or hypnosis followed by drumming. Participants completed the *Phenomenology of Consciousness Inventory* with reference to **the final four minutes of drumming**. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

The researchers compared two different measures:

- **A questionnaire-derived “hypnoidal” score:** an estimate of how closely the reported experience resembled patterns associated with hypnotic responsiveness. This was higher when drumming came first.
- **The actual Harvard-scale suggestion-response score:** this did not show an effect of presentation order. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

Thus, **drumming-first produced a more hypnosis-like profile of reported experience, without a demonstrated improvement in standardized hypnotic responding**. The questionnaire estimate should not be mistaken for a direct brain measurement or an actual increase in tested hypnotizability. People with higher questionnaire-derived and Harvard scores were also more likely to describe relaxation and “shamanic-type” experiences during drumming. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

Why would order matter? One possible explanation is that experiencing formal hypnosis first changes the comparison standard used to describe subsequent drumming. That is an interpretation, **not an explanation established by the study’s abstract**. The accessible abstract does not provide the group means or effect size, so it does not let me quantify the difference.

A related experiment helps clarify the role of context. In 2014, researchers assigned **39 participants** to drumming or meditation music, combined with either imagery-based “journeying” instructions or relaxation instructions. Drumming with journeying instructions produced more reports of heaviness and dreamlike experiences than drumming with relaxation instructions. Those instructions were consciously delivered; cortisol changes did not differ significantly between conditions. This shows that **what people are instructed to imagine can change their experience of the same kind of rhythmic stimulation**—not that they subconsciously interpreted an unnoticed message. [citation marker unresolved in reader]

**The strongest supported connection is that internally focused experience can coexist with some external processing. Whether external meaning is processed while an absorbed visualizer remains entirely unaware of the signal is the additional step these studies have not established.**
