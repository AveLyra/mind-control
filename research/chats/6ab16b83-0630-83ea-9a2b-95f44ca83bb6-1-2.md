**Yes—for transmitting energy or data to a miniature device, and for producing some brain responses without noticeable flicker. But those are different mechanisms.** The magnetic link we discussed is already invisible; the more interesting visual possibility is light that the eye detects even though a person barely notices its modulation. Both have experimental support. [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38608028/)

## 1. For the miniature device or false-tooth idea: invisible transmission is possible

An electronic receiver does not need a signal to be visible to a human. For example, a **2024 study demonstrated a battery-free neural stimulator only 9 millimeters wide**, powered by an external magnetic transmitter. The implanted device converted that received energy into electrical stimulation. That demonstrates miniature wireless powering—not a tested false-tooth computer, and not natural human magnetoreception. [source 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38608028/)

**Near-infrared light can also power suitable electronics.** A 2025 animal study demonstrated an implanted photovoltaic device that converted near-infrared illumination into electricity for peripheral nerve stimulation. Here, the light acts on an engineered receiver rather than conveying a message directly to the brain. [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40817613/)

For a dental implementation, the engineering questions would be how much energy reaches the receiver, how large the receiver must be, whether surrounding tissue and dental materials obstruct the signal, and how much power the electronics consume. **Invisibility alone does not settle those questions.** Applying the implant results to a tooth device is an engineering extrapolation, not an established result.

## 2. For affecting brain activity: nearly imperceptible flicker is a real example

There is an important distinction between **an invisible wavelength** and **an unnoticed change in visible light**. Ordinary human vision covers approximately 400–700 nanometers. A light within that range can remain visible while changes in its output are difficult to perceive. [source 3](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/)

A November 2024 study tested **“invisible spectral flicker”**: light with a rapidly changing spectral composition whose flicker was almost imperceptible. It nevertheless produced a measurable **40-hertz visually evoked EEG response**. Participants rated it as more comfortable and less visibly flickering than conventional flicker. [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

Put plainly:

> A person can perceive relatively steady illumination while their visual system still responds to a repeating change within it.

Here, **40 hertz is the repetition rate of the modulation—not the light’s wavelength or optical carrier frequency**. The demonstrated effect is a rhythmic neural response, not the insertion of a thought or message. [source 5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39613776/)

This also depends on the actual stimulus design. A **different 2025 study of a low-visibility, multi-luminaire lighting system found no significant 40-hertz EEG response**, although its conventional stroboscopic control worked. “Imperceptible flicker” is therefore not a guarantee of neural entrainment. [source 6](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40397383/)

## 3. Could that support a brain–computer interface? Yes, with recording equipment

A separate **2024 proof-of-concept used flicker at 58–62 hertz**, described as imperceptible, for a nine-command visual brain–computer interface. Researchers measured the resulting brain signals with sensitive magnetic sensors called **OPM-MEG**. The evaluation involved five participants and offline decoding, so it was a laboratory demonstration rather than an unobtrusive, everyday interface. [source 7](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38812288/)

The distinction is:

**The visual stimulus produces a brain response; recording equipment measures it; software identifies which known stimulus the response corresponds to.**

That can support selecting among predefined commands without conspicuous flashing. It does **not** amount to unrestricted thought reading. Also, the sensory response can occur without recording equipment—but using it as a computer’s input requires a way to measure and decode that response. [source 8](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38812288/)

## 4. Truly invisible light can carry information a person perceives—with a converter

There is an especially relevant **2025 demonstration of near-infrared “upconversion” contact lenses**. The lenses converted normally invisible near-infrared light into visible light. Human participants wearing them could distinguish information encoded in the timing of the illumination and in spatial images. [source 9](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

That provides a concrete version of:

> **A signal invisible to an unaided observer becomes perceptible to someone equipped with an appropriate receiver.**

But it is **not subliminal infrared vision**: the engineered lens makes the signal accessible to ordinary visual perception. The receiver is doing the conversion. [source 10](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40409271/)

**So the answer is genuinely yes, but the mechanism matters:** invisible fields or infrared can serve engineered devices; barely perceptible modulation of visible light can evoke measurable brain activity and support constrained interfaces. The cited experiments do not establish detailed thought transfer, belief implantation, or unrestricted remote communication with an uninstrumented person’s brain. [source 11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38608028/)
