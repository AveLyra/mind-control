**Partly. You can train some of the self-monitoring skills involved, but the evidence does not yet establish a reliable way to detect that specific mismatch during hypnosis.** Training studies demonstrate improvements in certain kinds of introspective accuracy—not an all-purpose ability to identify how every response was generated.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24820248/)

The closest evidence concerns **mindful awareness of intentions**. Experienced meditators reported noticing their intention to move earlier than nonmeditators. However, this compared existing groups rather than randomly assigning training, and an earlier *reported* intention is not necessarily a more accurate measurement of its actual onset.  [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6375248/)

There is stronger experimental evidence for trainable components. A randomized two-week meditation study improved participants’ accuracy in judging their memory performance, but not their perceptual performance. Separately, eight sessions of feedback on confidence judgments improved how well confidence matched accuracy, with some transfer between tasks. Neither study tested recognition of hypnotically altered agency.  [source 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24820248/)  [source 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6390881/)

**A practical exercise would be to distinguish intention, execution, and experienced authorship.** The following is a proposed observation exercise, not a validated treatment or anti-hypnosis protocol:

- **Make the intention explicit.** Before a simple action, such as lifting a finger, decide what you will do. Notice the decision, the movement, and the accompanying feeling of control separately.

- **Compare deliberate and fluent execution.** Tap several times rhythmically, then return to deciding each tap individually. Compare how effortful and self-initiated each version feels.

- **Practice uncertainty rather than reconstruction.** When you did not notice an intention, label that “intention not noticed,” rather than concluding “there was no intention.” Occasionally choose to delay or change the next movement.

The useful target is: **“I can distinguish what happened, what I noticed, and what I inferred about it.”** It is not making every action feel deliberately controlled.

One important caution: meditators in another experiment showed *greater* distortion of the perceived timing between an action and its outcome. Increased awareness therefore does not guarantee universally more accurate perception. Feedback and willingness to remain uncertain matter—not just looking inward harder.  [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27909466/)
