Yes. There is now a recognizable research area sometimes called **dream engineering**: influencing sleeping cognition with carefully timed sensory stimulation. A 2024 systematic review found 51 relevant studies using sound, touch, smell, light, vestibular stimulation, and conditioned cues. Effects on dream content ranged from essentially zero to ~80%, depending heavily on the method.  [source 0](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38417380/)

The closest things to what you're imagining are:

- **Targeted Dream Incubation (TDI).** MIT's Dormio experiments detect the transition into N1/hypnagogic sleep and deliver verbal prompts such as “think of a tree.” In the original experiment, about **67% of prompted sleep-onset reports incorporated the target theme**.  [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590944/)

- **Influencing REM dreams.** A new 2026 pilot extended this further: after incubating “tree” during sleep onset, **4 of 8 participants who reached REM incorporated the theme into their first REM dream**, and 5/8 did so in later REM dreams. Very small sample, but unusually close to experimentally steering dream content.  [source 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13341471/)

- **Lucid-dream induction.** Researchers have conditioned sleepers to recognize particular sensory cues during REM, attempting to trigger awareness that they're dreaming. The systematic review found several studies specifically using this “targeted lucid reactivation” approach.  [source 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38417380/)

- **Hypnotic suggestion.** This has produced measurable physiological effects on subsequent sleep. One controlled experiment found that susceptible participants who heard a “sleep deeper” hypnotic suggestion **before falling asleep** subsequently had substantially more slow-wave sleep. That's modulation of sleep physiology, rather than remotely hypnotizing an already-unconscious person.  [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24882909/)

The particularly interesting distinction is that researchers can do more than simply **wake/not wake** someone:

**detect sleep state → deliver carefully calibrated stimulus → alter sleep depth, memory processing, lucidity probability, or dream content → ideally without fully awakening them.**

What hasn't been demonstrated is anything approaching reliably scripting a complex dream or causing arbitrary hallucinations/“visions” on command. Current results are more like **biasing the sleeping brain toward a theme or state**.

And this connects unusually well with your earlier smartphone idea: Dormio's authors explicitly say TDI fundamentally requires **sleep-state tracking + delivery and recording of audio** and was designed to make the technique mobile and inexpensive.  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7590944/) A phone can already provide two of those three pieces; the difficult part is sufficiently accurate real-time sleep staging.
