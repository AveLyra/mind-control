Assuming you mean a casual listener to the audio, the proposed mechanism is **masking**: pink noise makes spoken words less noticeable while some speech cues may still reach the sleeper. Researchers have tested something close to this.

In a [2014 sleep study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01319/full), words played during NREM sleep were mixed with continuous pink and brown noise. The words were **above each participant’s measured detection threshold in that noise**, and none reported noticing them. But the 16 analyzed participants showed **no semantic priming on average** after waking. The authors found correlations suggesting possible encoding in some individuals, which is weaker evidence than a reliable effect across the group.

A separate [2012 study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0050997) found brain responses to the meaning of sentences presented through moderate pink noise during N2 and REM sleep. Stronger noise weakened those responses.

So pink noise can make speech unobtrusive while leaving some capacity for semantic processing. The evidence does **not** establish a reliable way to deliver a message that is below the sleeper’s hearing threshold, understood by them, and unnoticed by an awake listener. The tone study you quoted cannot bridge that gap: detecting a faint tone requires much less information than extracting words from masked speech.
