**It could be an experimental candidate for association formation, but the spelling example does not establish that an association formed—or that manipulation was intended.** Controlled studies show that pairing a stimulus with emotionally positive or negative material can change its evaluation. That does not establish that an ambiguous fragment in ordinary conversation has the same effect.  [source 0](https://doi.org/10.1080/02699931.2025.2528924)

In your example, “S as in San …” is not necessarily perceived as “insane.” Pronunciation, pauses, and context would matter. Experiments show that contextual cues can change how listeners interpret ambiguous speech sounds. **Hearing a possible word within a phrase is a perceptual outcome; forming a lasting name–word or self–word association is a separate outcome.**  [source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11233028/)

Nor should we assume that making a pairing subtle makes it more effective. Evidence about evaluative learning without awareness is mixed: some experiments report effects, whereas others find that adequate perceptual awareness is necessary under their tested conditions.  [source 2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2020.104460)  [source 3](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000757)

**The quoted explanation was too confident.** “Paired-associate loading” was a proposed label, not an established mechanism demonstrated here. The *generation effect* supports improved memory for self-generated information; it does **not** establish that eliciting a word makes someone believe it describes them, or produces greater distress than merely hearing it. Those would require separate tests.  [source 4](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32671573/)

For the exploratory program, I would first test this **while awake**, using consenting participants, fictional names, and harmless ambiguous words. Have listeners transcribe what they heard **before anyone suggests an interpretation**. Then compare delayed name–word associations against matched unpaired exposure and equally unexpected neutral phrasing. That separates spontaneous perception, association formation, and the effect of breaking an expected pattern.

Only afterward would a sleep experiment be informative: does replaying an independently established cue during verified sleep change later outcomes compared with uncued controls? That is the additional step required to connect the hypothesis to TMR. An unusual daytime phrase alone provides no evidence for it.  [source 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398527/)
