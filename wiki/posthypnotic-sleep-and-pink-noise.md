# Posthypnotic suggestion, sleep conditioning, and pink noise

[Home](../README.md) · [Hypnosis](hypnosis.md) · [Sleep](sleep.md) · [Conditioning](conditioning.md) · [Combinations](combined-techniques.md) · [Source checks](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md)

**Posthypnotic suggestions can influence specific cued responses after the original induction, including at later visits. Sleep learning can change a simple response after waking, and one controlled study reported reduced smoking for several days. Pink noise has separate roles as stimulation, masking, and a learned cue; those roles have different outcomes and can interfere with the intended effect.**

Assessed October 29, 2026. The 39 selected source entries include methods reports, reanalyses, syntheses, and a retraction notice, rather than 39 independent experiments. The focus is hypnosis, sleep influence, and lasting behavior. No intervention or new experiment was performed.

<a id="durability"></a>
## What counts as lasting behavior?

| Observation | What it establishes | What still needs testing |
|---|---|---|
| The suggestion works again when deliberately cued a week later | Retained responsiveness to that cue under the tested conditions | Autonomous behavior between visits, transfer to other settings, and effects against the person's preferences |
| A suggestion passes repeated tests across eight weeks | Persistence under repeated testing, with its accompanying context | Persistence without rehearsal, reactivation, or repeated contact |
| Behavior changes for days after a sleep session | A waking outcome beyond an immediate sleep response | Objective confirmation, independent replication, and longer follow-up |
| Sleep physiology improves during several nights of stimulation | An effect during repeated exposure | An effect that survives stopping stimulation, or any particular learned behavior |

Duration, repeated exposure, cue reactivation, and generalization are separate variables. A later test can itself reactivate or rehearse the response.

<a id="posthypnotic"></a>
## Posthypnotic effects: genuine persistence, uneven replication

| Evidence | Observed outcome and interval | Strongest qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Eight-week studies, 1991 and 1996 | Cued responses were tested repeatedly over eight weeks. In the N = 78 study, passing declined over time. | Selected highly hypnotizable participants; repeated tests. The studies and the 2001 follow-up share researchers. [T01–T02](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t01) |
| Attempted replication, 2001, N = 59 | Responses declined with time; instructions to reenter hypnosis offered no durability advantage. | The earlier difficulty effect failed to replicate. This qualifies a proposed durability determinant, rather than showing that all posthypnotic effects disappeared. [T03](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t03) |
| Behavior outside the original setting, 2010 | Participants were asked to perform a daily action after the session. | A waking social request alone produced substantial compliance. Posthypnotic suggestion/amnesia did not confer a uniform advantage; perceived demands mattered. The abstract does not specify a fixed follow-up duration. [T04](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t04) |
| Food choices, 2023, 50 completers | Preferences and choices in a simulated supermarket changed with suggestion activation, and effects persisted to a visit about a week later. | Actual interval was 3–10 days. No renewed induction, but activation/deactivation was repeated. Simulated baskets do not establish changed consumption or weight. The 2022 methods report is not another outcome replication. [T05–T06](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t05) |
| Handgrip, 2024, N = 48 | At the later visit, cue activation increased objective grip by a reported 2.9 kg relative to that visit's baseline in the hypnosis group. | No immediate objective increase; individual between-group time-point comparisons were nonsignificant, including the later post-cue score (p = .077). The paper also reports mixed-ANOVA effects: the statistical contrasts must remain distinct. [T07](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t07) |
| Stress coping, 2024, N = 60 | Lower reported stress during/recovering from a stress task and fewer negative thoughts a week later. | Heart rate/adrenaline did not differ by group; cortisol did not show the expected response. Both groups received the active cue to take home, so later cue ratings are not a persistent active-versus-neutral comparison. [T08](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t08) |
| Memory-access suggestion, 2024, N = 24 highly suggestible participants | Faster and more confident recognition, including with new materials one week later. | Accuracy did not improve. Persistence of confidence/speed is a different outcome from better memory. [T09](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t09) |

Task-specific replication is stronger for some immediate effects. A 2002 Stroop experiment and a later independent team's replication found reduced interference after suggestion in responsive participants. Eye tracking in the later study opposed simply looking away; pupil results favored increased effortful control. A separate study obtained related effects using imaginative instructions without mentioning hypnosis. These results support specific changes in cognitive control while leaving the mechanism, long-term transfer, and necessity of formal induction open. [T10–T12](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t10)

The modern grip, stress, and memory-access papers come from an overlapping research program. Different tasks add useful evidence, but are not independent laboratories repeating one durable behavioral effect. No participant-level overlap is asserted here.

<a id="sleep-conditioning"></a>
## Sleep conditioning: carryover into waking behavior

The 2012 tone–odor experiments demonstrated new associations expressed as different sniff responses during sleep and after waking. The stringent arousal-free waking comparison was small (N = 6); a larger inclusive analysis agreed. The sleep-stage transfer comparison required relaxed exclusions. The authors acknowledged that global EEG and later unawareness cannot exclude every brief/local episode of awareness. This is a real sensory-motor learning result with a next-morning endpoint. [T15](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t15)

Its 2020 neural-dynamics paper reuses original data, analyzing 43 participants across two original conditions. It is a mechanistic reanalysis, not an independent replication of sleep learning. [T16](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t16)

The most directly relevant everyday-behavior result is the 2014 smoking study. Across 66 participants in several conditions, it reported reduced diary-recorded smoking after sleep conditioning, with the stage-2 group averaging roughly a one-third reduction over the following week. Controls included waking conditioning, unpaired exposure, and aversive odors alone. Participants wanted to quit; these results do not establish overriding an opposed goal or durable abstinence. No independent exact smoking replication was identified in this bounded search. Fresh complete-body retrieval failed; the abstract and selected indexed primary results were inspected. [T17](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t17)

Two counterweights matter:

- **A failed behavioral replication:** the original sleep-cued counter-bias study (N = 40) reported an implicit-association advantage immediately and a week later. An independent N = 31 replication found neither. The later phase/delta paper reanalyzes the original dataset; it does not rescue the effect with a new sample. Implicit-association scores are also different from observed discriminatory conduct. [T18–T20](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t18)
- **A different clinical application failed:** in N = 35 hospitalized patients with alcohol dependence, sleep exposure to alcohol odor did not improve craving or physiological cue-reactivity relative to control. This was cue exposure, not the smoking study's aversive pairing, so it is an informative transfer limit rather than an exact failed smoking replication. [T39](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t39)

<a id="pink-noise"></a>
## Pink noise: distinguish stimulation, masking, and learned meaning

| Role | Relevant evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Drive sleep oscillations** | A sound-comparison study (N = 12) found pink noise elicited larger slow-oscillation responses than tested alternatives. | Larger EEG responses do not themselves show greater suggestibility, comprehension, or behavioral control. [T23](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t23) |
| **Mask environmental sound** | A 2026 N = 12 crossover pilot found attenuation of selected traffic-noise sleep/metabolic disturbances. | Acute physiological endpoints under a particular noise environment; no conditioning or post-exposure behavioral follow-up. [T27](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t27) |
| **Continuous background exposure** | A separate 2026 N = 25 trial found reduced REM sleep and worse subjective outcomes with pink noise despite some masking benefits. | An active exposure can trade benefits on one endpoint for costs on another. A companion report uses the same trial identifier/sample description; do not count it as independent replication. [T28–T29](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t28) |
| **Learned contextual cue** | In a 2015 fear/extinction experiment, the associated contextual sound was pink noise. Sleep re-exposure increased later fear expression versus sleep controls; waking re-exposure attenuated it. | The result directly concerns an acquired association, not generic relaxation. Sleep profiles did not change, so a cue effect need not operate by making sleep deeper. [T21](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t21) |
| **Trigger for a posthypnotic response** | The selected posthypnotic studies establish other deliberately activated cues. | A controlled demonstration that pink noise specifically improves lasting posthypnotic behavior was not identified. Treat that extension as a hypothesis. |

The 2013 closed-loop acoustic study reported a memory benefit. A later double-blind attempt to reproduce the word-pair procedure, alongside a separate navigation experiment, enhanced oscillations but found no behavioral benefit. This is a concrete replication limit on translating state changes into performance. [T22, T24](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t22)

The unretracted 2021 synthesis found a small overall memory effect (g = 0.25, p = .07), with positive subgroup results. A separate 2023 analysis raised concerns about effect-size decline, blinding, and retention-score reliability. Its publication-year predictions are meta-regression estimates, not proof that contemporary stimulation necessarily harms memory. Neither synthesis is a new behavioral experiment. A different often-cited meta-analysis was retracted for errors affecting its conclusions and is excluded from positive evidence here. [T25–T26](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t25), [T37](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t37)

Claims of harm need the same care as benefits. The N = 72 pink-noise/insight study had a worse numerical pattern, but its three-group insight comparison was nonsignificant (p = .22); it does not establish impaired creativity. Separately, an N = 8 physiology study found that disruptive sounds could increase momentary delta activity while worsening sleep continuity and next-day vigilance. More delta is therefore insufficient to classify an exposure as beneficial. [T30–T31](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t30)

For longer exposure and behavior, a remote older-adult trial found increased slow-wave activity across active stimulation periods but minor daily-function effects; two weeks of exposure is not two weeks of persistence after withdrawal. Another acoustic trial found improved next-day attention after two consecutive nights in short sleepers. Its retrieved abstract describes acoustic tones; it is included as adjacent behavioral evidence, not a verified pink-noise-specific trial. [T32–T33](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t32)

<a id="combinations"></a>
## Which combinations have actually been tested?

| Proposed combination | Evidence status | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Hypnotic suggestion before sleep | Direct human studies show greater slow-wave sleep in selected participants. A later N = 33 reanalysis found no memory benefit and reduced slow-wave/spindle coupling. | A selected change in sleep depth does not establish improved conditioning or posthypnotic behavior. The reanalysis reused two earlier datasets. [T13–T14](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t13) |
| Learned pink-noise context + sleep/wake re-exposure | Direct experiment, with opposing directions of fear expression. | Which competing association is strengthened, independent replication, durability, and transfer beyond the tested response. [T21](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t21) |
| Learned cues timed to sleep oscillations | Direct navigation and later memory studies reported benefits; another N = 16 up/down-state study found no phase or cued/uncued advantage. | Different tasks and comparisons; these are not interchangeable exact replications or a full factorial test of adding pink-noise stimulation. [T34–T36](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t34) |
| Hypnosis + conditioning | A historical controlled report linked hypnotic suggestion to conditioned salivation. | Publisher-abstract extract only; no established multi-day behavior, independent replication, sleep component, or pink-noise component here. [T38](../research/updates/2026-10-29-posthypnotic-sleep-pink-noise.md#t38) |
| Posthypnotic cue + pink-noise state stimulation + sleep conditioning | The full combination was not established by the selected studies. | Added behavioral benefit, interference, duration, cue specificity, and necessity of hypnosis all require direct evidence. |

Matching components does not establish synergy. Learned sounds can reactivate associations while also changing sleep physiology or causing arousal; those effects need to be distinguished. A closed-loop TMR study tests timing a learned cue, which is different from adding a separate pink-noise stimulus to a posthypnotic intervention.

<a id="replications"></a>
## Relationships that change the apparent evidence count

- T01–T03 share researchers; T03 explicitly attempted and partly failed to reproduce earlier determinants of durability.
- T05 supplies methods/plans; T06 supplies final outcomes. They do not provide two independent demonstrations.
- T16 reuses the original sleep-learning data; T20 reuses the original counter-bias data.
- T14 reuses two hypnosis/sleep datasets. T28–T29 describe the same registered trial/sample, rather than two independent noise trials.
- T24 and T19 provide actual negative replication evidence, with the changes in task/design identified separately.
- T37 is a retraction notice; its underlying meta-analysis supplies no valid positive estimate for this review.

<a id="horizon"></a>
## The most useful next evidence

Prioritize independent replications of the lasting food-choice and smoking outcomes, with actual behavior distinguished from simulated choices, diary reports, or subjective feelings. For posthypnotic effects, separate persistence without cueing from successful reactivation and compare hypnosis with matched waking instructions. For pink noise, separate masking, physiological stimulation, and learned association; preserve sleep continuity and measure the intended waking outcome. Direct combinations must beat relevant component controls rather than merely produce a favorable EEG response.

The restricted effects provide real research leads. Reliable, durable execution of arbitrary instructions across settings and against a person's preferences remains unestablished in this bounded review. That limit concerns the larger claim; it does not erase the measured cue effects or behavioral changes.
