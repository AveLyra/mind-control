## Research questions

1. **Define what awareness concerns.** Separate awareness of a stimulus, flicker, a spatial or temporal pattern, a learned code, its meaning, the experimental hypothesis, and an earlier experience remembered later. Distinguish perceptual awareness, metacognitive reporting, and recall. During sleep, keep sleep staging, arousals, dream experience, lucidity, and later recall separate.

2. **Compare measurement methods.** Examine subjective visibility/perceptual-awareness scales, confidence, forced-choice discrimination, detection and identification, signal-detection measures such as d′, metacognitive measures, and relevant report/no-report approaches. Explain what each method measures and its limitations. Determine how closely the awareness task must match the information, stimulus conditions, timing, and response demands of the main task. Address awareness of modulation separately from awareness of content.

3. **Audit statistical inference.** Explain what a nonsignificant awareness test can and cannot establish. Compare confidence intervals, prespecified equivalence bounds or smallest effects of interest, Bayesian approaches with prior-sensitivity checks, and latent-variable or measurement-error approaches where relevant. Address power, individual differences, missing data, multiple analyses, response criteria, trial selection, post hoc “unaware” subgroups, regression to the mean, and reuse of noisy awareness scores. Do not treat finite data as proof of exactly zero awareness.

4. **Compare paradigms without assuming transfer.** Review visual masking and priming, rapid imperceptible flicker/SSVEP interfaces, spectral modulation, closed-eye waking stimulation, non-lucid NREM/REM stimulation, and lucid-dream communication. Distinguish a visible target with unnoticed flicker from an unperceived symbol or message. Use auditory sleep learning as a separately labeled comparison, and assess what evidence would be needed to transfer an inference to optics. Separate unaided perception from equipment that converts an invisible carrier into a perceptible signal.

5. **Test the strongest alternative explanations.** Examine residual conscious cues, expectation, attention, demand characteristics, report bias, inadvertent awakening, dream awareness, equipment leakage, recording artifacts, classifier contamination, and circadian or alertness changes. Seek independent replications, preregistered tests, null results, corrections, retractions, and disputes over interpretation. Identify evidence that would falsify each leading explanation.

6. **Separate levels of achievement.** For each positive result, identify whether it demonstrates physical delivery, an evoked response, discrimination, access to a previously learned meaning, acquisition of new meaning, delayed retention, or transfer to a different task. Assess whether neural decoding establishes anything about the participant’s own access to the information. Avoid extending a restricted task into claims of arbitrary message comprehension or control of beliefs or behavior.
